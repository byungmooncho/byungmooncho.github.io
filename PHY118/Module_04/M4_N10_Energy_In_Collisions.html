Energy in Collisions – Elastic and Inelastic

SynopsisWhen two bodies collide, one of two things will happen: they will either collide and separate, or they will stick together and move as a unit.  When they bounce, it’s called an elastic collision.  When they stick, it’s called an inelastic collision.  Note 8 showed that momentum is conserved in both — the sum before equals the sum after, whatever the carts do.  This Note asks the other question: what happens to the kinetic energy?

The answer is the real difference between the two kinds.  In a perfectly elastic collision the total kinetic energy after is the same as before — the carts’ bumpers store it as elastic PE for an instant and give all of it back.  In an inelastic collision some of the kinetic energy is gone, into thermal energy, sound, and the deformation of whatever stuck; in a perfectly inelastic collision (they stick) as much is lost as the momentum law allows.  Momentum never has a choice.  Kinetic energy always does.

Momentum and kinetic energy also answer different questions about stopping something.  Both are built from the same m and v, but p = mv and KE = ½mv² weigh the velocity differently, and a bullet and a freight car make the difference impossible to miss.
Before you read on — predict

A moving cart sticks to an identical cart at rest.  What fraction of the kinetic energy survives the collision — all of it, half, or none?  Commit, in your guided notes.

Performance expectations

Distinguish elastic from inelastic collisions by what happens to the kinetic energy.  Calculate the kinetic energy before and after a collision and the fraction lost.  Explain why momentum and kinetic energy give different answers about stopping a moving object.

Perfectly inelastic: as much as the law allows

From the same lab: cart A, 0.51 kg, at 0.75 m/s into a loaded cart B, 1.5 kg, at rest.  They lock together.  Momentum fixes the final speed; the kinetic energy has to live with whatever that leaves.

  1. Predict the speed of the pair from momentum conservation.
  2. Calculate the kinetic energy before and after, and the fraction that was lost.
  3. The lost kinetic energy did not vanish.  Say where it went, and why the momentum did not go with it.
  4. Show that the fraction lost is mB/(mA + mB) — so a heavy target swallows more of the energy.
i) (0.51)(0.75) + 0 = 0.383 kg·m/s = (0.51 + 1.5)v′ → v′ = 0.19 m/s — the board that day read 0.13–0.14 m/s measured; the carts rubbed as they coupled
ii) KE before = ½(0.51)(0.75)² = 0.143 J;  after = ½(2.01)(0.19)² = 0.036 J;  lost = 0.107 J = 75 %
iii) Into thermal energy, sound and the deformation of the coupler — forms that carry no net momentum.  Momentum is a vector and the internal forces come in equal and opposite pairs; heat has no direction to carry it off in.
iv) KEafter/KEbefore = ½(mA+mB)v′² / ½mAvA² with v′ = mAvA/(mA+mB) gives mA/(mA+mB) kept, so mB/(mA+mB) lost: 1.5/2.01 = 75 %.  Equal masses lose exactly half.

Same person, same push: a bullet and a freight car

A 0.01 kg bullet at 500 m/s, and a 100 000 kg freight car at 0.01 m/s.  In each case a person pushes back with 500 N until the thing stops.

  1. Calculate the momentum and the kinetic energy of each.
  2. Use the work–energy relation (Note 4) to find how far each one travels against the 500 N before stopping.
  3. Use the impulse–momentum relation (Note 9) to find how long each one takes to stop.
  4. In two sentences: which quantity sets the stopping distance, which sets the stopping time, and why you cannot stop the car with your hands even though it is barely moving.
i) bullet: p = (0.01)(500) = 5 kg·m/s, KE = ½(0.01)(500)² = 1250 J.  car: p = (10⁵)(0.01) = 1000 kg·m/s, KE = ½(10⁵)(0.01)² = 5 J.
ii) Fd = KE: bullet d = 1250/500 = 2.5 m;  car d = 5/500 = 0.01 m — one centimetre.
iii) FΔt = p: bullet Δt = 5/500 = 0.01 s;  car Δt = 1000/500 = 2 s.
iv) Kinetic energy sets the distance (work = force × distance); momentum sets the time (impulse = force × time).  The car has almost no energy — it would stop in a centimetre — but 1000 kg·m/s of momentum means two full seconds of 500 N, and a person cannot keep 500 N on a moving wall of steel for two seconds without being moved instead.
