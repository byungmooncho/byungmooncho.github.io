Gravitational Potential Energy

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Lift something and you do work against gravity. Let it go and you get that work back as motion. Because gravity hands the work back — every time, by any route — the energy you spent can be treated as stored in the position itself rather than lost. That stored quantity is U = mgh, and the only hard part of this page is the signs.
You already know this Work and the dot product from Ch07a; that a force at 180° to the motion does negative work. The conservative-force argument that justifies defining a potential energy at all is on Ch08b — this page uses the result and that page proves it.

Energy: stored, and moving

Potential energy— “stored” energy. Chemical (gasoline), gravitational (water behind a dam), elastic (a compressed spring).
Kinetic energy— energy due to the motion of a body — see Ch07b.

This page is about the second item in the first row: the gravitational kind, which is the one you can write down exactly with nothing more than m, g and a height.

Work done by gravity, twice

Trevor computes it both ways round, because the sign is the whole lesson and it flips.

And that is why a potential energy can be defined

We get this work back. Gravity is a conservative force, so the energy spent lifting is not gone — it is recoverable by dropping. That makes it sensible to book it against the position rather than write it off.

U = mgh      Ug = −Wg

Potential energy is defined as minus the work done by the conservative force. Lifting: gravity does −mgd, so U goes up by mgd. Dropping: gravity does +mgd, so U goes down. The minus sign is what makes the bookkeeping match the intuition.

A notation note, and Trevor's own warning College Physics writes this quantity as PEg; University Physics and this page write U. Same quantity. And Trevor's handwritten caution, which is worth more than the symbol: the signs here can be tricky — always think through what makes sense. If your algebra says lifting a box lowered its potential energy, the algebra is wrong, not the world.

Which way is positive

There is a choice to make and Trevor makes it explicitly: generally, using up as positive keeps the signs logical — that is, lifting an object gives it more potential energy.

The reference level is yours to choose Only changes in gravitational potential energy are physical. h is measured from wherever you declare h = 0 — the floor, the bench, sea level — and a different choice shifts every U by the same constant, leaving every ΔU untouched. Declare it once at the start of a problem and do not move it halfway through.

In the wild — launching anvils

Trevor closes the chapter with a short deck built around a video rather than a derivation, and it is a better exercise than it sounds: it is the whole module's toolkit pointed at a measurement you have to extract yourself.

The challenge, as Trevor sets it

How to Shoot an Anvil 200 Feet — RiverfrontTimes video, published 20 Oct 2009. An anvil is launched vertically by a charge of black powder.

  1. Use the time of flight from the video to determine how high the anvil goes.
  2. How does that compare with the name of the video?
  3. Do it first with the kinematics equations.
  4. Then use that information to determine the launch speed using the conservation of energy.

The two routes are the point. Kinematics gets the height from the time; energy conservation gets the launch speed from the height, with ½mv2 at the bottom equal to mgh at the top. The mass cancels, which is worth noticing — you can answer this without knowing how heavy the anvil is.

About the number in the title Time the flight yourself before you trust the headline. A symmetric flight of total time T reaches h = g(T/2)2/2, so a 200-foot (61 m) apex needs about 7 seconds in the air. Whether the video shows that is the exercise — and “the title is optimistic” is a legitimate finding, provided you can show the arithmetic that says so.

Review

Potential energy— “stored” energy, recoverable because the force that stored it is conservative.
Gravitational— U = mgh, written PEg in College Physics.
Lifting— stores gravitational potential energy. Gravity does negative work.
Lowering— “removes” it. Gravity does positive work.
The habit— fix h = 0 once, let the cosine supply the signs, and check the answer against what should obviously happen.
