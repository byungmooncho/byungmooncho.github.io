Power and Efficiency

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Work does not care how long you took. Carry a box up three flights in a minute or in an hour and the work is the same number. But everyday experience insists those are not the same effort, and the quantity that captures the difference is power — work per unit time. Efficiency is the companion question: of the energy you put in, how much came out as the thing you wanted?
You already know this Work from Ch07a, gravitational potential energy from Ch08a, and the idea that energy is conserved. Power introduces no new physics at all — it is a rate, and the whole page is about what dividing by time buys you.

Work does not involve time

Trevor sets the contrast up before defining anything. The amount of work done on an object depends only on the forces involved and how far the object is moved — nothing in W = F⃗ · d⃗ mentions a clock.

You know, though, that it takes more “effort” to do work quickly. That involves the time, and the quantity is power.

The definition and its units
P = WΔt   =   ΔEΔt
Unit— the watt. 1 W = 1 J/s — one joule per second.
Horsepower— 1 hp = 746 W.

Power is a rate, so it can be written with either work or energy on top. Those are the same statement: the energy converted and the work done are the same number, counted once from the force side and once from the energy side.

The kilowatt-hour, and a unit that reads backwards

The quantity on your electricity bill is the kilowatt-hour, and it catches people out every time, because it looks like a rate and is not.

1 kW·h = (1000 J/s)(3600 s) = 3.6 × 106 J

A kilowatt is a power; multiply it by a time and you are back to an energy. The utility sells you energy and prices it by the kilowatt-hour because the joule is inconveniently small — 3.6 million of them in one unit on the bill.

The unit trap A kilowatt-hour is not “kilowatts per hour”. It is kilowatts times hours. If you ever find yourself writing kW/h, stop — that would be a rate of change of power, which is not what anyone is billing you for.

Running up stairs

Trevor's worked case, and the one that makes power physical rather than algebraic: climbing a staircase raises you by a height h, which takes mgh of energy however fast you go. Run instead of walk and the energy is unchanged — the time is what you have altered.

Why you are winded at the top Trevor's own answer, kept because it is the physiological half of the same point: you feel winded after running up a set of stairs because you have used up your “easy to use” energy stores and now must bring in more oxygen. The energy was always going to cost you mgh. Running demanded it at a rate your fast stores could supply and your aerobic system then has to repay.

Efficiency

Efficiency is the measure of how much usable work is done by a system — the ratio of work output to energy input.

Eff = WoutEin   × 100%

A ratio of two energies, so it is dimensionless and usually quoted as a percentage. Nothing in the definition says where the rest of the energy went — only that it was not the output you wanted.

Be careful what you call “useful” Trevor's caution, and it is sharper than it first looks. An incandescent light bulb is inefficient if you don't need the heat — but it can and does help warm a cold space. The same bulb has two different efficiencies depending on what you decided to count as the output. Efficiency is a statement about your purpose, not only about the device.

Review

Power— the rate at which work is done, or energy is converted. P = W/Δt, measured in watts.
1 watt= 1 joule per second. 1 hp = 746 W.
Kilowatt-hour— an energy, not a power: 3.6 × 106 J.
Efficiency— useful work out divided by energy in. Depends on what you count as useful.
