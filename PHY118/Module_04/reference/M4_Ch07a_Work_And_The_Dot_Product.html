Work and the Dot Product

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Everyday “work” means effort. Physics work is narrower and it is the narrowness that makes it useful: only the part of a force that lies along the motion counts. Push a wall for an hour and you have done none. That restriction is what the dot product encodes, and it is the reason cos θ is in the formula rather than bolted on as a correction.
You already know this Vectors, components, and resolving a vector onto an axis from Module 2; free body diagrams and the normal force from Module 3. The dot product is new as a name, but “the component of one vector along another” is a Module 2 move you have made many times.

Energy, briefly, before work

Trevor opens with the orientation rather than a definition: energy is the ability to do work, and it comes in two broad kinds.

Potential energy— “stored” energy. Chemical (gasoline), gravitational (water behind a dam), elastic (a compressed spring).
Kinetic energy— energy due to the motion of a body.

Both are taken up properly elsewhere in this module — kinetic energy on Ch07b, gravitational potential energy on Ch08a. This page is about the quantity they are both defined against.

Force and displacement

The definition, and the unit that follows from it
W = F⃗ · d⃗ = F d cos θ

Work is measured in N·m — newton·metre — and that combination has its own name, the joule. One joule is one newton·metre. (Trevor notes in passing that “joule” rhymes with “school”.)

A joule, physically Trevor's calibration: a quarter-pounder with the bun weighs roughly one newton. Lift it one metre and you have done about one joule of work. It is a small unit — which is why the car example on Ch07b comes out in the millions.

The dot product

The dot product — also called the scalar product — is one of the two ways to multiply vectors together. (The other is the cross or vector product.) Its result is a scalar, not a vector, which is why work has a size but no direction.

What it means, in words: the magnitude of the component of vector 1 along the direction of vector 2, multiplied by the magnitude of vector 2. The useful thing about that sentence is that it is symmetric — you can take the component of either vector along the other and get the same number.

The consequence worth carrying A force at right angles to the motion does zero work, however large it is. The normal force on a sliding box, the tension in the string of a conical pendulum, the centripetal force on anything in a circle — all do no work. This is not a special case to remember; it is cos 90° = 0.

Worked example — pulling a suitcase

A traveler pulls her suitcase through the airport, with the handle making a 60° angle from the horizontal. If she pulls on the handle with a constant force of 200 N, what work does she do in pulling the suitcase a distance of 50 m?

Trevor asks first

Which of these is the most accurate free body diagram?

Answer: 2. The handle is at 60°, so the pull is drawn at 60° — no steeper, no shallower, and certainly not horizontal. Option 3 invents a second force that nothing in the problem supplies. Drawing the pull at the stated angle is the whole job of the diagram here, because the angle is what the cosine will eat.
The arithmetic
W = F⃗ · d⃗
W = Fp d cos θ
W = Fp d cos 60°
W = (200 N)(50 m)(½)
W = 5000 J

Half the pull is wasted lifting against the handle's angle. Had she pulled horizontally with the same 200 N she would have done 10 000 J — twice the work on the same suitcase over the same distance. The cosine is not a formality.

Worked example — lifting a brick

A 2.0 kg brick is lifted 2 metres using a force of 50 N. How much work is done on the brick?

Net force first, then the work
Identify the forces, find the netFind the direction of motion, apply the equation
F⃗net = F⃗app + F⃗g
Fnet = Fapp − Fg 		W = F⃗net · d⃗
W = (Fapp − Fg) d cos θ
W = (Fapp − mg) d cos θ
W = (50 N − 2 kg · 9.8 m/s2)(2 m) cos 0°
W = (50 N − 20 N)(2 m)(+1)
W = (30 N)(2 m)
W = 60 J

This is the net work — the total done on the brick by everything acting. The work done by the applied force alone would be (50 N)(2 m) = 100 J, and gravity does −40 J over the same lift. Which number a question wants is the question's business, not the formula's.

Read the question Trevor's own closing instruction, and the one that costs the most marks: does it want the total work, or the work done by a single force? The two differ by exactly the work the other forces did, and nothing in the symbols tells you which was meant.

Review

Energy— the ability to do work.
Work— W = F⃗ · d⃗ = Fd cos θ, measured in joules. 1 J = 1 N·m.
The dot product— gives a scalar. Only the component of the force along the motion counts.
Perpendicular— a force at 90° to the motion does no work, whatever its size.
Before you answer— total work, or the work done by one named force? Decide before you compute.
