Momentum and Impulse

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Newton's second law has a second reading. Instead of “force equals mass times acceleration”, write it as “force equals the rate of change of mv” — and a new conserved quantity appears, along with a tool for every collision problem. Momentum is what survives an interaction; impulse is how it gets transferred, and stretching the transfer over more time is the whole engineering of safety.
You already know this ΣF⃗ = m a⃗ and Newton's third law from Module 3; vectors and components from Module 2; inertia, which this page will carefully separate from momentum.

Momentum of a single object

Momentum is the mass of an object times its velocity. It is a vector, so it has a magnitude and a direction — and the direction is where most sign errors come from.

p⃗ = m v⃗      units: kg·m/s

There is no special name for the unit. A 1 kg mass moving at 1 m/s has one kilogram-metre per second of momentum, and that is what it is called.

Do not mix up momentum and inertia Trevor's own warning, with his two examples. Inertia depends only on the mass. An object can have a large inertia and no momentum — a parked lorry. Momentum depends on both the mass and the velocity. An object can have a small mass and a large momentum — a bullet. Inertia is a tendency; momentum is a quantity you compute, conserve, and track through a collision. Newton's second law, revisited

This is the hinge of the whole chapter. Write the second law with the acceleration expanded, and momentum falls out without any new assumption.

From F = ma to the impulse–momentum theorem
ΣF⃗ = m a⃗ = m Δv⃗Δt = Δ(m v⃗)Δt = Δp⃗Δt
ΣF⃗ Δt = Δp⃗     units: N·s

The left-hand side is the impulse. Force alone does not change momentum — force applied for a time does. And N·s is the same unit as kg·m/s, which it must be: 1 N·s = 1 kg·m/s²·s = 1 kg·m/s.

And conservation, if nothing pushes from outside
If  ΣF⃗ext = 0   then  Δp⃗ = 0   so  p⃗i = p⃗f

Conservation of momentum is not a separate law. It is the second law with the net external force set to zero. The word external is doing real work: forces between the colliding objects are internal to the system and cancel in pairs by Newton's third law.

Using it: collisions

Trevor's procedure, which is worth following literally:

1. Look— just before the interaction and just after it. What happens during does not matter.
2. Change— the vector equation into an algebra equation, by choosing a positive direction and giving every velocity a sign.
3. Simplify— and solve.
Why “just before and just after” is allowed During the collision the forces are enormous, rapidly changing, and unknown. The point of the momentum method is that you never need them: whatever they did, they did it in equal and opposite pairs, so the total momentum came out the same as it went in. Two snapshots replace the whole messy middle.

Internal explosions, and rockets

The same algebra runs backwards. Start with everything at rest, let an internal force push the pieces apart, and the total momentum must still be zero — which forces the pieces to move in opposite directions.

Impulse — stretching the time

Read ΣF⃗ Δt = Δp⃗ with the right-hand side fixed and it becomes a design rule. For a given interaction the change in momentum is settled — you are going from this speed to that one regardless. What you can still choose is how long it takes.

F⃗avg = Δp⃗Δt   →   double Δt, halve the average force

This is why you roll with a punch. The transfer of momentum takes place over a longer time, which reduces the force — and it is force, not momentum, that does the damage.

Airbags and collapsible frames work the same way Trevor's numbers: the airbag deploys before you reach it, in about 1/20 s. It then extends your stopping time by a factor of two or more, and it spreads the force over a larger area as well. The car's crumple zones are the same trick at vehicle scale — a car that crumples is not failing, it is lengthening Δt. Review

Momentum— p⃗ = mv⃗, a vector, in kg·m/s. Components can be treated separately.
Not inertia— inertia depends on mass alone; momentum needs the velocity too.
Impulse— ΣF⃗Δt = Δp⃗. Newton's second law, rewritten.
Conservation— no net external force → total momentum unchanged. Internal forces cancel in third-law pairs.
The design rule— increasing the interaction time reduces the average force during the collision.

What momentum conservation alone cannot tell you is how much kinetic energy survives. That question — elastic, inelastic, and everything between — is the sibling page Ch09b · Energy in Collisions.
