Work Done by Variable Forces

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea W = Fd cos θ assumes the force holds still while the object moves. A spring does not: the further you stretch it, the harder it pulls back. The fix is to stop treating work as a product and start treating it as an area — the area between the force curve and the distance axis on a force–distance graph. For straight-line graphs that area is a rectangle or a triangle and plain geometry gives the work exactly.
You already know this Work as F⃗ · d⃗, the dot product, and the spring force Fs = k Δx from the springs material in Module 3. Nothing new is assumed here beyond the area of a rectangle and the area of a triangle.

Finding the work done graphically

Start with the easy case — a constant force F(x) = Fo pushing parallel to the motion. Plot force against position and the graph is a flat line. The work is the familiar product, and on the graph that product is the area of a rectangle.

The area is the work — check the units
W = F⃗ · d⃗  =  F d cos θ   →   W = Fo (x2 − x1)

Height is measured in newtons and width in metres, so the area carries units of N·m — which is the joule. That is not a coincidence to be memorised; it is the reason the area interpretation works at all, and it is the check to run whenever you read work off a graph.

Variable forces — the spring

Now let the force change as the object moves. The standard example is a spring: pull the block out by Δx and the spring pulls back with Fs = k Δx. The force is not one number any more, so there is no single F to put in W = Fd cos θ. The graph, though, is still perfectly well behaved — it is a straight line through the origin with slope k, and the area under it is a triangle.

Reading the triangle
W = Fs Δx cos θ
W = ½ (k Δx) · Δx  cos 0°
W = ½ k (Δx)2

The ½ is the only thing that distinguishes this from the constant-force result, and it comes straight from the shape of the region — a triangle rather than a rectangle. Another way to say it: over the stretch, the spring force runs from 0 up to k Δx, so its average value is half the final value.

Watch the sign convention This is the work done against the spring in stretching it — the force you apply and the displacement point the same way, so cos 0° = +1. The spring’s own force points back toward equilibrium, opposite the displacement, so the spring does −½k(Δx)2 of work over the same stretch. Decide which force you are asking about before you pick the sign.

When you can use geometry

Any force–distance graph made of straight lines can be handled with nothing more than the areas of rectangles and triangles. Two cases cover almost everything you will meet: a spring stretched from its relaxed length, and a spring stretched further from an already-stretched position.

The trapezoid, step by step
W = ½(F2 − F1)Δx + F1Δx
W = ½(k x2 − k x1)Δx + F1Δx
W = ½k(x2 − x1)Δx + k x1Δx
W = ½k(Δx)2 + k x1Δx

The left-hand case is the right-hand case with x1 = 0 — the extra k x1Δx term vanishes. So there is really one result here, not two, and it is worth recognising that rather than memorising both.

When the force or the direction varies

Straight-line graphs are the friendly case. In general the force can change size and direction while the object travels a curved path, and then no single rectangle or triangle will do. Trevor’s move here is the one that underlies every integral you will ever meet: chop the path into pieces short enough that the force is near enough constant over each one, work out F cos θ Δℓ for each piece, and add them up.

Add up the pieces
W = Σ i = 1 8  Fi cos θi Δℓi

Each term is a bar in the lower panel: its height is Fi cos θi and its width is Δℓi, so each term is an area and the sum is the total signed area. Make the pieces shorter and the estimate improves, because the force has less room to change across any one of them.

Be careful of the units when looking at graphs

The area trick is not about work. It is about what happens whenever you multiply the quantity on the vertical axis by the quantity on the horizontal one — and three graphs that look nearly identical give three completely different physical quantities. Read the axis labels, not the shape.

The habit worth forming Before you compute an area on any graph in this course, multiply the units on the two axes and see what comes out. N × m gives joules, so that area is work. (m/s) × s gives metres, so that area is a displacement. N × s gives N·s, so that area is an impulse. The units tell you what you have computed, which is more reliable than remembering which graph was which.

Review

The rule— The work done can be found from a force–distance graph by determining the area between the force function and the x-axis.
Straight lines— Rectangles and triangles are enough. A trapezoid is a rectangle plus a triangle.
The spring— W = ½k(Δx)2 from the relaxed length; add k x1Δx if it was already stretched.
Curved paths— Chop into short pieces, compute F cos θ Δℓ on each, and add — signs included.
Units first— Multiply the two axis units to find out what the area means.

For more complicated variable forces, methods from the calculus determine the same area analytically. In PHY 118 that is the usual route; in College Physics the graphical method is the whole story, and it is enough for every spring problem in the course.
