Impulse Is The Change Of Momentum

SynopsisMomentum, p, measured in kg m/s, is a fundamental physical quantity, as fundamental as matter and energy.  You learned that just as matter and energy’s total always remains the same, i.e., conserved, the total momentum always remains the same.  So, if you add up the momentum of every single matter in the universe, this total momentum of the universe will remain constant.  Such a calculation is likely intractable given the vast size of the universe, but the idea is clear.  We can, however, define our own “universe” or, in physics lingo, “system.”  The system is what we represent to be the universe of interest.  If we also make this universe “closed,” we now have a completely contained system where everything inside this system strictly follows all conservation laws (matter, energy, and momentum).  The idea of the “system” is an important topic, but for now, it suffices to say that a “closed system” is a contained universe of interest as we define it.  For example, a system might be two balls in an empty space (no external forces like gravity or friction) moving toward each other along a straight line.

The primary focus of this Note is impulse.  As the title states, an impulse is simply the change of momentum.  That is it.  So, using the usual physics notation for change, delta, this change of momentum is Δp and follows the standard convention of final minus initial.  Impulse has a symbol and unit: J [kg m/s = N s] where the second equality is (Newton second, and you should convince yourself that [N s] is indeed the same as [kg m/s]).

Impulse: J = Δp = pf – pi.  So what about impulse?  Remember, a moving massive object such as a freight train is almost impossible to stop with the strength of a typical human.  It’s because of the massive momentum of the freight train, and to cause the change in momentum (slow it down, for example), a very large force is required.  It turns out we have a bit of a way out: This change in momentum is brought about by the applied force times the duration of this applied force.  So, if you can only apply a small force, so long as you can apply this force for a very long time, you will be able to cause enough change in the momentum to slow/stop the freight train, eventually.

Therefore, another statement for impulse is: J = FΔt, where F is the applied force in N and Δt is the duration of the applied force in seconds.  This way of stating impulse is actually just a variation of Newton’s second law, F = ma.  If you write a = Δv/Δt (change of velocity over the change of time), you see that Newton’s second law is actually F = m(Δv/Δt), and mΔv is none other than the change in momentum, J!  J = Δp = mΔv = m(vf – vi) and, therefore, F = J/Δt.  Rearranging, J = FΔt.

In this Note, we will use the impulse equation to solve problems involving momentum change, i.e., collisions.

A force, for a time

Push on something for a while and its momentum changes by exactly force times time.  Two things to keep straight: the impulse is the change, so it is final minus initial with the signs in; and a newton-second is a kilogram-metre-per-second, so the two sides really are the same kind of quantity.

  1. A tow-truck applies a force of 2000 N on a 2000-kg car for a period of 3 seconds.  (A) What is the magnitude of the change in the car’s momentum?  (B) If the car starts at rest, what will be its speed after 3 seconds?
  2. A 2-kilogram body is initially traveling at a velocity of 40 meters per second east.  If a constant force of 10 newtons due east is applied to the body for 5 seconds, the final speed of the body is: 15 m/s / 25 m/s / 65 m/s / 130 m/s.
i) (A) Δp = FΔt = (2000 N)(3 s) = 6000 N·s = 6000 kg·m/s — [N·s] = [kg·m/s²][s] = [kg·m/s]
   (B) Δp = m(vf − vi): 6000 = (2000)(vf − 0) → vf = 3 m/s
ii) pf = pi + J = (2)(40) + (10)(5) = 80 + 50 = 130 kg·m/s → vf = 130/2 = 65 m/s (choice 3) — 130 is the momentum, not the speed

Stopping something: the force depends on how long you take

The momentum change in a stop is fixed by what was moving — mv down to zero.  What you get to choose is the time.  A haystack, an airbag, bent knees: all of them take the same impulse and spread it over a longer Δt, and F = J/Δt comes down with it.

Run it.  Stop a moving cart.  Pick the stopping time; the impulse is fixed by the cart, so watch what happens to the force — and look at the force–time graph, where the shaded area is the impulse and never changes.

The instant before a batter hits a 0.14-kilogram baseball, the velocity of the ball is 45 meters per second west.  The instant after the batter hits the ball, the ball’s velocity is 35 meters per second east.  The bat and ball are in contact for 1.0 × 10−2 second.  Calculate the magnitude of the average force the bat exerts on the ball while they are in contact.
  3. In an automobile collision, a 44-kilogram passenger moving at 15 meters per second is brought to rest by an air bag during a 0.10-second time interval.  What is the magnitude of the average force exerted on the passenger during this time?  440 N / 660 N / 4400 N / 6600 N.
i) Δp = (60)(20) = 1200 kg·m/s = F(0.50) → F = 2.4 × 10³ N (choice 4)
ii) East positive: vi = −45, vf = +35.  Δp = m(vf − vi) = (0.14)(+35 − (−45)) = (0.14)(80) = 11.2 kg·m/s;  F = 11.2 / 0.010 = 1.1 × 10³ N — the signs are the whole question: a reversal is 80 m/s of change, not 10
iii) Δp = (44)(15) = 660 N·s = F(0.10) → F = 6600 N (choice 4) — and without the bag, Δt would be far shorter and F far larger: that is what the bag is for

Non-constant forces

But not all forces are constant.  What do you do if a changing force is applied for a period of time?  In that case, we can make a graph of the force applied on the y-axis vs. time on the x-axis.  The area under the Force–Time curve is the impulse, or change in momentum.

  1. For the case of the sample graph, determine the impulse applied by calculating the area of the triangle under the curve.
  2. A 2.0 kg cart at rest receives that impulse.  How fast is it moving afterwards?
  3. What constant force, acting for the same 10 s, would have delivered the same impulse?  Mark it on the graph.
i) J = area of the triangle = ½bh = ½(10 s)(5 N) = 25 N·s
ii) 25 = (2.0)(vf − 0) → vf = 12.5 m/s
iii) Favg = J/Δt = 25/10 = 2.5 N — a horizontal line at 2.5 N; the rectangle under it has the same area as the triangle.
