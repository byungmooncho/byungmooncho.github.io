Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces

SynopsisYou have been told twice now that gravity gives back what it takes and friction never does.  Fair enough — but how would you know?  Hand someone a force they had never met before and ask them which kind it is: what would they actually do to find out?

There is a test, and it is simple enough to run in your head.  Take the object on a round trip and count the work.  This Note runs that test, and then follows what it buys us: the reason height has an energy attached to it at all, a second force that passes the test, and an honest answer to where the joules friction takes actually end up.

The test: take it round a loop

A round trip is any journey that finishes exactly where it started — same place, same height.  Over a whole round trip the object has gained nothing and lost nothing in position, so if a force is the kind that gives back what it takes, the work it did on the way out must be undone on the way home.  The total should come to zero.  If it does not come to zero, that force kept something.  That is the whole test, and everything else in this Note follows from it.

  1. Say in your own words what has to be true at the end of a round trip for the test to apply.
  2. Before we run it: predict the answer for gravity, and for friction.

Run it.  A 2.0 kg block goes round a closed loop that rises 1.5 m and comes back down — the same loop on two benches, side by side.  The left bench is smooth; the right one is rough, friction 5.0 N throughout.  Drag the block round and watch the running totals: gravity’s work on each bench, and friction’s.  Then take the long route — same hill, twice the distance.

Gravity passes, and the route never matters

Here is the surprising part, and it is worth sitting with.  Carry an object from A up to B and the work gravity does on it is −mgh — the same number whether you go straight up, up a long shallow ramp, or wander all over the hillside first.  Gravity cannot see your route.  It only sees how much higher you finished.  So on a round trip, where you finish no higher at all, the work comes to exactly zero.  A force like this is called conservative.

  1. A 2.0 kg book is carried from A to B, a height of 1.5 m.  Calculate the work gravity does along the straight route.  Take g = 9.8 m/s².
  2. Now calculate it along the other two routes.  What do you get?
  3. The book is carried back down to A.  How much work does gravity do over the whole round trip?
  4. One of the three routes is much longer than the others.  Say why that makes no difference to your answer.
straight up: Wg = −mgh = −(2.0)(9.8)(1.5) = −29.4 J — gravity pulls down while the book goes up
the ramp, the wander: −29.4 J and −29.4 J — same start height, same finish height, same number
back down to A: +29.4 J, so the round trip is 0 — what was taken on the way up is handed back on the way down
the long route: only the sideways part is longer, and gravity does no work on sideways motion — it is square to the force.

Friction fails, and the route is all that matters

Now the same test on friction.  Friction always points against the motion — that is its whole character — so when the object turns round and comes back, friction turns round with it and opposes the return trip too.  It takes on the way out and it takes again on the way home.  The round trip cannot come to zero; it comes to a negative number that gets bigger the further you travel.  A force like this is called non-conservative.

  1. A 2.0 kg block is pushed 3.0 m across a rough bench and then pushed straight back to where it started.  Friction on it is 5.0 N throughout.  Calculate the work friction does on the way out.
  2. Calculate the work friction does on the way back.
  3. Add them.  Is the round trip zero?
  4. Take the block the long way round instead — 6.0 m out and 6.0 m back.  Now how much work does friction do?
  5. Compare that with the round trip for gravity in item 5, and say what the difference is.
out: Wf = −fd = −(5.0)(3.0) = −15 J
back: friction has turned round too, so again −15 J — not +15
round trip: −30 J.  Not zero.  Friction kept 30 J.
the long way: −(5.0)(12.0) = −60 J — twice the distance, twice the loss.
gravity’s round trip was 0 and did not care about the route; friction’s is negative and is nothing but the route.

Now forget the round trip and just go from A to B.  Two routes climb the same 1.5 m: a direct one, 3.0 m long, and a detour three times as long that wanders up past B and back down before it arrives.  Run both on a smooth hillside and on a rough one, and watch the total work done on the block by gravity and friction together.  On which hillside does the route matter?

This is why height has an energy attached to it

We have been writing PE = mgh since Note 1 without asking what entitles us to.  Here is the answer.  Because gravity’s work depends only on where you start and where you finish, you can label every height with a number and be sure the label is honest — the energy an object has at height h does not depend on how it got there.  That label is the potential energy.  A conservative force is exactly a force you are allowed to do this for, and WC = −ΔPE is that permission written down.

Friction gets no such label.  There is no “friction potential energy” you could write on the bench, because the energy lost depends on the path taken and not on where the block ended up.  Two blocks sitting side by side on the same bench can have lost wildly different amounts.  That is the real difference between the two kinds of force, and the round-trip test is how you tell which is which.

  1. Explain, in a sentence a classmate would follow, why you can write PE = mgh for gravity but cannot write anything like it for friction.
  2. Two identical blocks sit at the same spot on the same bench.  One was slid there directly, the other took a long detour.  Do they have the same PE?  Has the same amount of energy been lost to friction?
i) A potential energy is a label on a place.  Gravity’s work depends only on the place you end up, so the label is honest; friction’s work depends on the route, so no label on a place could ever record it.
ii) Same spot, same height: same PE.  Different routes: different energy lost — the detour cost more, and the bench cannot tell you which block is which by looking at where they sit.

A second force that passes: the spring

Gravity is not the only conservative force, and a spring is the one you can hold in your hand.  Compress a spring and it pushes back with F = kx, where k is the spring constant in N/m and x is how far you have squashed it from its natural length.  Let it go and it gives the energy straight back — round trip, zero, test passed.  So a spring gets a potential energy of its own: PE = ½kx².  Note the square: squash it twice as far and it stores four times as much.

  1. A spring with k = 200 N/m is compressed 0.15 m.  How hard does it push back at that moment?
  2. How much energy is stored in it?
  3. The spring is released and pushes a 0.50 kg cart away along a smooth track.  How fast does the cart leave?
  4. Compress the same spring 0.30 m instead — twice as far.  How many times more energy is stored?
F = kx = (200)(0.15) = 30 N
PE = ½kx² = ½(200)(0.15)² = 2.25 J
all of it becomes the cart’s KE: ½(0.50)v² = 2.25 → v = 3.0 m/s
at 0.30 m: ½(200)(0.30)² = 9.0 J — four times as much, for twice the squash.

Where friction’s joules actually end up

Calling the energy “lost” or “wasted” is a bit of a dodge, because energy is conserved and it cannot have gone nowhere.  It went into thermal energy — the block and the bench are both very slightly warmer than they were, and the warmth is spread through enormous numbers of jostling atoms.  Nothing has been destroyed.  What has happened is that the energy has been shared out so finely that you can no longer get hold of it to push anything with, and that is what makes friction non-conservative rather than merely inconvenient.

  1. The round trip across the bench in items 7–9 removed some joules from the block.  Say where they are now.
  2. Rub your hands together hard for a few seconds.  Describe what you just did using WNC.
  3. Energy is conserved, yet we say mechanical energy is lost to friction.  Explain why both statements are true.
i) In the block and the bench, as thermal energy — 30 J spread through their atoms.  Warmer, not gone.
ii) Your muscles did work on your hands; friction between them did negative work on the motion, WNC < 0, and that energy appeared as warmth in your palms.
iii) The total is conserved: KE + PE + thermal is unchanged.  The mechanical part, KE + PE, went down by exactly the thermal energy that appeared.  “Lost” means lost to mechanics, not lost from the universe.

Looking ahead

You now have the two kinds of force and the test that separates them, so every energy problem from here splits the same way: the conservative part becomes a change in potential energy, and whatever is left over is WNC.  Next we put a clock on it — not how much energy moved, but how fast.
