Measurement and Uncertainty: Identifying Four Metals by Density

Synopsis You will determine the density of four metal rods twice — first with an imprecise set of tools, then with a precise set — and use each density to identify the metal. The physics here is simple; the skill being practiced is not the physics. This lab is about recording every measurement to the precision of the device that made it, reporting each value with the correct significant digits and its uncertainty, and judging a result by its percent error.

Performance Expectations By the end of this lab, you will be able to…
  • PE1Read a device with tick marks — a ruler or a spring scale — down to the smallest tick, estimate one more digit between the ticks, and give the reading an uncertainty of half the smallest division, rounded to match the reading.
  • PE2Read a Vernier caliper and a digital scale exactly as they resolve the reading, without guessing, and give each reading its uncertainty.
  • PE3Report every measured value with its uncertainty — for example 3.30 ± 0.05 cm — and with the correct significant digits.
  • PE4Compute a volume, a density and a percent error from averaged measurements, and show one sample calculation of each.
  • PE5Identify a metal from its density, using the percent error and the rod's appearance, and apply the 10% rule.
  • PE6Compare two tool sets using your own numbers: which was more precise, which gave the more confident identification, and whether the extra time was worth it.
📏The one idea that matters: the device sets the uncertainty

Where the last digit of your reading comes from depends on the kind of device you are holding.

📐Devices with tick marks — you estimate the last digit Read down to the smallest tick mark, then estimate one more digit between the tick marks; that estimated digit is the last significant figure of your measurement. On a centimeter ruler with millimeter tick marks, an edge that falls between 12.4 cm and 12.5 cm might be recorded as 12.43 cm — the 3 is your estimate, and the uncertainty is half the smallest division, ±0.05 cm. The spring scale works exactly the same way: with tick marks every 5 g, a needle sitting between 45 g and 50 g might be recorded as 47 g. Half the 5 g interval is 2.5 g, which rounds to ±3 g so the uncertainty has the same decimal places as the reading: 47 ± 3 g. This is a general rule for anything with tick marks — rulers, spring scales, protractors, analog meters.
🔬The Vernier caliper and the digital platform scale — no guessing These devices resolve the last digit for you, so you simply read the value and write it down exactly as given; the uncertainty is the smallest interval the device resolves. The caliper reads to 1/50 mm = 0.002 cm, so a reading of 2.540 cm carries ±0.002 cm; the digital scale reads to 0.01 g, so a reading of 46.73 g carries ±0.01 g.

Compare the spring scale's ±3 g with the digital scale's ±0.01 g — that factor of 300 is exactly what “the precision of the device” means.

Report every measured value with its uncertainty, e.g. 3.30 ± 0.05 cm — and note the decimal places of the value and the uncertainty must match. We say “uncertainty” rather than “error”: nothing is wrong with these measurements; the device simply cannot tell us more.

Predict first. The end of a rod falls between the 12.4 cm and 12.5 cm marks on a ruler with millimeter ticks, a little less than halfway. How do you record the length?

Part I — Ruler and hanging spring scale

  1. For each of the four rods, measure the length, the diameter, and the mass three times each.
  2. Hang the rod from the spring scale with the thread to read its mass.
  3. Do not repeat the same measurement three times in a row. Cycle through length, diameter, mass, then repeat, so a mistake does not get copied three times.
  4. Enter the raw readings into the Excel sheet (left-hand tables), which averages the trials for you.

Part II — Vernier caliper and platform scale

  1. Review the handout on reading a Vernier scale first.
  2. Then repeat all of Part I with the caliper and the platform scale, entering the readings into the right-hand tables.
Your caliper readings should carry more digits than your ruler readings — if they don't, you are not using the Vernier scale.

Check yourself. Your ruler gives a rod's diameter as 1.27 cm. Your caliper reading of the same rod is also written as 1.27 cm. What does that tell you?

Data analysis

From your averages, the sheet computes each rod's volume and density and suggests which metal your density is closest to. Density is mass per unit volume, and each rod is a cylinder:

ρ = m / V      V = πr²L r is the radius (half the diameter), L the length, m the mass

Because density is characteristic of a material, a measured density compared against a table of accepted values identifies the metal — and the agreement is quantified by the percent error:

% error = | ρmeasured − ρaccepted | / ρaccepted × 100%
  1. Treat the sheet's suggestion as a candidate, not an answer.
  2. Look up the accepted density of that metal in a reputable table, record where you found it (you will cite it), and type the accepted value into the yellow cell. The sheet then computes your percent error, below each table.
  3. Decide which metal each rod is, using the percent error and the rod's appearance.

Check yourself. A rod's density comes out 8.71 g/cm³. The sheet's typical values list brass at 8.5 g/cm³ and copper at 8.96 g/cm³. What should you do?

Questions for your discussion

Work these into your Discussion and Additional Questions sections, in sentences and paragraphs — never as a list. Apply the 10% rule when discussing your errors.

  1. Which tool set gave the more precise measurements, and how do you know from the numbers?
  2. Did the more precise tools give the smaller percent error and the more confident identification?
  3. How did the measurement time compare between the two tool sets?
  4. Was the extra time worth it?
Reminders: sentences and paragraphs only — no bullet points or numbered lists anywhere in the report. Never write “human error.” Your references must include the source of your density table.
