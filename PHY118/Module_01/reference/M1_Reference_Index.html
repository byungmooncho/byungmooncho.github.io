Module 1 Reference Pages

Web versions of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

How to use these These are reference pages, not the module notes. Each one is a self-contained walk-through of one slide deck — worked examples, figures you can look at as long as you like, and a short self-check at the end. Read the note for a topic first; come here when you want the idea explained a second way, or when you are working a problem and need to see the method laid out step by step. Nothing here is graded.

Chapter 1 — Introduction to Science and the Realm of Physics, Physical Quantities, and Units

  1. M1R1 Dimensions in Physics Length, mass and time and the units that go with them — and how checking the dimensions of every term catches a wrong equation before you use it.
  2. M1R2 Conversions in Physics Changing units by multiplying by a well-chosen version of the number one, so the unit you don’t want cancels — then checking the answer makes sense.
  3. M1R3 Powers of 10 The classic nine-minute film from the late 1970s: forty powers of ten, from the edge of the visible universe to the inside of an atom. Why scientific notation exists.
  4. M1R4 Significant Digits and Uncertainty Precision versus accuracy, what the digits you write claim about a measurement, and how to work out the uncertainty of a measured or calculated value.
  5. M1R5 Significant Digit Rules Which digits count, how many to keep after adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing, exact numbers, and why you round only at the end.
  6. M1R6 Trig Functions Sine, cosine, tangent and the Pythagorean theorem for the missing sides and angles of a right triangle — you will use them all semester, starting with vectors in Module 2.

Chapter 2 — Kinematics

  1. M1R7 Position, Displacement, Distance Where something is, how much ground it covered, and how far it ended up from where it started — three ideas everyday English blurs. Δ means “change in.”
  2. M1R8 Understanding the Kinematics Equations The three constant-acceleration equations, checked the way you should check any equation: what each symbol means, its units, and when it may be used.
  3. M1R9 Kinematics Equations and Graphs Each equation is the shape of a graph: a parabola for position, a straight line for velocity — and the position equation read off as the area under the velocity line.
  4. M1R10 The Velocity Kinematics Equation One worked example, done carefully: a sprinter who accelerates out of the blocks and then holds a steady speed. Two segments, two accelerations.
  5. M1R11 Two-Object Kinematics Problems A separate set of equations for each object, linked by what they share — the same position at the same instant. The police car and the speeder.
  6. M1R12 Free Fall Coordinates Constant acceleration with a known value, g = 9.80 m/s² downward. Choose which way is positive, then put the sign in front of g exactly once.
  7. M1R13 Kinematics Equations Review The three equations, their symbols and units — and reading the kinematics equation straight off a spreadsheet trendline fitted to motion-sensor data.

For Lab 1 Lab 1 (Measurement and Uncertainty) leans on two of these: Significant Digits and Uncertainty and Significant Digit Rules. Read them before you write up your measurements.
