Free Fall Coordinates

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Free fall is just constant-acceleration motion with a known acceleration: g = 9.80 m/s², pointing down. That makes the kinematics equations immediately usable — if you decide which way is positive and stick to it. In this class g is always a positive number; the direction goes into the sign in front of it, chosen from your coordinate system, exactly once.

Galileo's contribution

Objects fall with a constant acceleration (increasing speed). This assumes no air resistance (or ignores it).

Schematic redrawings after Figures 1-4 and 1-5 in Robert H. March, Physics for Poets (Contemporary Books, Chicago, 1978), p. 19.

So when is something “in free fall”? When gravity is the only thing acting on it — no air resistance, no hand pushing, no string holding. A dropped ball, a thrown ball after it leaves your hand, a rock tossed upward on its way up and on its way down: all free fall. A feather drifting down is not, but a feather in a vacuum tube is.

Objects in free fall

In this “special” case, the acceleration is 9.80 m/s². Use the letter g to represent this acceleration, so g = 9.80 m/s². For quick estimates, you could use g = 10.0 m/s².

Please note that for this class, g is a positive number.

Dr. Johnson-Steigelman prefers to keep g positive because later equations will misbehave with negative g values — for example the pendulum period, T = 2π√(ℓ/g), has no real value if g < 0.

A common mistake is for students to “correct” for the direction twice — once by writing a minus sign in the equation and again by plugging in g = −9.8. This causes the sign to be incorrect.

Many ways to account for gravity

Standard equations
x = x0 + v0t + ½at²
v = v0 + at
Keep a as a variable; substitute a = ±g when you set up the problem.
Down is negative
y = y0 + v0yt − ½gt²
vy = v0y − gt
This method switches out the a for g and puts in a negative sign, assuming that downwards is negative.
g carries its own sign
y = y0 + v0yt + ½gt²
vy = v0y + gt
This method switches out the a for g and assumes that g has a negative sign. Not what we do in this class.

Preferred method in this class:

  • We will determine the direction (and +/−) from the set-up of the problem.
  • The variable g is always positive.

Review

You have choices on how to set up your axes.

  • Once you decide, stick with it.
  • Make sure that values are consistent.
  • Add +/− carefully.
  • Always use g as a positive number, but add a negative sign when needed.
