Kinematics Equations Review

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea A closing review of Chapter 2: the three constant-acceleration equations, what each symbol means, the units on every term — and one new skill that ties it all together. When a motion sensor records a cart rolling down an incline, the spreadsheet's trendline is literally the kinematics equation with numbers filled in. Slope and intercept of a velocity graph, or the quadratic coefficients of a position graph, hand you a, v0, and x0 directly.

Understanding equations

In order to properly understand a physics equation, you should consider the following:

  1. What are the variables?
  2. What are the units of each variable?
  3. Are there any constraints on the equation?

Velocity of a cart on an incline

A motion sensor tracked a cart rolling down a ramp. Here is the cart's velocity vs. time, with the spreadsheet's linear trendline.

This graph is mostly linear. (Note that the first few and last few data points aren't quite linear — the release and the catch.)

y = m x + bspreadsheet: slope, intercept
v = a Δt + v0physics, reordered

The slope gives us the acceleration: a = 1.0564 m/s².

The intercept gives us the speed at time zero (almost): v0 = −0.0493 m/s.

Why “almost”? The cart was released from rest, so the true v0 is 0. The fit gives −0.05 m/s because the first few points (the hand letting go) don't quite follow the line. A small, physically sensible discrepancy like this is a sign the model is right; a large one would mean something else is going on.

Position of a cart on an incline

This graph is parabolic.

y = A x² + B x + Cspreadsheet quadratic
x = ½a(Δt)² + v0Δt + x0physics, reordered
A = ½a  →  a = 2A = 2 × 0.5271 m/s² = 1.052 m/s²
v0 = B = −0.0479 m/s
x0 = C = −0.0018 m

Two independent fits — the velocity slope (1.056 m/s²) and twice the position coefficient (1.052 m/s²) — agree to within half a percent. That is “using pieces of info to reconstruct other info.”

Concept question A student fits a position–time graph and reports “a = 0.5271 m/s²” straight from the x² coefficient. What went wrong?

Looking ahead: this is exactly what you do in the Module 1 labs (Notes 4–9): collect position or velocity data, fit a trendline, and translate its coefficients into a, v0, and x0 with the right units. Free fall is the case where a should come out near 9.8 m/s².

Review

  • Understanding the equations is very important in physics.
  • Know the units, forms, and variables of each equation.
  • Use pieces of info to reconstruct other info — a trendline's slope, intercept, or coefficients are the kinematics parameters, once you match the forms.
