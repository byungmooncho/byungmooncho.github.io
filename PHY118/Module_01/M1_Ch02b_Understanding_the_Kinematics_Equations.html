Understanding the Kinematics Equations

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea “Knowing” a physics equation is not the same as having memorized it. To understand an equation you should be able to say what each variable stands for, what units each term carries (and check that they all match), and what constraints limit when the equation may be used. This page walks through the three constant-acceleration kinematics equations with that checklist.

“Knowing the equations”

There is more than one way to “know” an equation, and they are not equally useful:

MemorizingYou can recite it. Useful, but fragile — one swapped letter and it's wrong.
RecognizingYou know it when you see it on a formula sheet. Necessary, not sufficient.

UnderstandingYou know what every symbol means, what units each term carries, and when the equation is allowed. This is the goal.

Notes on notation

Subscripts Allow us to “tag” variables that might be used in multiple ways.
v represents the final velocity or speed; v0 (“v-naught”) represents the initial speed.
Deltas Δ represents a difference: Δx = x − x0.
Δt vs. t Dr. Johnson-Steigelman tends to write Δt rather than t. They are the same thing whenever the clock starts at zero:
Δt = t − t0 = t − 0 = t.
Why keep the delta? Writing Δt reminds you that it is a time interval — how long the motion lasted — not a clock reading. Textbooks usually drop it, so be ready to read t as “elapsed time.”

Understanding equations: three questions

In order to properly understand a physics equation, you should consider the following:

  1. What are the variables? What physical quantity does each letter stand for?
  2. What are the units of each variable? Every term added together must carry the same units.
  3. Are there any constraints on the equation? When is it allowed — and when is it not?

The unit check is a free error detector Before you plug in numbers, write the units of each term. If one term comes out in m/s and another in m, you have remembered the equation wrong (or mixed up a variable) — and you caught it before it cost you points.

Concept question A car's acceleration changes smoothly from 0 to 3 m/s² during a 5-second interval. Can you use v = v0 + aΔt with a single value of a for that whole interval?

Review

  • Understanding an equation means that you know its variables, units, and form.
  • You also know any limitations on the equation. For all three kinematics equations, the acceleration a must be constant.
  • The three equations: v = v0 + aΔt;  x = x0 + v0Δt + ½a(Δt)²;  v² = v0² + 2a(x − x0). Unit check: m/s, m, and m²/s² on every term, respectively.
