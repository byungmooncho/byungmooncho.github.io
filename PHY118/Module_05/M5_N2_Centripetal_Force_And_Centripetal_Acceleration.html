Centripetal Force and Centripetal Acceleration

SynopsisNote 1 gave you the variables and the kinematics.  This Note asks the question it deliberately left alone: if the velocity is turning, there is an acceleration, and therefore a force — how big are they, and which way do they point?

We derive the centripetal acceleration from the change in the velocity vector rather than asserting it.  Two instants, the same speed, a small angle Δθ between them: the arc is s = rΔθ, the speed is v = s/Δt = r(Δθ/Δt), and the change in the velocity vector has length |Δv| ≈ vΔθ.  Divide by Δt and the r falls out of the ratio: a = v(Δθ/Δt) = v(v/r) = v²/r.  Then Fc = mac, and the job is always the same: find the real forces, add them, and set the sum equal to mv²/r.

After that we let α stop being zero.  The three rotational kinematics equations from Note 1's table come into their own, and the tangential-acceleration row that was dead weight there is suddenly the row you need.

We close with gravitation.  F = GM1M2/r² is a centripetal force whenever an orbit is circular, and setting it equal to mv²/r gives you the orbital speed, the orbital period, and — run backwards from g — the mass of the Earth.

A roller coaster goes round a vertical loop.  At the very top the track is above the car and the car does not fall off.  Write one sentence saying which force holds it up there — and what happens to that force if the car goes slower.

Centripetal acceleration, derived

Two instants a short time apart, the same speed at each.  Redraw the two velocity vectors from a common tail and the difference between them is a short vector pointing sideways — inward.  The triangle it makes is similar to the one the two radii make, and that similarity is the whole derivation.

Both velocity arrows are the same length — the speed never changed — and their difference is not zero.

Written out, the way it goes in four lines:

arc length  s = rΔθ     speed  v = s/Δt = r(Δθ/Δt),  so  Δθ/Δt = v/rNote 1's two equations, rearranged
|Δv| ≈ vΔθthe short side of an isosceles triangle with equal sides v and apex angle Δθ
a = |Δv|/Δt = v(Δθ/Δt) = v(v/r) = v²/rand it points where Δv points: at the centre

Centripetal is a job description, not a kind of force.  You never draw an arrow labelled “centripetal force” on a free-body diagram.  Draw the forces that are really there — tension, gravity, a normal force, friction — then notice that their sum points at the centre and equals mv²/r.

A conical pendulum: the bob travels a horizontal circle, so the net force is horizontal and points at the axis.  The vertical components must cancel, which is what fixes the tension.
  1. A conical pendulum is a weight or bob fixed on the end of a string suspended from a pivot, moving in a horizontal circular path.  (A) What is the tension in the string, in terms of m, g and θ?  (B) What is the centripetal acceleration of the bob, in terms of g and θ?
  2. The pendulum is 0.85 m long and swings at 18° from the vertical.  (A) What is the magnitude of the centripetal acceleration, in m/s²?  (B) What is the radius, in metres, of the horizontal circular path?  (C) What is the speed of the mass, in m/s?
i) (A) Vertically nothing accelerates, so T cos θ = mg and T = mg/cos θ.  (B) Horizontally the only force is the string's horizontal component, so T sin θ = mac; substitute T and the mass cancels: ac = g tan θ.
ii) (A) ac = (9.8) tan 18° = 3.18 m/s².  (B) r = L sin θ = (0.85) sin 18° = 0.26 m.  (C) ac = v²/r, so v = √(acr) = √((3.18)(0.26)) = 0.91 m/s.— the mass never appears in any of it, which is the point worth noticing
The same car at three places on the loop.  The weight always points down; the normal force always points from the track toward the centre.  What changes is whether they add or oppose.
  1. Modern roller coasters have vertical loops.  The radius of curvature is smaller at the top than on the sides, so the downward centripetal acceleration at the top is greater than g and the passengers stay pressed into their seats.  What is the speed of the coaster at the top of the loop, in m/s, if the radius of curvature there is 17.0 m and the downward acceleration of the car is 1.50g?
  2. Write Fc = mv²/r in terms of FN and Fg at each of the three places marked on the figure — top, bottom and side.
  3. At the top of the loop, what is the slowest the car can go and still keep contact with the track?  Work it symbolically.
i) ac = v²/r = 1.50g, so v = √(r(1.50)(9.8)) = √((17.0)(1.50)(9.8)) = 15.8 m/s.
ii) Top: both forces point down, which is toward the centre, so Fg + FN = mv²/r.  Bottom: the normal force is up toward the centre and the weight is down away from it, so FN − Fg = mv²/r.  Side: the weight is vertical, perpendicular to the radius, so it contributes nothing and FN = mv²/r.
iii) Contact is lost when the track stops pushing, FN = 0.  Then gravity alone supplies the whole centripetal force: mg = mv²/r, and the mass cancels again, so vmin = √(gr).  Below that the car needs less force than gravity is already providing, so it leaves the track.

Rotational motion with angular acceleration

Everything in Note 1 was uniform: α = 0, so at = rα = 0 and only the centripetal acceleration survived.  Let the spin rate change and the three rotational kinematics equations do real work, and an object carries both accelerations at once, at right angles to each other.

ω = ω0 + αt     θ = θ0 + ω0t + ½αt²     ω² = ω0² + 2αθModule 1 in different letters, exactly as the table said
  1. A track star runs a 295 m race on a 295 m circular track in 33 s.  What is his angular velocity, in rad/s, assuming a constant speed?
  2. On takeoff, the propellers on a UAV increase their angular velocity from rest at a rate of ω = 28.8t rad/s for 4.8 s.  (A) What is the instantaneous angular velocity, in rad/s, at t = 3.3 s?  (B) What is the angular acceleration, in rad/s²?
  3. The angular velocity of a rotating rigid body increases from 450 to 1400 rev/min in 135 s.  (A) What is the angular acceleration of the body, in rad/s²?  (B) Through what angle, in rad, does it turn in this 135 s?
  4. An ultracentrifuge accelerates from rest to 100 000 rpm in 2.50 min.  (A) What is the average angular acceleration, in rad/s²?  (B) What is the tangential acceleration, in m/s², of a point 4.50 cm from the axis of rotation?  (C) What is the centripetal acceleration of that point at full rpm, in m/s² and in multiples of g?  (D) What total distance, in metres, does that point travel?
i) One lap is one full turn, so Δθ = 2π rad:  ω = Δθ/Δt = 2π/33 = 0.19 rad/s.— the 295 m is there to be ignored; it tells you the radius, which nothing in this part needs
ii) (A) ω = ω0 + αt with ω0 = 0, so ω(3.3) = 28.8(3.3) = 95.0 rad/s.  (B) The coefficient of t is the angular acceleration: 28.8 rad/s².
iii) ω0 = 450(2π)/60 = 47.1 rad/s and ω = 1400(2π)/60 = 146.6 rad/s.  (A) α = Δω/Δt = (146.6 − 47.1)/135 = 0.737 rad/s².  (B) θ = ω0t + ½αt² = 47.1(135) + ½(0.737)(135)² = 1.31 × 104 rad, which is 13 077/2π = 2081 revolutions.
iv) ω = 100 000(2π)/60 = 1.047 × 104 rad/s over 150 s.  (A) α = 10 472/150 = 69.8 rad/s².  (B) at = rα = (4.50 × 10−2)(69.8) = 3.14 m/s².  (C) ac = rω² = (4.50 × 10−2)(10 472)² = 4.93 × 106 m/s², and dividing by 9.80 gives 5.04 × 105 g.  (D) θ = ½αt² = ½(69.8)(150)² = 7.85 × 105 rad, so d = rθ = (4.50 × 10−2)(7.85 × 105) = 3.53 × 104 m — about 35 km, from a point on a 4.5 cm radius.— compare (B) and (C): the tangential acceleration is 3 m/s² and the centripetal one is five million. In a spinning rotor the centripetal term is the one that destroys things
Two numbers here differ from the ones in the printed handout.  In 9(C) the handout's 4.93 × 105 g is ac divided by 10 rather than by 9.80; the correct multiple is 5.04 × 105.  In 9(D) the handout writes θ = 7.85 × 103 rad, but ½(69.8)(150)² is 7.85 × 105 — an exponent slip only, because the distance that follows it, 35 336 m, is right.
A on the rim, B and C halfway out on two different spokes, over one interval Δt.
  1. Which of the following is true regarding the angular velocity?  (A) All three points have the same angular velocity, as all three have the same angular displacement θ in the same time interval Δt.  (B) B and C have half the angular velocity of A, as they are at half the distance from the centre.  (C) B and C have twice the angular velocity of A.
  2. Which of the following is true regarding the tangential speed?  (A) All three have the same tangential speed.  (B) A has a greater tangential speed as it is further from the centre.  (C) B and C have a greater tangential speed as they are closer to the centre.
  3. Which of the following is true regarding the centripetal acceleration?  (A) All three have the same centripetal acceleration.  (B) B and C have half the centripetal acceleration of A.  (C) B and C have twice the centripetal acceleration of A.
i) (A).  One rigid object: every point sweeps the same angle in the same time, and ω has no r in it.
ii) (B).  v = rω with the same ω, so A at radius r moves twice as fast as B and C at r/2.
iii) (B).  ac = rω² with the same ω, so half the radius is half the centripetal acceleration.  Check it the other way too: halving r also halves v, and (v/2)²/(r/2) = v²/2r — the same half, as it must be.

Universal gravitation

Every pair of masses attracts, along the line between them, with

Fg = G M1M2 / r²,     G = 6.67 × 10−11 m³·kg−1·s−2and F12 = −F21: a Newton's third law pair

When an orbit is circular, that force is the centripetal force — nothing else is acting.  Setting the two expressions equal is the whole method, and the orbiting body's own mass cancels out of it.

The Earth in a circular orbit.  Gravity is the only force on it, it points at the Sun, and it is therefore the centripetal force.
  1. Set Fc = Fg for the Earth's orbit and solve for the orbital speed vE in terms of G, MSun and rSE.  Then use vE = 2πrSE/T to get the orbital period T.
  2. Evaluate T with MSun = 1.989 × 1030 kg and rSE = 150 × 106 km.  How close is it to a year, and what does the discrepancy tell you?
  3. Calculate Earth's mass given that the acceleration due to gravity at the North Pole is 9.830 m/s² and the mean radius of the Earth at the North Pole is 6371 km.
i) mEvE²/rSE = GMSmE/rSE².  The Earth's mass cancels and one power of r goes with it: vE = √(GMS/rSE).  Substituting 2πrSE/T and rearranging gives T = √(4π²rSE³/GMS) — which is Kepler's third law, T² ∝ r³, derived rather than asserted.
ii) T = √(4π²(1.50 × 1011)³ / ((6.67 × 10−11)(1.989 × 1030))) = 3.17 × 107 s = 367 days.  Two days long.  The orbit is slightly elliptical and 150 million km is a rounded mean — at three significant figures in r, and with r³ under the root, half a per cent in the radius is about three quarters of a per cent in T, which is three days.
iii) At the surface the weight mg is the gravitational force, so mg = GMEm/rE² and the test mass cancels: g = GME/rE².  So ME = grE²/G = (9.830)(6371 × 103)²/(6.67 × 10−11) = 5.98 × 1024 kg.— you weighed the Earth with a stopwatch and a metre rule
A rotating space station.  The floor is the outer wall, and the normal force it pushes inward with is the whole centripetal force — which is what the astronauts feel as weight.
  1. A rotating space station is said to create “artificial gravity” — an acceleration crudely similar to gravity.  The outer wall becomes the floor, and the centripetal acceleration supplied by the floor lets astronauts exercise and maintain muscle and bone strength more naturally.  If the station is 458 m in diameter, what angular velocity, in rad/s, would produce an artificial gravity of 9.80 m/s² at the rim?
i) The question gives the diameter, so r = 458/2 = 229 m.  From ac = rω²,  ω = √(ac/r) = √(9.80/229) = 0.207 rad/s, which is one turn every 30 seconds.— the diameter-to-radius step is the whole question; using 458 m gives 0.146 rad/s, and the printed handout's 0.10 rad/s is neither

Looking ahead

Centripetal force is Newton's second law with the geometry of a circle imposed on it; universal gravitation is the force law that makes orbits circular.  Note 3 changes the subject from a point going round a circle to a body turning about an axis, where mass that is far from the axis counts for more than mass close to it.  That is the moment of inertia, and it is what the rest of the module is about.

Where this sits. Note 1 supplied θ, ω, α and the kinematics; this note supplied the dynamics.   Between them they cover every circular-motion problem in the module that does not involve an extended body.
Back to your prediction

At the top of the loop, gravity is what holds the car in its circle — it points at the centre, which is exactly where the centripetal force has to point, and the track's push adds to it.  Go slower and less centripetal force is needed, so the track pushes less; at v = √(gr) the track has stopped pushing altogether and gravity alone is just enough.  Slower than that and the car cannot stay on the track.  If you answered “the track holds it up”, notice that the track pushes down at the top, not up.
