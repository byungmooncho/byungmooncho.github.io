Moment of Inertia: Lab Analysis

SynopsisNote 3 asserted nothing and derived everything, and it ended at τ = Iα.  The goal of this lab is to verify τ = Iα — not to use it.

The apparatus is a metal disc free to turn on an axle, with a small pulley of radius rP on the same axle, a string wound round the pulley, and a hanging mass MH on the other end of the string.  Let go, and the falling mass winds the string off and spins the disc.

Two free-body diagrams run the whole analysis: one for the hanging mass, which gives you the tension, and one for the disc, which turns that tension into a torque.  The string ties the two together — it cannot stretch, so a = rPα.  Vary MH, measure α each time, plot α against τ, and if τ = Iα is right the points lie on a straight line through the origin whose slope is 1/I.

Then the check that makes it an experiment rather than a demonstration: compare the I you read off that slope with the I = ½MDrD² you get from weighing the disc and measuring it with a ruler.  Two independent routes to the same number.
Before you read on — predict

The hanging mass is falling.  Is the tension in the string equal to its weight?  Write one sentence saying yes or no, and why.

Performance expectations

Draw the free-body diagram for each body in a connected rotational system and write Newton's second law for each.  Use the inextensible-string condition a = rα to couple them.  Predict the angular acceleration from the hanging mass.  Linearise τ = Iα and get the moment of inertia from the slope of a graph.  Compare an experimental value with an independently calculated one and account for the difference.

The apparatus, and the two free-body diagrams

Two bodies, one string.  Treat them separately first and connect them afterwards — that is the whole method, and it is the same one you used for two blocks and a pulley back in Module 3.

The apparatus and the two diagrams it splits into.  Notice what is absent from the disc's diagram: gravity and the axle's reaction both act along the axis, at zero lever arm, so the string is the only thing that can turn it.
  1. Write Newton's second law for the hanging mass, taking down as positive, and rearrange it for the tension T.
  2. Is T bigger or smaller than MHg, and by how much?  What would T be if the disc had no moment of inertia at all?
  3. Write the torque the string exerts on the disc about the axle, and then Newton's second law for rotation.  Why do the disc's weight and the axle's reaction force not appear?
i) Down positive:  MHa = MHg − T, so T = MH(g − a).
ii) Smaller, by exactly MHa.  If the string were slack T would be zero and the mass would be in free fall; if the disc could not turn at all the mass would hang still and T = MHg.  Everything in this lab lives between those two.  With I = 0 the disc offers no resistance, so a = g and T = 0.— which is the sanity check to run on your final formula before you trust it
iii) τ = rPT, and Newton's second law for rotation gives rPT = IDα.  The disc's weight and the axle's reaction both act at the axle, so their lever arm about it is zero and neither contributes any torque.  The string, at rP, is the only torque there is.

From the hanging mass to the torque

Two equations, three unknowns — a, T and α.  The third equation is the string.  It does not stretch, so the length that unwinds off the pulley is the distance the mass falls, and differentiating that statement twice ties the two accelerations together.

  1. Write the condition that ties a to α, and combine it with your two equations to eliminate T and a.  You should get a single equation in α and MH.
  2. Solve that for α.  Check the two limits:  what does your expression give as MH → 0, and what does it give if I → 0?
  3. Take rP = 0.025 m, I = 0.0030 kg·m² and a hanging mass of 0.200 kg.  Find (A) α, in rad/s²;  (B) a, in m/s²;  (C) T, in N;  and (D) τ, in N·m.  Then check (D) against Iα.
i) The string is inextensible, so a = rPα.  Substituting T = MH(g − a) into rPT = Iα and then a = rPα gives
    rP[MH(g − rPα)] = Iα.
ii) Expand and collect:  rPMHg = (I + MHrP²)α, so
    α = rPMHg / (I + MHrP²).
    MH → 0 gives α → 0 — no hanging mass, no spin.  I → 0 gives α → g/rP, so a = rPα = g: free fall, as it must be.— the MHrP² in the denominator is the hanging mass's own contribution to the system's inertia, and it is why α is not simply proportional to MH
iii) (A) α = (0.025)(0.200)(9.8)/(0.0030 + (0.200)(0.025)²) = 0.0490/0.003125 = 15.7 rad/s².  (B) a = (0.025)(15.68) = 0.392 m/s².  (C) T = (0.200)(9.8 − 0.392) = 1.88 N — against a weight of 1.96 N.  (D) τ = (0.025)(1.882) = 0.0470 N·m, and Iα = (0.0030)(15.68) = 0.0470 N·m.  They agree, as they have to — that is what we solved.

The graph, and what its slope is

Everything above assumed τ = Iα.  The experiment tests it, and the test is a straight line: vary the hanging mass, measure the angular acceleration each time, compute the torque from the tension, and see whether the points fall on a line through the origin.

Plot α on the vertical axis against τ on the horizontal and the slope is 1/I, not I.  Which way round you plot decides what you have to do to the slope to get the answer, so decide before you draw the axes.
  1. You plot α on the vertical axis against τ on the horizontal.  (A) What is the slope, in terms of I?  (B) What should the intercept be?  (C) If instead you plotted τ against α, what would the slope be?

  3. The disc itself has mass 1.50 kg and radius 0.064 m.  (A) What is ID = ½MDrD²?  (B) By what percentage does it differ from the value you got from the slope?  (C) Which of the two would you quote, and why?
i) (A) α = (1/I)τ, so the slope is 1/I.  (B) Zero — no torque, no angular acceleration.  (C) Plotted the other way round the slope is I directly, which is easier to read but harder to test, because the systematic errors show up in the intercept and that is clearer on the α-against-τ plot.
ii) The torques are 0.0121, 0.0240, 0.0356, 0.0470 and 0.0582 N·m.  Take any point and divide:  0.0470/15.68 = 0.00300;  0.0240/8.00 = 0.00300;  0.0121/4.04 = 0.00299.  A line through the origin fitted to all five gives slope = Στα/Στ² = 333 rad s−2/(N·m), so I = 1/333 = 0.00300 kg·m².  The points are collinear through the origin, which is the result: τ is proportional to α, and the constant of proportionality is I.
iii) (A) ID = ½(1.50)(0.064)² = 0.00307 kg·m².  (B) (0.00307 − 0.00300)/0.00307 = 2.3% low.  (C) Either, with the other quoted beside it — the point of the lab is that two independent routes agree to about 2%, and a single number with no comparison would not have told you that.— 2% is the right order for this apparatus; if your two values differ by 30% you have a mistake, not an uncertainty

What could make the two values disagree

They did disagree, by 2.3%.  Every term you left out of the model is a candidate, and each one leaves a different fingerprint on the graph.

  1. A constant friction torque τf at the axle opposes the motion.  Redo the rotational equation with it included, solve for α, and say what it does to the slope and to the intercept of the α-against-τ plot.
  2. Name two other things the model leaves out, and for each say whether it would make the I from the slope come out too big or too small.
i) rPT − τf = Iα, so α = (τ − τf)/I = (1/I)τ − τf/I.  The slope is unchanged at 1/I; the line is shifted right, crossing the τ axis at τf.  Friction does not corrupt the moment of inertia you read off the slope — it shows up as the intercept, which is exactly why you plot it this way round.
ii) Any two of:  the pulley's own moment of inertia, which the model lumps into nothing and which makes the measured I come out too big, since the slope is shallower than the disc alone would give;  the string's mass, same sign, same reason, and tiny;  the disc not being a uniform solid cylinder — a rim slightly thicker than the middle puts mass at large r and makes the true I larger than ½MDrD² predicts, which is the sign we actually see;  α measured over an interval in which the string has partly unwound, changing rP.

Looking ahead

This is the last of Module 5's teaching notes, and it is the one that closes the loop: Note 3 derived τ = Iα from F = ma and the bridge relations, and this Note measures it.  The sheets that remain in the module — the centripetal-force examples and the gravitation example — are practice on Notes 1 and 2 rather than new material.

Where this sits. The method generalises well past this lab.  Any two bodies joined by a string over a pulley, any rolling body on an incline, any yo-yo: one free-body diagram per body, a constraint linking the accelerations, and eliminate the tension.  The only thing that changes is which moment of inertia goes in.
Back to your prediction

No — the tension is less than the hanging mass's weight, by exactly MHa.  If the two were equal the net force on the mass would be zero and it would not accelerate at all, so the fact that it is falling faster and faster is itself the proof.  In the worked numbers the weight is 1.96 N and the tension is 1.88 N: only 4% apart, which is exactly why the mistake is so easy to make and so hard to notice.
