Centripetal Force Examples

SynopsisAn in-class sheet, not a lesson.  One situation — a child on a merry-go-round — worked all the way from the spin rate to the force, and then repeated at a different radius so that you can see which quantities changed and which did not.

Everything you need is in Notes 1 and 2:  ω from the rate of turn, vt = rω, ac = vt²/r = rω², and Fc = mac.  The work is in getting the units right at the first step and then not losing track of which radius you are at.
A correction to the printed sheet.  The stem on the paper copy reads “spinning at 10 rpm (10 rev/s)”.  The parenthesis is wrong.  10 rpm is 10 revolutions per minute, which is 0.167 rev/s — a factor of 60 away.  Use 10 rev/min.  Every number below follows from that, and if yours are 60 or 3600 times too big this is why.
Before you start — predict

Two children of the same mass ride the same merry-go-round, one at 2 m from the centre and one at 3 m.  Write one sentence saying which of them has to hold on harder, and roughly by how much.

Performance expectations

Convert a rate of turn in rev/min to an angular velocity in rad/s.  Draw a top-down free-body diagram for a body in uniform circular motion and identify which real force is playing the centripetal role.  Compute vt, ac and Fc at a given radius, and say how each one scales when the radius changes at fixed ω.

The merry-go-round

A merry-go-round of radius 3.0 m turns at a steady 10 rev/min.  It is one rigid platform, so every point on it shares the same ω — and every point at a different radius has a different speed.

The platform top-down.  The two radii are to scale: child B is at 1.5 times child A's radius, and her velocity arrow is 1.5 times as long.
  1. Convert the rate of turn to an angular velocity.  What is ω, in rad/s?  (Watch the correction above — it is 10 revolutions per minute.)
  2. Child A has mass 35 kg and sits 2.0 m from the centre.  Find (A) her tangential speed vt, in m/s;  (B) her centripetal acceleration ac, in m/s²;  and (C) the centripetal force on her, in N.
  3. Which real force on child A is the centripetal force?  Say what happens to her if the merry-go-round speeds up past the point where that force can supply what is needed.
i) 10 rev/min × (2π rad / 1 rev) × (1 min / 60 s) = 10(2π)/60 = ω = 1.047 rad/s.— multiply rev/min by 2π/60 ≈ 0.1047 and you are done; taking the printed “10 rev/s” literally gives 62.8 rad/s and every answer after it is 3600 times too large in the force
ii) (A) vt = rω = (2.0)(1.047) = 2.09 m/s.  (B) ac = vt²/r = (2.094)²/2.0 = 2.19 m/s² — or straight from ac = rω² = (2.0)(1.047)², same answer and one step shorter.  (C) Fc = mac = (35)(2.193) = 76.8 N.
iii) Friction between her and the floor of the platform, pointing inward, toward the axis.  There is nothing else available: her weight is vertical and the normal force is vertical.  Once the required mrω² exceeds what friction can supply, she stops going round with the platform and slides — outward as seen by someone riding it, in a straight line as seen from the ground.

The same child further out

Now move her, and change nothing else.  The platform is rigid, so ω is the same for both positions — which is the whole reason this comparison is clean.

The two free-body diagrams, top-down.  Same child, same ω, same force direction — only the radius is different, and the arrow is exactly 1.5 times as long.
  1. A second 35 kg child sits at the 3.0 m rim.  Is holding on easier, the same, or harder for her than for child A?  Work out vt, ac and Fc at 3.0 m and say by what factor each one changed.
  2. Child A's seat is 2.0 m out and child B's is 3.0 m out.  Without computing anything, say how vt, ac and Fc each depend on r at fixed ω — and check your answer against the numbers you just got.
i) Harder, by exactly 3/2.  vt = (3.0)(1.047) = 3.14 m/s;  ac = rω² = (3.0)(1.047)² = 3.29 m/s²;  Fc = (35)(3.290) = 115 N.  Each is 1.5 times child A's value, which is 3.0/2.0.
ii) At fixed ω, vt = rω, ac = rω² and Fc = mrω² are all three directly proportional to r, so all three scale by the same factor — 1.5 here.— it is only when you hold the speed fixed instead that ac = v²/r runs the other way; which quantity is held fixed decides the direction of the trend, and the question always tells you

Looking ahead

Everything on this sheet was uniform circular motion: ω constant, so ac constant and Fc constant.  Note 2 works the same method on situations where the centripetal force comes from something other than friction — a string, a track, gravity — and Note 2b applies it to the Moon.

Back to your prediction

The child further out has to hold on harder, and by exactly the ratio of the radii — 3.0/2.0 = 1.5, so 115 N against 76.8 N.  If you guessed “harder” but expected a bigger factor, you may have been thinking of ac = v²/r with v fixed; here it is ω that is fixed, because it is one rigid platform, and then the force is simply proportional to r.
