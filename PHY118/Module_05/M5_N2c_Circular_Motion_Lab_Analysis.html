Circular Motion: Lab Analysis

SynopsisNote 2 said that the centripetal force is whatever real force happens to be pointing at the centre, and that Fc = mvt²/r.  This lab tests the second half of that: how do we show that Fc is proportional to v²?

The apparatus is a rubber stopper on a string, swung in a horizontal circle above your head, with the string running down through a short tube and a hanging mass on the lower end.  The trick is the tube.  The tension is the same everywhere along one string, so whatever the hanging mass weighs, that is the tension pulling inward on the stopper — and the hanging mass is something you can simply choose, by counting out washers.

So you set the centripetal force, you measure the speed it produces, and you see whether doubling the force multiplies the speed by √2.  That is the whole experiment.
Before you read on — predict

You are swinging the stopper steadily and someone quietly adds washers to the hanging mass, doubling it.  To keep the stopper going round the same circle, do you have to swing it faster, slower, or the same?  Write one sentence, and if you can, a factor.

The apparatus, and the one idea that runs it

Two free-body diagrams again — one for the stopper and one for the hanging mass — and one observation that connects them and makes the whole thing work.

The ringed idea: the tension is the same throughout the string.  So the hanging weight at the bottom is the centripetal force at the top — and you set it by choosing how many washers to hang.
  1. Draw the free-body diagram for the hanging mass and write Newton's second law for it, given that it hangs still.  What does that make T?
  2. Now the stopper, seen from above.  Which force on it points at the centre of the circle, and what is its size?  Put those two results together into one statement about Fc.
  3. Why does it matter that the string runs through a tube rather than being tied to something?  What would change if you tied the lower end to the bench instead?
i) Two forces on it: its weight MHg down and the tension T up.  It hangs still, so they balance:  T = MHg.
ii) Seen from above, the only horizontal force on the stopper is the string, and the string runs to the centre, so the tension is the centripetal force.  Combining the two:  Fc = T = MHg.  That is the whole design.  The centripetal force on the stopper has stopped being something you measure and become something you choose.— the tension is the same throughout one string, which is the sentence the entire lab rests on; it is why the tube has to be smooth
iii) The tube lets the string change direction without changing tension, and it lets the hanging mass hang freely.  Tie the lower end down instead and the tension becomes whatever the knot happens to be holding — unknown, and not something you can set.  Any friction in the tube breaks the same assumption, which is why it is the first thing to suspect if your results come out crooked.

How do we show that Fc is proportional to v²?

You can set the force and you can measure the speed.  The question is what to do with the pairs of numbers so that the answer is visible rather than argued.

  1. You cannot time one revolution accurately by hand.  Say how to measure vt well, given a stopwatch, and write the formula you would use.
  2. You vary Fc by changing the number of washers, keeping r fixed.  Starting from Fc = mvt²/r, what should you plot against what to get a straight line, and what is its slope?
  3. Suppose m = 25 g, r = 0.80 m, and you hang four washers totalling 0.100 kg.  (A) What is Fc?  (B) What speed should the stopper be going?  (C) How long should 20 revolutions take?
  4. You then double the hanging mass to 0.200 kg at the same radius.  By what factor should vt change, and by what factor should the time for 20 revolutions change?  Check against the numbers.
i) Time many revolutions and divide.  Count N of them — twenty is comfortable — over a total time t, then each lap takes T = t/N and vt = 2πrN/t.  Your reaction time is the same whether you timed one lap or twenty, so spreading it over twenty divides its effect by twenty.
ii) Fc = mvt²/r is linear in vt², not in vt.  So plot Fc on the vertical axis against vt² on the horizontal; a straight line through the origin confirms the proportionality, and its slope is m/r — which you can check against the stopper you weighed and the radius you measured.— plotting Fc against vt gives a curve, and a curve cannot tell you whether the exponent is 2 or 1.8; that is the whole reason for squaring first
iii) (A) Fc = MHg = (0.100)(9.8) = 0.98 N.  (B) vt = √(Fcr/m) = √((0.98)(0.80)/0.025) = √31.36 = 5.60 m/s.  (C) One lap is 2π(0.80) = 5.027 m, so twenty laps are 100.5 m, taking 100.5/5.60 = 17.9 s.
iv) Doubling Fc doubles vt², so vt goes up by √2 = 1.41 and the time for twenty laps goes down by the same factor.  Check:  vt = √((1.96)(0.80)/0.025) = √62.72 = 7.92 m/s, which is 1.41 × 5.60; and 100.5/7.92 = 12.7 s, which is 17.9/1.41.  That factor of √2 is the result the lab is after — a force law going as v would have given you 8.9 s.

Looking ahead

The same apparatus answers a second question for free: hold the washers fixed and change r instead, and Fc = mvt²/r says the speed should go as √r.  And the honest caveat is worth saying out loud — the string is never quite horizontal, because something has to hold the stopper up against gravity, so the real apparatus is a conical pendulum and the tension is very slightly larger than MHg.  Note 2 has the conical pendulum worked in full.

Back to your prediction

Faster, by a factor of √2.  Doubling the hanging mass doubles the tension, which doubles the centripetal force; and since Fc goes as v², doubling it multiplies the speed by √2 ≈ 1.41, not by 2.  If you said “twice as fast”, that is exactly the assumption this lab is built to test, and the stopwatch will disagree with you by about 5 seconds in 18.
