Angular Momentum and Its Conservation

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Linear momentum has a rotational twin too: L = Iω. When no outside torque acts on a system, the total L before equals the total L after — which is why a skater who pulls her arms in spins faster without pushing on anything. The bookkeeping is exactly the collision bookkeeping of Module 4, with I for m and ω for v.
You already know this Conservation of linear momentum from Module 4 — p = mv, conserved when no external force acts, with kinetic energy not conserved in a sticking collision. Everything on this page is that argument run again in rotational variables, using the moment of inertia from Ch10b.

What angular momentum is

L= Iω — angular momentum, in kg·m2/s
I— moment of inertia, in kg·m2
ω— angular speed, in rad/s

The unit is worth writing out once. kg·m2 × rad/s gives kg·m2/s, because the radian is dimensionless — it is a ratio of two lengths and carries no units of its own. That is the same disappearing act radians performed in v = ωr back in Ch10a.

It has a direction too Like linear momentum, L is a vector. Its direction lies along the rotation axis, given by the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of your right hand the way the object turns and your thumb points along L. For everything on this page the axis is fixed, so the direction reduces to a sign — one sense of rotation positive, the other negative — exactly as it did for one-dimensional linear momentum.

Angular momentum is conserved

With no external torque on the system, the total angular momentum does not change. For two interacting objects that is the same four-term statement you wrote for linear collisions:

The conservation statement, three ways
Li = Lf
L1i + L2i = L1f + L2f
I1ω1i + I2ω2i = I1ω1f + I2ω2f

Trevor's closing line on the slide is the practical one: the details of the motion before and after are either given or can be determined. In other words the equation has one unknown once you have read the problem properly, and the work is in identifying which I goes with which ω — not in the algebra.

The four shapes these problems come in

Trevor's survey slide is really a catalogue of set-ups, and recognising which one you have in front of you is most of the work.

A moving mass has angular momentum too An object that is not spinning at all still carries angular momentum about a chosen axis: L = mvr, where r is the perpendicular distance from the axis to its line of travel. That is what cases 1–3 above supply on the “before” side, and it is why the answer changes when the same ball strikes the rod at a different height.

Worked example: dropping one disk onto another

A disk with I1 = 0.80 kg·m2 is turning at 12 rad/s. A second disk, I2 = 0.40 kg·m2 and at rest, is dropped onto it along the same axis and the two stick. Find the final angular speed, and the kinetic energy before and after.

Conserve L, then check K
L = I1ω1i + I2(0) = (0.80)(12) = 9.6 kg·m2/s
ωf = LI1 + I2 = 9.61.20 = 8.0 rad/s
Ki = ½(0.80)(12)2 = 57.6 J     Kf = ½(1.20)(8.0)2 = 38.4 J   ⇒   19.2 J lost (33%)

Angular momentum came out the same on both sides because that was the assumption. Kinetic energy did not, and nothing is wrong: the two surfaces rubbed against each other until they matched speeds, and that friction warmed them. This is the rotational twin of a perfectly inelastic linear collision, down to the fraction of energy lost.

Worked example: walking towards the centre

A uniform turntable of mass 120 kg and radius 2.0 m turns freely at 1.5 rad/s. A 60-kg person stands at the rim and walks in to 0.50 m from the axis. What is the new angular speed?

Inventory first, then one division
Itable = ½MR2 = ½(120)(2.0)2 = 240 kg·m2  (unchanged)
Iperson,i = mr12 = (60)(2.0)2 = 240   ⇒   Ii = 480 kg·m2
Iperson,f = (60)(0.50)2 = 15   ⇒   If = 255 kg·m2
Iiωi = Ifωf  ⇒  ωf = (480)(1.5)255 = 2.8 rad/s

Trevor's slide stops at the symbolic line mr12ωi + ½MR2ωi = mr22ωf + ½MR2ωf, which is worth reading carefully: the turntable term appears on both sides with the same I but different ω, so it does not cancel.

Where the energy goes — both ways

The two worked examples above both conserve angular momentum, and their kinetic energies move in opposite directions.

Check yourself

If angular momentum is conserved, where did the extra 476 J in the turntable example come from?

From the person. Walking inwards on a rotating platform means pushing against the surface that keeps trying to carry you outwards, and that push is applied over a distance — so it is work, done by muscles, converting chemical energy into kinetic energy. Conservation of angular momentum is a statement about torques, not about energy, and the person's feet exert no torque about the axis while they walk straight in.

Why the skater speeds up Same physics as the turntable, and it is the demonstration everyone has seen. Arms out, most of the skater's mass sits at large r and I is large. Arms in, I drops; since Iω must hold its value, ω rises to compensate. She is not pushing against the ice to spin up — the ice is nearly frictionless, which is exactly why L is conserved in the first place.

Review

Angular momentum— L = Iω, in kg·m2/s; a vector along the rotation axis.
A moving mass— carries L = mvr about an axis, with r the perpendicular distance to its line of travel.
Conservation— no net external torque ⇒ Li = Lf. Add up every contribution on each side.
Sticking together— the rotational perfectly inelastic collision: ωf = L/(I1 + I2), and energy is lost.
Changing shape— pull mass inwards and I falls, so ω rises. Energy is added by whatever pulled it in.
The warning— L conserved does not mean K conserved — check them separately, exactly as for linear collisions.
