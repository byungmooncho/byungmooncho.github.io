Moment of Inertia

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Forces make things accelerate, and mass is what resists. Torques make things spin faster, and the thing that resists is not mass — it is mass and where that mass sits. A broomstick is easy to spin about its middle and awkward to spin about its end, and nothing about it changed except the axis. That dependence has a name, a symbol and a short table, and almost everything in the rest of the module needs it.
You already know this The angular variables themselves, from Ch10a — θ, ω, α, the rotational kinematics equations and the conversions vt = ωr, at = αr. Trevor opens this pair of decks by revisiting exactly that set, with one addition worth repeating: those variables were introduced for a point on a record, and they describe an extended rigid object just as well. Every piece of it shares one θ, one ω, one α.

Newton's second law, rewritten for spinning

The linear statement is familiar: the acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the applied force and inversely proportional to its mass. The rotational statement runs parallel to it, and the parallel is exact except in one place.

LinearRotational
forces make objects accelerateunbalanced torques make objects spin faster or slower
mass resists that change — “inertia” moment of inertia resists it — sometimes called rotational inertia
depends on the amount of matter, full stop depends on the amount of matter and how far it is from the axis

That last row is the whole content of this page. The shape of an object — and the axis you choose — affects how easy it is to spin.

Start with a single point

For a “point” object, like a small ball whirled on a string, the moment of inertia is as simple as it gets.

And for a real object, add the pieces
I = Σ mi ri2

Chop the object into pieces, multiply each piece's mass by the square of its own distance from the axis, and add. Trevor's note on method: for simple objects you can just add the pieces; for symmetric ones use calculus; for anything that is neither, use numerical methods. And whatever route you take, all the little m's have to add up to M.

Where you hold the stick

Trevor's physical demonstration, and the quickest way to feel that the axis matters. Take a metre stick. Spin it about its middle, then spin it about one end. Same stick, same mass, four times the effort.

The shapes worth memorising

Why does the fraction change?

Take two round objects of the same mass and the same radius — a solid disk and a hoop. The disk's fraction is a half and the hoop's is one, so the hoop is twice as hard to spin. Nothing about the amount of matter differs. Only its placement does.

Objects that aren't on the chart

“Complicated” here means an object built out of ones that are on the chart. Those you can add, as long as every piece is measured about the same axis. Trevor's example: a stick used as the diameter of a hoop, spun about the hoop's centre.

Adding two pieces about one axis
I = Iring + Istick = MringR2 + 1/12 MstickL2
= MringR2 + 1/12 Mstick(2R)2 = MringR2 + 1/3 MstickR2

This differs from the slide, which uses Istick = ½MstickL2 and finishes at MringR2 + 2MstickR2. A rod about its centre is 1/12ML2, as the deck's own table gives two slides earlier — so the stick's contribution is a sixth of what the slide prints. Sanity check: the stick is a rod of length 2R, so its mass sits on average much closer to the axis than the hoop's does, and its term must come out smaller than MR2, not twice as large.

The one rule when adding Every term must be a moment of inertia about the same axis. A rod's 1/12ML2 is about its centre; if the axis runs through its end instead, the right entry is 1/3ML2. Picking the wrong row of the table is the most common way this calculation goes wrong — and, as above, it is a mistake that survives being written down neatly.

Review

Moment of inertia— how hard it is to change an object's rotation, the rotational counterpart of mass. Symbol I, units kg·m2.
Definition— I = Σmiri2 — for a single point, I = mr2.
It depends on the axis— not just the object. Same stick, centre vs end: 1/12 vs 1/3 of ML2.
The common shapes— disk ½MR2, hoop MR2, solid sphere (2/5)MR2, hollow sphere (2/3)MR2. All of the form (fraction or one) × MR2.
Composites— add the pieces, all measured about the same axis.
