Stability and Tipping

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Being in equilibrium says nothing about staying there. A cone standing on its base and a cone balanced on its point are both in equilibrium; nudge each one and they behave in opposite ways. The difference is what the gravitational torque does after the nudge — and the geometry decides it, through one angle: tipping begins when the weight line leaves the base.
You already know this The two equilibrium conditions from Ch12a, and the centre of mass and the gravitational torque from Ch12b. The only new idea here is asking what happens when the object is displaced a little.

Three kinds of equilibrium

All three objects below satisfy both conditions: the normal force balances the weight, and the two act along the same vertical line so neither produces a torque.

What tells them apart is the nudge

Tip each one slightly and look at where the weight now acts relative to the point of contact. That single comparison gives the whole classification.

A wording caution Trevor's middle slide labels the tilted cones “Unstable, Στ ≠ 0”. Read that carefully: a tilted cone is not in equilibrium at all — that is what Στ ≠ 0 says. “Stable” and “unstable” describe the upright positions, by what the torque does once the object is displaced from them. A cone on its base that is tipped a little and swings back is the picture of a stable equilibrium, not an unstable one.

How far before it tips?

The question that turns this into arithmetic. Take an object with base radius r whose centre of mass sits a height h above the surface. Tilt it, and the weight line sweeps towards the tipping edge. The critical tilt is the one that puts the weight line exactly over that edge.

The tipping angle
φ = 90° − tan−1 hr  =  tan−1 rh

Trevor arrives at the first form, through θ = tan−1(h/r) and θ + φ = 90°. The second form is the same thing in one step and is worth keeping in mind, because it says the tipping angle depends on nothing but the ratio r/h — wide base over high centre of mass. Neither the object's weight nor its absolute size enters.

The bookcase and the saloon car differ by a factor of six in tipping angle, and the reason is entirely the ratio. It is also why furniture sold for homes with small children comes with a strap for the wall: 9.5° is not very far.

Check yourself

Two identical filing cabinets, one empty and one filled with paper. Which tips more easily?

It depends entirely on where the paper goes, not on how much there is. Weight does not appear in φ = tan−1(r/h). Load the bottom drawers and h falls, so the cabinet becomes harder to tip; load the top drawers and h rises and it becomes easier — which is why you should never open two full top drawers at once.

Two ways to make something stable The formula offers exactly two handles: make r bigger or make h smaller. A wider wheelbase, outriggers on a crane, feet on a desk lamp — those raise r. Ballast in a keel, a heavy base on a floor lamp, a low-slung engine — those lower h. Real designs usually do both.

Review

Stable— displaced slightly, the torque returns it. The weight line still falls within the base.
Unstable— displaced slightly, the torque carries it further away. The base is a point or an edge.
Neutral— displaced, it is in equilibrium again — the contact point moves with the object.
The test— which side of the contact point the weight line falls on.
Tipping angle— φ = tan−1(r/h), with r the base radius and h the height of the centre of mass.
What matters— only the ratio r/h. Not the weight, not the overall size.
