Centripetal Force

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Go round a bend at a steady 30 mph and your speedometer says nothing is happening. Your stomach disagrees, and your stomach is right: velocity is a vector, the direction is changing, so there is an acceleration — and it points at the centre of the turn. Everything on this page follows from that one observation, including the most-misused phrase in the module. The centripetal force is not a new force. It is a job, and some real force has to do it.
You already know this Vector subtraction from Module 2, Newton's second law and free-body diagrams from Module 3, and from Ch10a the fact that the centripetal acceleration can also be written ac = ω2r. This page is where v2/r itself comes from.

The velocity is changing even though the speed is not

Take two instants a short time apart, with the object at symmetric positions either side of the top of the circle. The two velocity vectors have the same length — the speed has not changed — but they point in different directions, so v⃗2 − v⃗1 is not zero.

Put the two vectors tail to tail and the difference is the vector that closes the triangle from the head of v⃗1 to the head of v⃗2. Its direction is the thing to notice: with the object at the top of the circle, it points straight down — at the centre.

Centripetal means centre-seeking, and that is all the word claims. The acceleration is called the centripetal acceleration; the net force producing it, by Newton's second law, is called the centripetal force.

Where v2/r comes from

Trevor derives it rather than quoting it, and the derivation is worth following once because it explains the square. Resolve the two velocities into components, using the angle θ from the figure above.

Subtracting the two velocities
HorizontalVertical
v⃗1 v cos θ + v sin θ
v⃗2 v cos θ − v sin θ
Δv⃗ = v⃗2 − v⃗1 0 − 2v sin θ

The horizontal components are identical and cancel; only the vertical parts survive, and they survive doubled, pointing downwards in the figure — that is, towards the centre. The magnitude is 2v sin θ.

Now the time. In that interval the object travels the arc between the two positions, which is 2rθ long — the arc length from Ch10a, with the angle counted on both sides of the vertical. At speed v that takes Δt = 2rθ/v.

Dividing by the time, then letting the angle shrink
ac = 2v sin θΔt = 2v sin θ2rθ/v = 2v2 sin θ2rθ
for small θ,   sin θ → θ   ⇒   ac = v2r

Trevor asks the small-angle question rather than answering it: for small angles, how are sin θ and θ related? They become equal, which is only true with θ in radians — the reason the whole module insists on them. The v2 arrives honestly: one factor of v from the size of Δv⃗, the other from the fact that going faster also shortens Δt.

The centripetal force is not an extra force

This is the sentence to carry out of the module. The centripetal force is not a new interaction that appears when something goes in a circle. It is the net force in the centre-seeking direction, produced by forces that were already on your free-body diagram.

ΣF⃗ = F⃗1 + F⃗2 = ma⃗c = F⃗c

Trevor's case is an aeroplane turning. It banks, which tilts the lift vector; the lift is the only force besides gravity, and it is doing two jobs at once.

Two directions, two different statements
VerticalHorizontal
ΣF⃗y = 0 ΣF⃗x = max
Fly = Fg Flx = mac

The aeroplane is not rising or falling, so the vertical equation is an equilibrium statement. It is accelerating horizontally — that is what turning means — so the horizontal equation is Newton's second law with ac on the right.

Never draw it on the free-body diagram If you add an arrow labelled “Fc” next to the lift, the tension or the normal force, you have counted the same force twice. Fc is the name of the sum, not another arrow. Draw the real forces, resolve them, and whatever is left pointing at the centre is mac.

Unit check

Fc = mac = m v2r  →  kg (m/s)2m = kg m2m·s2 = kg ms2 = N

It had to come out in newtons, and checking that it does is the cheapest possible test of the algebra above.

Horizontal circles — a worked case

Trevor's example, and the cleanest one in the set because exactly one force acts in the centre-seeking direction: a ball on a string, swung on a frictionless level table around a nail the string can slide freely on.

Solving it
FT = m v2r = 1.00 kg (10.0 m/s)20.75 m = 1000.75 kg·m2m·s2 = 133 N

133 N on a 1 kg ball — about the weight of a 13.5 kg mass. Circular motion at ordinary speeds on a short radius demands large forces, which is why the string matters and why the question is always whether the real force can supply it.

Comparing centripetal accelerations

Because v is squared and r is not, the two variables do not have equal say. Trevor's four cases are drawn to scale, and the comparison is the point of the slide.

Review

Uniform circular motion— moving at constant speed in a circular path. The velocity is still changing.
Centripetal acceleration— ac = v2/r, directed at the centre. Proportional to the square of the speed and inversely proportional to the radius.
Centripetal force— Fc = mac = mv2/r — the net force in the centre-seeking direction. It can be caused by one force or by several. It is not an extra force acting on the object.
Also worth holding— in angular terms, ac = ω2r (Ch10a).
