Module 5 Reference Pages

Web versions of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

How to use these These are reference pages, not the module notes. They are here for when you are stuck on one idea and want it set out from the beginning, in one place, with the derivation written out and the figures redrawn. Nothing in the module requires you to read them, and nothing in them is assessed that is not also in the notes — so treat them as the thing you reach for when something has not landed, rather than as another reading list.
Where they came from Each page is a web version of one or two of Trevor’s lecture decks, transcribed faithfully, with every figure redrawn as a scalable diagram and the chapter numbering matched to College Physics. His attribution and licence travel with each page.

Chapter 6 (College Physics)

  • 06a
    Centripetal Force

    Go round a bend at a steady 30 mph and your speedometer says nothing is happening.

  • 06b
    Vertical Circles, Hills and Dips

    Turn the circle on its side and gravity stops being a bystander. At the top of a loop it helps pull you round; at the bottom it fights the thing that is pulling you round.

  • 06c
    Centripetal versus Centrifugal

    Your phone slides across the dashboard on a left-hand turn and it is obvious that something threw it.

  • 06d
    The Conical Pendulum

    Hang a ball on a string and swing it so it sweeps out a horizontal circle instead of a back-and-forth arc.

  • 13a
    Universal Gravitation and Weight Elsewhere

    The force that drops an apple is the force that holds the Moon — that is what universal means, and it was a genuinely radical claim.

  • 13b
    Orbits, Weightlessness and Artificial Gravity

    Astronauts float, so there must be no gravity up there. It sounds like a deduction and it is a myth — Ch13a already showed that at Space Station altitude gravity is still about 88% of what it is on the ground.

Chapter 9 (College Physics)

  • 10c
    Torque

    A force makes something accelerate; a torque makes something turn.

  • 12a
    Equilibrium of Forces and Torques

    An object is in equilibrium when two things are true at once: the net force on it is zero, and the net torque on it is zero.

  • 12b
    Centre of Mass and Gravitational Torque

    Every free-body diagram you have drawn since Module 3 has quietly shrunk an object to a point.

  • 12c
    Stability and Tipping

    Being in equilibrium says nothing about staying there. A cone standing on its base and a cone balanced on its point are both in equilibrium; nudge each one and they behave in opposite ways.

Chapter 10 (College Physics)

  • 10a
    Angular Variables

    A spinning wheel is awkward to describe with x, v and a, because every point on it has a different speed and none of them goes anywhere.

  • 10b
    Moment of Inertia

    Forces make things accelerate, and mass is what resists. Torques make things spin faster, and the thing that resists is not mass — it is mass and where that mass sits.

  • 10d
    Newton's Second Law for Torques

    F = ma has a rotational twin, and it is the equation the whole chapter has been building towards: τ = Iα.

  • 11a
    Rotational Energy and Rolling Motion

    A spinning object carries kinetic energy even when its centre is going nowhere, and the expression for it is the rotational twin of the one you already use: Krot = ½Iω2.

  • 11b
    Angular Momentum and Its Conservation

    Linear momentum has a rotational twin too: L = Iω. When no outside torque acts on a system, the total L before equals the total L after — which is why a skater who pulls her arms in spins faster without pushing on anything.
