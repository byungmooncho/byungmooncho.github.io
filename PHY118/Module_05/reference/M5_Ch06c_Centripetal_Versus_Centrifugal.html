Centripetal versus Centrifugal

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Your phone slides across the dashboard on a left-hand turn and it is obvious that something threw it. Nothing did. The phone went straight while the car turned under it. Trevor's closing slide is the one that settles the argument, and it is more interesting than “centrifugal force is fake”: which word you need depends on where you are sitting. From outside the turn there is a centripetal force and nothing else. From inside it, something has to explain why you are pressed against the door — and that something is the centrifugal force.
You already know this Newton's first law from Module 3 — an object keeps moving in a straight line unless a force acts — and from Ch06a the rule that the centripetal force is the net force in the centre-seeking direction, assembled from real forces you can name.

The phone on the dashboard

Trevor's opening is one everybody has lived through. You go around a left-hand turn and your phone slides across the dash to the passenger side.

The only horizontal force actually acting on the phone is friction from the dashboard, and it points towards the centre of the turn, not away from it. If that friction were large enough the phone would turn with the car. It is not, so the phone slides.

So Trevor's answer to his own question — is there actually a force involved? — is no: the phone is trying to keep moving in a straight line, which is inertia and not a force at all. His sharper sentence is the one worth memorising: the centrifugal force is really only noticed because of the lack of sufficient inward force.

Not on the free-body diagram Every force on a free-body diagram should have an agent — a named object doing the pushing or pulling. Road, string, seat, Earth. Try to name the agent of the outward force on the phone and there is nobody there. That is the test, and it is why the arrow never goes on a diagram drawn from outside the turn.

It depends on your point of view

This is the deck's closing slide, and it is the part that stops the argument being a trick question. Both words are used by working physicists; they belong to different observers.

A stationary observer outsideAn observer riding inside
Sees the object moving in a circle, and sees a real inward force — friction, tension, the normal force — causing that circular motion. Feels pressed outwards against the door, the wall or the seat, and needs something to blame for it.
Uses the centripetal force. Newton's laws work as written, with no extra terms. Uses the centrifugal force to explain being held against the outside wall, or why objects slide to the outside of a turn.

The reason the two accounts differ is that the rider's frame is turning, and Newton's laws were written for frames that are not. Keeping the books in a rotating frame costs you an extra outward term — the price of insisting that you are the one standing still.

Which one do you write down? For every problem in this module, the roadside view: real forces only, summed towards the centre, set equal to mv2/r. The centrifugal force is for describing what a passenger feels, not for solving.

Artificial gravity

The inside view is not merely a figure of speech. If the rotating frame is large enough and steady enough, what a person inside it feels is indistinguishable from weight — and that is the whole design principle behind a rotating spacecraft.

Sizing one
FN = m v2r = mω2r   →   for one g:   ω = √g/r

Writing it with ω rather than v (from Ch10a) is what makes it a design equation: the engineer chooses the radius and reads off the spin rate. Bigger ring, slower spin — which matters, because a small fast ring produces noticeably different “gravity” at your head and at your feet.

Trevor illustrates this with two NASA space-settlement paintings from the 1970s Ames studies — a torus station with its ring of mirrors seen from outside, and a view from inside a cylinder where the landscape curves up and over the viewer's head. They are not reproduced here, but they are worth looking at: NASA Ames 1970s space-settlement art (IDs AC75-1085 and AC75-1086).

Review

Point of view— which of the two words you need depends on where the observer is. Trevor's own closing line, and the honest answer.
From outside— a real inward force — friction, tension, the normal force — causes the circular motion. This is the centripetal force, and it is the only one in the equations.
From inside— the centrifugal force explains being held against the outer wall, or objects sliding to the outside of a turn. It appears because the rider's frame is rotating.
The sliding phone— inertia, not a force. It is noticed only because the inward force available — friction — was not enough.
Artificial gravity— a rotating station whose outer wall supplies FN = mv2/r. The occupants read that as weight.
