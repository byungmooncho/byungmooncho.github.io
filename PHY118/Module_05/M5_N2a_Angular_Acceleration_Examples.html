Angular Acceleration: Examples

SynopsisA practice sheet, not new material.  These are the two angular-acceleration problems from the middle of Note 2, pulled out and worked longhand so there is a clean copy of each to follow.

Both are rotational kinematics with α ≠ 0, which means the three equations from Note 1's table — the ones that sat there looking like the linear ones — are finally the equations you need.  The only real work is at the two ends: converting rev/min into rad/s at the start, and remembering at the finish which of at = rα and ac = rω² the question actually asked for.  They are different accelerations, at right angles to each other, and on the ultracentrifuge they differ by a factor of a million and a half.
Before you start — predict

A spinning rotor is speeding up.  A point on its rim therefore has two accelerations at once.  Write one sentence naming them and saying which way each one points.

Performance expectations

Convert rev/min to rad/s.  Apply the rotational kinematics equations when the angular acceleration is not zero.  Distinguish tangential from centripetal acceleration for a point on a rotating body, and compute each.  Express a centripetal acceleration as a multiple of g.

Two accelerations, at right angles

Before either problem, be clear which acceleration is which.  A point on a rotating body that is speeding up has both — one along the direction of travel, one straight at the axis — and they are completely different sizes.

The same point, the same instant, two accelerations.  at says the speed is changing; ac says the direction is.  Reading one question's answer off the other formula is the single most common way to lose this problem.
  1. The angular velocity of a rotating rigid body increases from 450 to 1400 rev/min in 135 s.  (A) What is the angular acceleration of the body, in rad/s²?  (B) Through what angle, in rad, does it turn in this 135 s?
  2. An ultracentrifuge accelerates from rest to 100 000 rpm in 2.50 min.  (A) What is the average angular acceleration, in rad/s²?  (B) What is the tangential acceleration, in m/s², of a point 4.50 cm from the axis of rotation?  (C) What is the centripetal acceleration of that point at full rpm, in m/s² and in multiples of g?  (D) What total distance, in metres, does that point travel?
i) First the conversions:  ω0 = 450(2π)/60 = 47.1 rad/s and ω = 1400(2π)/60 = 146.6 rad/s.  (A) α = Δω/Δt = (146.6 − 47.1)/135 = 0.737 rad/s².  (B) θ = ω0t + ½αt² = 47.1(135) + ½(0.737)(135)² = 6359 + 6718 = 1.31 × 104 rad, which is 13 077/2π = 2081 revolutions.— it does not start from rest, so the ω0t term is half the answer; dropping it gives 6718 rad and looks plausible
ii) ω = 100 000(2π)/60 = 1.047 × 104 rad/s, reached over 2.50 min = 150 s.  (A) α = 10 472/150 = 69.8 rad/s².  (B) at = rα = (4.50 × 10−2)(69.8) = 3.14 m/s².  (C) ac = rω² = (4.50 × 10−2)(10 472)² = 4.93 × 106 m/s²; divide by 9.80 and that is 5.04 × 105 g.  (D) θ = ½αt² = ½(69.8)(150)² = 7.85 × 105 rad, and the distance is d = rθ = (0.0450)(785 250) = 3.53 × 104 m — 35 km, from a point that never leaves a 4.5 cm circle.— check (C) against (B): they are the same point at the same instant and they differ by a factor of 1.6 million, because one carries α and the other carries ω²

Back to your prediction

The two are the tangential acceleration, which points along the direction of travel and exists only because the rotor is speeding up, and the centripetal acceleration, which points straight at the axis and is there whenever the thing is turning at all.  They are at right angles, so neither one adds to or cancels the other — and in question 2 they come out as 3.14 m/s² and 4.93 × 106 m/s².  If you named only one, it was almost certainly the centripetal one.
