Circular Motion Kinematics

SynopsisI summarize the rotational kinematics equations.  Note that rotational kinematics is just like linear kinematics.  Instead of linear displacement, you have an angular displacement since the motion has circular symmetry, so the distance from the center of rotation is fixed, and only the angle subtended by the motion changes while rotating.  There are equations for angular velocity and angular acceleration that look just like the equations for velocity and acceleration in linear kinematics.

The angular displacement gives the arc length or the actual distance covered, and the angular velocity gives the tangential velocity for a given radius of circular trajectory (see below).  Angular displacement is θ, and the arc length is s = rθ, provided θ is in radians.  That proviso is why we bother with radians at all: in degrees the same statement needs a conversion factor bolted onto it, and you would carry that factor through every line of every derivation that follows.  A full circle is 2π radians, and “one revolution,” “one rotation” and “one cycle” all mean the same trip.  Angular velocity is ω = Δθ/Δt in rad/s, and the tangential velocity it gives you at radius r is vt = rω.  Notice that ω has no r in it and vt does: every point on a rigid turning object has the same angular velocity, and a different speed.

We close out with equations for centripetal acceleration, which connect the force required to keep a body in circular motion to its angular velocity.   The velocity of an object in circular motion is perpendicular to the radius always — that is, tangential to the circle.   The net force on it is parallel to the radius and points toward the center of the circle, and center-pointing is what the word centripetal means.  Put those two together and v⃗ and F⃗ are perpendicular to each other.  Since a⃗c = Δv⃗/Δt = (v⃗f − v⃗i)/Δt, and the change of a vector that is only turning points inward, the acceleration points at the center too: ac = vt²/r = rω², and Fc = mac = mv²/r = mrω².

Everything in this Note is uniform circular motion only.  When |v⃗t| is constant and r is constant, ω is constant, so ac is constant, so Fc is constant, and the trajectory is a uniform circle of radius r traced at a fixed spin rate.

Angular displacement, and the radian

Fix a centre and a radius and the only thing left to say about a particle on that circle is its angle.  Two things to keep straight: the arc is the distance actually travelled, so s = rθ is a real distance in metres; and that formula is true in radians and false in degrees.  Degrees to radians is × 2π/360, radians to degrees is × 360/2π, and π rad = 180° is the one worth keeping in your head.

The same object at two instants.  r is fixed; only θ changes.  The thick arc is what it travelled, the blue arrows are its velocity and the red arrows the net force on it.
  1. Express the following angles in radians: 10°, 50°, 75°, 260°.
  2. The following angles are in units of radians.  Express them in degrees: π/3, 0.55π, 1.8π, 4π.
  3. Express those same four angles in units of revolutions.
  4. A sector of radius 2.0 m subtends 75°.  Find the arc length twice — once as d = 2πr(θ°/360), once as s = rθ — and say why they agree.
i) 10° = 0.175 rad, 50° = 0.873 rad, 75° = 1.309 rad, 260° = 4.538 rad — each one × 2π/360
ii) π/3 = 60°, 0.55π = 99°, 1.8π = 324°, 4π = 720° — each one × 360/2π, i.e. × 180/π
iii) π/3 = 1/6 rev, 0.55π = 0.275 rev, 1.8π = 0.9 rev, 4π = 2 rev — divide the radian value by 2π; 4π being exactly 2 revolutions is the easy check
iv) d = 2π(2.0)(75/360) = 2.6 m and s = (2.0)(1.309) = 2.6 m.  They agree because 2π(θ°/360) is θ in radians — the radian has the conversion built in, which is the whole reason the equations use it.

Angular velocity, and the tangential speed it gives you

ω = Δθ/Δt, in rad/s, and never in rev/min once you are inside an equation.  Every rotating machine in every problem is quoted in rev/min, so the first line of the working is almost always the same one: multiply by 2π/60.  Then vt = rω turns that spin rate into a speed — a different speed at every radius, from the same ω.

Two riders on one spoke of the same merry-go-round, at 0.10 m and 0.50 m.  Radii and velocity arrows are both drawn to scale.
  1. Microwave ovens rotate at a rate of about 7 rev/min.  (A) What is this in revolutions per second?  (B) What is the angular velocity in radians per second?
  2. A merry-go-round makes 2.5 rotations in 20 s.  (A) What is its angular velocity?  (B) What is the speed of a rider sitting 0.50 m from the centre?  (C) And of a rider sitting 0.10 m from the centre?
  3. In lacrosse, a ball is thrown from a net on the end of a stick by rotating the stick and forearm about the elbow.  If the angular velocity of the ball about the elbow joint is 30.0 rad/s and the ball is 1.2 m from the elbow joint, what is the velocity of the ball?
  4. From your answers to 6(B) and 6(C): which of ω and vt depends on where you sit, and which does not?  One sentence.
i) (A) 7 ÷ 60 = 0.117 rev/s.  (B) ω = 2π(7/60) = 0.733 rad/s — round to 0.116 first and you get 0.729 instead: carry the fraction, round at the end
ii) (A) ω = 2.5(2π)/20 = 0.785 rad/s — no radius appears anywhere in that line.  (B) vt = rω = (0.50)(0.785) = 0.39 m/s.  (C) (0.10)(0.785) = 0.079 m/s — check (B) the long way: n(2πr)/t = 2.5(2π)(0.50)/20 = 0.39 m/s
iii) v = rω = (1.2)(30.0) = 36 m/s — which is the whole reason a lacrosse stick is long
iv) Speed depends on radius; spin rate does not.  Every point on a rigid turning object sweeps the same angle in the same time, so ω is the same wherever you are — but vt = rω, so five times the radius is five times the speed.

Why turning at constant speed is accelerating

Acceleration is change of velocity, and velocity is a vector.  Go round a circle at a perfectly steady speed and the size never changes, but the direction changes continuously, so Δv⃗ is not zero.  Subtract the two velocity vectors and look at where the difference points.

Both velocity arrows are the same length — the speed never changed — and their difference is not zero.

A rigid wheel of radius r rotates about an axis through the centre and perpendicular to the plane of the wheel.  Point A is on the rim; points B and C are on two different spokes, each at a distance r/2 from the centre.  Questions 13–15 are about one interval Δt of that rotation.

One interval Δt of the rotation, seen from along the axis.
  1. Using the Δv⃗ construction above, say in one or two sentences why the object is accelerating and which way the acceleration points.
  2. Which of the following is true regarding the angular velocity?  (A) All three points have the same angular velocity, as all three have the same angular displacement θ in the same time interval Δt.  (B) B and C have half the angular velocity of A, as they are at half the distance from the centre.  (C) B and C have twice the angular velocity of A, as they are at half the distance from the centre.
  3. Which of the following is true regarding the tangential speed?  (A) All three have the same tangential speed, as all three have the same angular displacement θ in the same time interval Δt.  (B) A has a greater tangential speed as it is further from the centre.  (C) B and C have a greater tangential speed as they are closer to the centre.
  4. Which of the following is true regarding the centripetal acceleration?  (A) All three have the same centripetal acceleration, as all three have the same angular displacement θ in the same time interval Δt.  (B) B and C have half the centripetal acceleration of A, as they are at half the distance from the centre.  (C) B and C have twice the centripetal acceleration of A, as they are at half the distance from the centre.
i) Redrawn from a common tail, v⃗f − v⃗i points sideways, back toward the centre, even though the two arrows are the same length.  As Δt shrinks, Δv⃗ swings until it is aimed exactly at the centre, so a⃗ = Δv⃗/Δt points at the centre with magnitude v²/r = rω².
ii) (A).  One rigid object: every point sweeps the same angle in the same time, so ω is the same everywhere on it.  ω has no r in it.
iii) (B).  v = rω with the same ω, so A at radius r moves twice as fast as B and C at r/2.
iv) (B).  ac = rω² with the same ω, so half the radius is half the centripetal acceleration.  Note that you get the wrong answer if you reach for v²/r carelessly — halving r also halves v, and (v/2)²/(r/2) = v²/2r, which is the same half.  Both forms agree, as they must.

Centripetal force

Fc = mac = mv²/r = mrω², and the second form is the one to reach for when the problem gives you rev/min.  One warning about the word: centripetal is a job description, not a kind of force.  You never draw an arrow labelled “centripetal force” on a free-body diagram.  You draw the forces that are really there — tension, friction, gravity, a normal force — and then notice that their sum points at the centre and equals mv²/r.  Whichever force is doing that job is the centripetal force in that problem.

  1. An ordinary workshop grindstone has a radius of 8.50 cm and rotates at 7500 rev/min.  (A) Calculate the magnitude of the centripetal acceleration at its edge in metres per second squared, and convert it to multiples of g.  (B) What is the linear speed in m/s of a point on its edge?
  2. A 23.0 kg child is riding a playground merry-go-round that is rotating at 40.0 rev/min.  What centripetal force (in N) must she exert to stay on if she is 2.00 m from its centre?
  3. What centripetal force (in N) does she need to stay on the same merry-go-round, at the same rotation rate, if she is 6.00 m from its centre?  Compare each force with her weight, and say what that means for someone trying to hold on at the rail.
i) (A) r = 8.50 × 10−2 m and ω = 2π(7500)/60 = 250π = 785 rad/s, so ac = rω² = (8.50 × 10−2)(785)² = 5.24 × 104 m/s², and ÷ 9.80 = 5350 g.  (B) v = rω = (8.50 × 10−2)(785) = 66.8 m/s — both conversions first; every error in this problem lives in that step. 5350 g is why a grindstone carries a maximum rated speed and why a chipped one is thrown away
ii) ω = 2π(40.0)/60 = 4.19 rad/s; ac = (2.00)(4.19)² = 35.1 m/s²; Fc = (23.0)(35.1) = 807 N
iii) ac = (6.00)(4.19)² = 105 m/s²; Fc = 2421 N — exactly three times, because Fc = mrω² is linear in r and the radius tripled.  Her weight is (23.0)(9.80) = 225 N, so the two are 3.58× and 10.7× her own body weight.  Nobody grips ten times their body weight with their hands, which is why nobody stays on the outside of a fast merry-go-round.
