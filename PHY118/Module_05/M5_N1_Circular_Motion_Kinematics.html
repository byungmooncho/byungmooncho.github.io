Circular Motion Kinematics

SynopsisI summarize the rotational kinematics equations.  Note that rotational kinematics is just like linear kinematics.  Instead of linear displacement, you have an angular displacement since the motion has circular symmetry, so the distance from the center of rotation is fixed, and only the angle subtended by the motion changes while rotating.  There are equations for angular velocity and angular acceleration that look just like the equations for velocity and acceleration in linear kinematics.

The angular displacement gives the arc length or the actual distance covered, and the angular velocity gives the tangential velocity for a given radius of circular trajectory (see below).  Angular displacement is θ, and the arc length is s = rθ, provided θ is in radians.  That proviso is why we bother with radians at all: in degrees the same statement needs a conversion factor bolted onto it, and you would carry that factor through every line of every derivation that follows.  A full circle is 2π radians, and “one revolution,” “one rotation” and “one cycle” all mean the same trip.  Angular velocity is ω = Δθ/Δt in rad/s, and the tangential velocity it gives you at radius r is vt = rω.  Notice that ω has no r in it and vt does: every point on a rigid turning object has the same angular velocity, and a different speed.

That is as far as this Note goes.  Note 2 picks up the other half of the story — centripetal acceleration and the force that supplies it — and the later notes turn α back on.

Angular displacement, and the radian

Fix a centre and a radius and the only thing left to say about a particle on that circle is its angle.  Two things to keep straight: the arc is the distance actually travelled, so s = rθ is a real distance in metres; and that formula is true in radians and false in degrees.  Degrees to radians is × 2π/360, radians to degrees is × 360/2π, and π rad = 180° is the one worth keeping in your head.

The same object at two instants.  r is fixed; only θ changes.  The thick arc is what it travelled, the blue arrows are its velocity and the red arrows the net force on it.
  1. Express the following angles in radians: 10°, 50°, 75°, 260°.
  2. The following angles are in units of radians.  Express them in degrees: π/3, 0.55π, 1.8π, 4π.
  3. Express those same four angles in units of revolutions.
  4. A sector of radius 2.0 m subtends 75°.  Find the arc length twice — once as d = 2πr(θ°/360), once as s = rθ — and say why they agree.
i) 10° = 0.175 rad, 50° = 0.873 rad, 75° = 1.309 rad, 260° = 4.538 rad — each one × 2π/360
ii) π/3 = 60°, 0.55π = 99°, 1.8π = 324°, 4π = 720° — each one × 360/2π, i.e. × 180/π
iii) π/3 = 1/6 rev, 0.55π = 0.275 rev, 1.8π = 0.9 rev, 4π = 2 rev — divide the radian value by 2π; 4π being exactly 2 revolutions is the easy check
iv) d = 2π(2.0)(75/360) = 2.6 m and s = (2.0)(1.309) = 2.6 m.  They agree because 2π(θ°/360) is θ in radians — the radian has the conversion built in, which is the whole reason the equations use it.

Angular velocity, and the tangential speed it gives you

ω = Δθ/Δt, in rad/s, and never in rev/min once you are inside an equation.  Every rotating machine in every problem is quoted in rev/min, so the first line of the working is almost always the same one: multiply by 2π/60.  Then vt = rω turns that spin rate into a speed — a different speed at every radius, from the same ω.

Two riders on one spoke of the same merry-go-round, at 0.10 m and 0.50 m.  Radii and velocity arrows are both drawn to scale.
  1. Microwave ovens rotate at a rate of about 7 rev/min.  (A) What is this in revolutions per second?  (B) What is the angular velocity in radians per second?
  2. A merry-go-round makes 2.5 rotations in 20 s.  (A) What is its angular velocity?  (B) What is the speed of a rider sitting 0.50 m from the centre?  (C) And of a rider sitting 0.10 m from the centre?
  3. In lacrosse, a ball is thrown from a net on the end of a stick by rotating the stick and forearm about the elbow.  If the angular velocity of the ball about the elbow joint is 30.0 rad/s and the ball is 1.2 m from the elbow joint, what is the velocity of the ball?
  4. From your answers to 6(B) and 6(C): which of ω and vt depends on where you sit, and which does not?  One sentence.
i) (A) 7 ÷ 60 = 0.117 rev/s.  (B) ω = 2π(7/60) = 0.733 rad/s — round to 0.116 first and you get 0.729 instead: carry the fraction, round at the end
ii) (A) ω = 2.5(2π)/20 = 0.785 rad/s — no radius appears anywhere in that line.  (B) vt = rω = (0.50)(0.785) = 0.39 m/s.  (C) (0.10)(0.785) = 0.079 m/s — check (B) the long way: n(2πr)/t = 2.5(2π)(0.50)/20 = 0.39 m/s
iii) v = rω = (1.2)(30.0) = 36 m/s — which is the whole reason a lacrosse stick is long
iv) Speed depends on radius; spin rate does not.  Every point on a rigid turning object sweeps the same angle in the same time, so ω is the same wherever you are — but vt = rω, so five times the radius is five times the speed.

Looking ahead

You now have the variables and the kinematics: an angle in radians, a spin rate that belongs to the whole object, and a table of equations you already knew in different letters.  Note 2 asks the question this Note deliberately left alone — if the velocity is turning, there is an acceleration and therefore a force, so how big are they and which way do they point?  That is centripetal acceleration and centripetal force, and it is where the last two rows of the table above get used.  Note 1a is the in-class assignment that bridges the two.

Back to your prediction

Same rate, different speed.  Both children sweep the same angle in the same time, so ω is identical — it is one rigid object.  But vt = rω, so the one at the rail is moving several times faster and needs several times the force to stay put.  If you wrote “the same” for both halves you were answering about ω and forgot r; if you wrote “different” for both, you were answering about v and forgot that the merry-go-round is one rigid object.
