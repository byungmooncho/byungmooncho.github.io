Buoyancy: Weighing Water With a Piece of Metal

Synopsis Hang a metal cylinder from a spring scale and read its weight. Now lower it into water, without letting it touch the bottom, and read the scale again. It reads less. Nothing has been removed from the cylinder, so the missing weight has to be a force — the buoyant force — pushing up on it. Archimedes' principle says that force equals the weight of the water the cylinder pushed out of the way, and this lab turns that statement into a measurement. The elegant part is what happens when you put the two halves together algebraically: the volume of displaced water appears on both sides and cancels, leaving the density of water expressed in terms of nothing but the density of the metal and the two scale readings. You never need to measure the water at all.

The physics you are using

In air, and then in water

Hanging in air, the cylinder is in equilibrium under two forces, so the scale reads its weight:

Tair = Fg = Mmetal g

Submerged, a third force appears. The cylinder is still in equilibrium, so now

FB + Twater = Fg   →   FB = Fg − Twater = Tair − Twater

The buoyant force is the drop in the scale reading. Nothing else is needed to measure it.

The cancellation

Archimedes' principle says that same force is the weight of the displaced water:

FB = ρwater Vdisplaced g

Set the two expressions for FB equal, and write Tair = Mmetalg:

ρwater Vdisp g = Tair − Twater = Tair( 1 − Twater/Tair ) = Mmetal g ( 1 − Twater/Tair )

The cylinder is fully submerged, so the water it pushed aside has exactly the cylinder's own volume: Vdisp = Vmetal. And Mmetal = ρmetalVmetal. Substitute, and both the volume and g cancel from every term:

ρwater = ρmetal ( 1 − Twater / Tair )
Look at what that equation does not contain. No volume. No g. No unit conversion between newtons and grams. The density of water comes out of the density of the metal and a ratio of two scale readings — and because it is a ratio, any constant calibration error in the scale cancels out of it too. This is the result the lab is built around, and deriving it yourself is worth more marks than any arithmetic below.

Predict first. You repeat the whole experiment with a cylinder of the same metal but twice the volume. What happens to the value of ρwater you calculate?

Why that question matters for how you write this up. Both scale readings double, so their ratio is untouched, and ρmetal is a property of the material rather than of the lump. The predicted density of water is therefore identical. That is a strong statement: it says the experiment does not depend on getting the size of the cylinder right, only on the two scale readings and on knowing what the metal is. Keep that in mind when you reach the Discussion, because the number you actually compute will come out about 9% low — and if the method is insensitive to volume, the volume had better not be where you lay the blame without checking.

The apparatus

A metal cylinder from the lab kit hangs by a short length of string from a spring scale. Below it sits a graduated cylinder about half filled with water. The scale is read with the cylinder hanging freely in air, and again with it fully submerged and clear of the bottom and sides. The water level is read before and after each dip.

Notice the precision mismatch before you start calculating. The balance gave the mass to a tenth of a gram — four significant figures. The graduated cylinder was read to the nearest half millilitre on a rise of under seven millilitres, which is two significant figures at best. Those two numbers are about to be divided by one another, and the weaker one governs the result. Carrying 70.5/6.83 to five digits does not make the answer more precise; it only hides which measurement is in charge.

Part II — The buoyant force and the metal

  1. The displaced volume. Subtract the average before-reading from the average after-reading. 1 mL = 1 cm³.
  2. The density of the metal. ρmetal = Mmetal / Vdisplaced, since a fully submerged solid displaces its own volume.
  3. Identify the metal. Compare your density against a table of common metals and name the best match. This is not decoration — it is the only external check in the whole experiment, and Part III turns on it.
  4. The buoyant force. FB = Tair − Twater, in newtons.

Part III — The density of water, three ways

You now have enough to get ρwater by more than one route, and they will not agree. Working out why is the point of this lab.

Route A — straight from Archimedes

ρwater = FB / ( Vdisplaced · g )

Watch the units: FB in newtons and V in cubic metres gives kg/m³; divide by 1000 for g/cm³.

Route B — the cancellation, using your own metal density

ρwater = ρmetal ( 1 − Twater/Tair )

Route C — the cancellation, using the published density of the metal you identified

Same formula as Route B, but with ρmetal taken from a reference table for the metal you named in Part II rather than from your own displacement measurement.

Routes A and B are not independent, and that is the trap. Both of them contain your displaced volume — A uses it directly, B hides it inside ρmetal = M/V. So if the volume reading is off, both answers are off, and their rough agreement with each other proves nothing at all. Route C is the only one that does not touch your volume measurement. If C lands close to 1.000 g/cm³ while A and B do not, you have located the faulty measurement without needing anyone to tell you. Your Discussion must address this explicitly; saying "the two methods agreed" when they share an input is a misreading of your own data.

What counts as agreement

The accepted density of water is 1.000 g/cm³, or 1000 kg/m³. Report a percent error for each route. Within 5% is a good result for this apparatus; outside 10%, name the measurement responsible rather than calling it experimental error.

Additional Questions

Choose three of the five below and answer those three fully. Name which three you chose. Answer them in a section of their own headed Additional Questions, in sentences and paragraphs with the working shown, never as a bare numbered list and never by restating the question before answering it. Depth on three beats coverage of five; this section is worth 8 points on the rubric.

  1. AQ1 — find the broken measurement. Route C uses the published density of the metal and avoids your volume entirely. Compute all three routes and compare. Then work backwards: assume Route C is correct and ρwater is exactly 1.000 g/cm³, and solve for what the displaced volume should have been. Compare that against the 6.83 cm³ you measured, express the difference in millilitres, and say whether it is a plausible reading error on a graduated cylinder. Finish by stating which single measurement you would improve first if you ran this again, and how.
  2. AQ2 — the derivation, done properly. Derive ρwater = ρmetal(1 − Twater/Tair) from first principles, stating every assumption as you use it — including the one that is easy to miss, that the cylinder is fully submerged so that Vdisplaced = Vmetal. Then explore what happens when that assumption fails: derive the corresponding expression for a partially submerged object, and explain why the volume no longer cancels. What would you have to measure in that case that you did not have to measure here?
  3. AQ3 — propagating the ratio. The scale readings are good to about ±0.005 N. Propagate that uncertainty through the ratio Twater/Tair and then into ρwater by Route C. (The fractional uncertainty of a quotient is the sum in quadrature of the fractional uncertainties; be careful, because the quantity you need is 1 − the ratio, and its uncertainty is not the same as the ratio's.) Quote Route C's answer with its uncertainty. Then do the same for Route A, propagating ±0.5 mL on the volume. Which route is intrinsically more precise, and by how much?
  4. AQ4 — a cylinder that floats. Suppose you repeated the experiment with a block of wood of density 0.6 g/cm³, held fully under water by the string. Predict both scale readings qualitatively — and then notice that one of them cannot be what you expected. Work out what the spring scale would actually read, explain the sign of the result physically, and say what it would mean about the direction of the string's force. Does the formula ρwater = ρmetal(1 − Twater/Tair) still hold for this case, and if so what does its output look like?
  5. AQ5 — why a boat floats and a bar of lead does not. Your cylinder lost about 9% of its apparent weight in water. Using your own measured metal density, compute the fraction of its weight any solid object of that density would lose when fully submerged, and show that this fraction is just ρwater/ρmetal. Use that to state the condition for floating in a single line. Then resolve the apparent paradox: a steel ship and a steel bar are made of the same material, and one floats. Quantify your answer — estimate the average density a hull must have, and say what fraction of a ship's volume is therefore not steel.

Prompts for your Discussion

These are not the Additional Questions. Work them into your Discussion section as part of your argument, in your own order. Do not answer them as a list, and do not limit yourself to them.

  1. You computed the density of water by three routes. Which do you report as the result, and why? A report that gives three numbers without choosing has not finished the analysis.
  2. Routes A and B share your displaced-volume measurement. What would it have taken for them to be genuinely independent checks on each other?
  3. Your measured metal density differs from the published value for the metal you identified. Is that difference large enough to call the identification into question, or does it point at the measurement instead?
  4. The three trials disagree with one another, particularly in the air reading. Compute the spread and say whether it is large enough to explain your discrepancy from 1.000 g/cm³.
  5. Some water clings to the cylinder when it is lifted out. Which measurement does that affect, in which direction, and is it big enough to matter here?
  6. If the cylinder had touched the bottom of the graduated cylinder during a dip, what would the scale have read and which way would your final answer have moved?
