Pressure and Pascal's Principle

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Pressure is what a force becomes once you stop caring where on the surface it lands. Spread the same force over more area and the pressure falls; that one ratio explains snowshoes, drawing pins and hydraulic jacks. Inside a fluid, pressure is not something applied from outside — it is the weight of everything above you, which is why it grows with depth and why it is the same at every point on a given level.
You already know this Density from the previous page; force, weight and the condition for equilibrium (ΣF⃗ = 0) from Module 3; and W = Fd cos θ from Module 4, which the hydraulic section leans on directly.

Pressure is force per unit area

Definition and units
P = FA      F⃗ = P A⃗      1 pascal = 1 newtonmetre² = 1 Nm²
101.3 kPa = 1 atm

The second form is the one you actually use in this module: a pressure acting on an area is a force, and that is how every free-body diagram in the next three sections gets built.

Don't confuse P and ρ Trevor flags this on the slide, and it is worth taking seriously because both appear in the same equation three sections from now. P is pressure, an upright capital P, in pascals. ρ is density, a Greek rho, in kg/m³. Hand-written they look almost the same; keep the rho's tail short and the P's bowl closed.

Different types of scientists have different favourite units. Meteorologists use millibars and inches of mercury, physiologists use mm Hg, engineers use psi and bar. Note that the bar is not an atmosphere — it is 100 kPa against 101.3, close enough to be confusing and far enough to matter.

Gauge pressure and absolute pressure

We live at the bottom of an ocean of fluid we call the atmosphere. It pushes on us from all sides, and because it is always there we usually ignore it — which means most instruments are built to ignore it too.

Two readings of the same pressure
Gauge, Pg— pressure measured relative to atmospheric pressure. What a tyre gauge, a blood-pressure cuff or a dive computer reports.
Absolute, Pabs— pressure measured relative to vacuum, i.e. relative to zero pressure.
Pabs = Pg + Patm

Be careful which one you are reporting. A sealed container at Pg = 0 is not empty — it holds an atmosphere. A container at Pabs = 0 is a vacuum, and no gauge can read below it.

Notation warning carried from Trevor's slide The OpenStax textbook writes this gauge pressure as plain P, not Pg. So the book's P = hρg and Trevor's Pg = ρhg are the same statement. When a problem gives you “the pressure” at a depth, read which one it means before adding an atmosphere to it.

Pressure in a liquid comes from the weight above

This is the derivation the rest of the module rests on, and Trevor does it the honest way — not by quoting a formula, but by drawing a free body and applying ΣF⃗ = 0 to a column of the liquid itself.

From ΣF = 0 to P = Patm + ρhg
0 = ΣF⃗ = F⃗a + F⃗g + F⃗p
0 = −Fa − mg + Fp  →  Fp = Fa + mg
PA = PatmA + mg    with  ρ = mV = mAh  so  m = ρAh
PA = PatmA + ρAhg
P = Patm + ρhg     i.e.  Pabs = Patm + Pg

Compare the last two lines. The term ρhg is the gauge pressure — the part a gauge reports, over and above the atmosphere. The derivation does not assume that; it produces it.

Watch the area while you read the diagram Trevor's handwritten note on this slide: the weight changes as we change the area of interest. Draw a fatter column and both Fatm and Fg grow in proportion, which is exactly why A divides out and the result is independent of how you chose to slice the liquid.

Worked example — how deep before the pressure is 2 atm?

Determine how deep one must go to experience a pressure of two atmospheres
PA = P0A + mg = P0A + ρVg    (V = Ah)
PA = P0A + ρAhg  →  P = P0 + ρhg    (h = d)
P = P0 + ρdg

Trevor's slide stops here, with the relationship rather than a number. Finishing it: two atmospheres means the gauge part must equal one atmosphere, so

ρdg = Patm  →  d = Patmρg = 101 300 Pa(1000 kg/m³)(9.80 m/s²) = 10.3 m

About ten metres of water doubles the absolute pressure — and every further ten metres adds another atmosphere. That is the number divers carry in their heads, and it is why a barometer made of water would have to be eleven metres tall while a mercury one, at 13.6 times the density, is 760 mm.

Every point at the same depth is at the same pressure

Nothing in the derivation mentioned the shape of the container, only the height of the column above the point. So two points joined by the same body of fluid, at the same level, are at the same pressure however differently shaped the fluid above them is.

Pabs = Patm + Pg      P = P0 + Pg      P = P0 + ρdg

Three ways of writing one statement, all of which appear in the homework. The last is the one to use when you are given a depth.

Pascal's principle

Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to all parts of the fluid and its container.

The word doing the work is undiminished. Push on one part of a sealed fluid and the extra pressure appears everywhere at once, at full strength — so a pressure raised at a small piston is available at a large one.

The hydraulic relation
P1 = P2   →   F1A1 = F2A2

Trevor's handwritten gloss: Pascal's principle is the origin of modern hydraulic systems. Car brakes, diggers, lifts and presses are all this one equation with a bigger second piston.

A caveat he also writes down: there are effects due to depth — the two pistons are at different heights, so strictly P2 = P1 + ρgΔh. As long as the height difference is small compared with the pressures involved, it is ignored.

What a hydraulic system cannot do

A jack multiplies force. It does not multiply energy, and conservation of energy says exactly what it costs.

Work in equals work out
W = F d cos θ,  θ = 0°    and    F = PA
Win = Wout  →  F1h1 = F2h2
P1A1h1 = P2A2h2    but  P1 = P2
A1h1 = A2h2

Read it as a trade. Multiply the force by ten and you divide the distance by ten; the product — the work — is untouched. A jack that lifts a car one centimetre may need thirty pumps to do it, and that is not a defect of the jack.

Review

Pressure— P = F/A, in pascals. A scalar: the force and the area carry the direction.
Units— many of them. 1 atm = 101.3 kPa = 760 mm Hg = 14.7 psi.
With depth— P = P0 + ρgd. Depth only — not shape, not width, not how much fluid there is.
Gauge vs absolute— Pabs = Pg + Patm. OpenStax writes the gauge part as plain P.
Same depth— all points at one level in a connected fluid are at one pressure.
Pascal— applied pressure is transmitted undiminished: F1/A1 = F2/A2.
The price— A1h1 = A2h2. Force is multiplied; work is not.
