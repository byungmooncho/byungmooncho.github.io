Flow Rate and Bernoulli's Equation

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Your textbook puts this material in a chapter of its own, and for good reason: everything so far has been about fluid standing still. Set it moving and two bookkeeping rules carry the whole chapter. Mass has nowhere to go, so the same volume per second passes every cross-section — narrow the pipe and the fluid must speed up. Energy has nowhere to go either, so the pressure, the speed and the height trade against each other in a fixed sum. That sum is Bernoulli's equation, and it is conservation of energy with the terms divided by volume.
You already know this Density and pressure from the previous pages; W = Fd cos θ, the work–energy theorem W = ΔK, and gravitational potential energy Ug = mgh from Module 4. The whole derivation below is those three, applied to a slug of fluid.

Flow rate

The trick Trevor uses throughout this deck is to stop thinking about a fluid and start thinking about a piece of one: pick out a volume, follow it, and treat it like any other object from Modules 3 and 4.

Two forms of the same definition
Q = VΔt    — the volume passing a given spot in a given time
with  V = AΔx  and  v = Δx/Δt  →  Δt = Δx/v
Q = AΔxΔx/v = AΔx vΔx  →  Q = Av     units: m³/s

Two different questions, one quantity. V/Δt is what you measure with a bucket and a stopwatch; Av is what you compute from the pipe. They are the same number, and moving between them is most of the problem-solving in this section.

A caveat Trevor marks on the slide The flow can change near the walls of the tube. Real fluid is slower at the edge than in the middle, so v in Q = Av is an average over the cross-section. Treating it as uniform is the idealisation this whole chapter runs on; viscosity is where it is repaid.

The continuity equation

Assume the density does not change — true for liquids, and a decent approximation for slow-moving gases. Then a fixed mass of fluid keeps a fixed volume, even though it is free to change shape. If the volume cannot change, neither can the flow rate.

Continuity
Q1 = Q2   →   v1A1 = v2A2

The flow rate is constant; the speed is not. Those two sentences sound contradictory until you notice that one is about volume per second and the other is about metres per second.

Trevor's hose Filling a bucket takes the same amount of time whether you hold your thumb over the end of the hose or not. The thumb raises the speed and lowers the area in exactly compensating measure, so Q is unchanged. What you gain is reach, not water. Worth saying out loud because the jet looks like more water.

Work and energy on a moving slug

To move through the pipe, the fluid has to be pushed, and whatever pushes it does work. The pusher is the fluid behind it, through its pressure.

Work done by pressure
W = F d cos θ   with  θ = 0°,  F = PA,  d = Δx
W = PAΔx  →  W = PV

This is the quiet step that makes Bernoulli work: pressure times volume is an energy. Check the units — (N/m²)(m³) = N·m = J. It is why pressure can appear in the same equation as a kinetic energy and a potential energy.

W = ΔK = ½mvf² − ½mvi²    with  m = ρV  →  K = ½ρVv²

Writing the mass as ρV is what lets the volume be divided out at the end, leaving an equation purely in pressures. Pressure changes the speed when the fluid moves to a region of different area.

Trevor notes that Δx does not have to be the length of the volume under investigation — it is just convenient to use that length.

Bernoulli's equation

Now add height. The slug does not only speed up, it can also climb, so gravitational potential energy joins the ledger. The result is the whole chapter in one line.

Energy bookkeeping for the slug
Ug = mgh = ρVgh      K = ½mv² = ½ρVv²
W = WP + Wg = ΔK
P1V − P2V − (ρVgh2 − ρVgh1) = ½ρVv2² − ½ρVv1²

Every term carries a factor of V. Divide it out, gather the subscript 1 terms on the left and the subscript 2 terms on the right, and the equation stops being about one slug and becomes a statement about the pipe.

P1 + ½ρv1² + ρgh1 = P2 + ½ρv2² + ρgh2
P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant    Bernoulli's equation

Every term is an energy per unit volume, which is why they can be added and why each of them has units of pressure. Bernoulli's equation is not a new law — it is conservation of energy, divided through by V.

Two special cases worth naming

Almost every problem you will meet kills one of the three terms, and the two that matter have names of their own.

“Static” fluidsFluids at constant depth
Conditionv = 0 m/sΔh = 0 m
What is left P1 + ρgh1 = P2 + ρgh2 P + ½ρv² = constant
Usually written ΔP = ρgΔh, or P = ρgd for pressure at a depth P1 + ½ρv1² = P2 + ½ρv2²
Calledthe depth result from the pressure pageBernoulli's principle
The sentence to carry away Where the fluid is faster, the pressure is lower. Trevor writes it the other way round on the slide — decreasing the pressure increases the speed — and both readings are correct, because the equation does not say which causes which. It says only that at constant height the two trade off. Note also that the left column recovers the result from the previous chapter. Setting v = 0 in Bernoulli's equation gives back P = P0 + ρgd. The static case was never a separate piece of physics.

Where the Bernoulli effect shows up

Trevor's last content slide collects four devices that all work the same way: run a fluid fast past an opening, drop the pressure there, and a second fluid is drawn in — entrained — through that opening.

Gas burner— natural gas jets through a venturi and pulls air in alongside it, so the gas arrives at the flame pre-mixed.
Perfume atomiser— squeezing the bulb drives air across the top of a tube standing in the liquid; the lowered pressure lifts perfume up the tube into the airstream.
Aspirator— water running down a narrow throat entrains air from a side port, giving a cheap vacuum pump with no moving parts.
Water-heater chimney— hot gas rising up the flue draws cool room air in at the base, which keeps the exhaust moving and the burner supplied.

Trevor's slide shows each of these as a cut-away diagram, together with a streamline plot over an aerofoil. Those are third-party OpenStax illustrations and are not reproduced here.

The aeroplane-wing caveat, in Trevor's own words Bernoulli's equation contributes to the lift on an airplane wing, but the majority of the force is due to the deflection of air. This is worth keeping, because the “longer path over the top” story is both extremely common and wrong. A wing works mainly by throwing air downwards; by Newton's third law the air throws the wing up. Bernoulli describes the pressure field that accompanies that deflection — it is a real part of the picture, not the whole of it.

Review

Flow rate— Q = V/Δt = Av, in m³/s. The volume passing a spot per second.
Continuity— v1A1 = v2A2. The flow rate is constant; the speed is not.
W = PV— pressure times volume is an energy. This is what lets pressure sit in an energy equation.
Bernoulli's equation— P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant. Conservation of energy per unit volume.
Bernoulli's principle— the constant-height case. Faster fluid, lower pressure.
