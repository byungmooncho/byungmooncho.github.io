Fluid Dynamics and Bernoulli's Principle

SynopsisNote 1 was fluid standing still.  Everything there came from one idea — the fluid above you has weight.  Now we set the fluid moving, and two new ideas take over, both of them conservation laws you already know, rewritten for something that flows.

Mass is conserved, so whatever goes into a pipe has to come out of it.  Squeeze the pipe and the fluid has no choice but to speed up — that is the continuity principle.

Energy is conserved, so a fluid that speeds up has to pay for the extra kinetic energy out of something else.  What it pays with is pressure.  That is Bernoulli's equation, and it is the work–energy theorem with the terms divided through by volume.

Put the two together and you get the result that sounds wrong the first time you hear it: where a fluid moves faster, its pressure is lower.  It is why a wing lifts, why a shower curtain gets sucked inward, and why the narrow part of a pipe is the part at risk of collapsing.
Before you read on — predict

A pipe carrying water narrows to half its width and then opens out again.  In the narrow section, is the water pressure higher than in the wide section, lower, or the same?  Commit to one in your guided notes, with a reason, before you read on.

Continuity — the fluid has to go somewhere

Water goes into one end of a pipe and comes out of the other.  None of it is created on the way through and none of it disappears, so in any given second, the amount that enters has to equal the amount that leaves.  That is mass conservation, and for a fluid of constant density it is volume conservation too.

Now watch what that forces on a pipe that changes width.

Drawn as a duct of fixed depth, so the height on the page is the area.  The wide section has 2.25× the area of the narrow one, and every other ratio in the picture is the same 2.25: the narrow slug is 2.25× as long, and the narrow arrow is 2.25× as long.  The two shaded slugs therefore have equal volume, which is the whole content of the continuity principle.

Writing that down.  In a time Δt, the slug in the wide section travels Δxw and the slug in the narrow section travels Δxn.  The volumes have to match:

AwΔxw = AnΔxnsame volume in, same volume out
divide by Δt:  Aw(Δxw/Δt) = An(Δxn/Δt)
Awvw = Anvnbecause Δx/Δt is the speed

That product Av is the volume flow rate, Q, in m³/s — litres per second, by another name.  Continuity says Q is the same everywhere along the pipe:  Qw = Qn.

Read it as a see-saw.  A and v multiply to a fixed number, so if Aw > An then vw < vn.  Narrow means fast.  You already knew this with your thumb over the end of a hose.

Fast flow is low-pressure flow

Here is where intuition goes wrong.  Squeezing a pipe sounds like it should raise the pressure inside the squeeze — you are cramming the same water through a smaller hole.  The opposite happens.  The narrow section is the low-pressure section.

The reason is energy.  The fluid arrives at the throat moving slowly and leaves it moving fast, so its kinetic energy went up.  Nothing pushed it but the fluid behind, so that energy had to come from somewhere, and the only account left to draw on is pressure.  The fluid spends pressure to buy speed.

Standpipes read the pressure in the duct below them: the taller the column, the higher the pressure.  The column over the throat is the short one.  Note also that the pressure recovers on the far side — the fluid gets its pressure back when it slows down again, which is what tells you nothing was lost, only traded.

Bernoulli's equation — energy conservation for a fluid

Now make that bookkeeping exact.  Take the work–energy theorem you used for blocks and carts in Module 4, apply it to a lump of fluid, and divide every term by the lump's volume.  Nothing new goes in; what comes out is Bernoulli's equation.

The derivation, in four lines.  The change in kinetic energy equals the work done on the lump.  Gravity does work, and so does the surrounding fluid, which pushes the lump along with a force PA over a distance — that is a work of P × volume:

½mvf² − ½mvi² = −[ (PEf − PEi) + (Pf − Pi)V ]ΔPV is the work done by the fluid
divide every term by V:  ½(m/V)vf² − ½(m/V)vi² = −[ mghf/V − mghi/V + ΔP ]
and m/V is ρ:  ½ρvf² − ½ρvi² = −[ ρghf − ρghi + ΔP ]every term is now an energy per unit volume
½ρvi² + ρghi + Pi = ½ρvf² + ρghf + Pfcollect the i's on one side and the f's on the other

Three terms, and each one is an energy per unit volume — which is why they can be added, and why they all come out in pascals:

½ρv² — the kinetic term  ·  ρgh — the potential term  ·  P — the pressure term

And notice the middle term: ρgh is exactly the quantity that ran all of Note 1.  Fluid statics is just this equation with the flow switched off, vi = vf = 0.

The pipe is horizontal, so hi = hf and the ρgh terms cancel on both sides:

½ρvw² + Pw = ½ρvn² + Pn

Note how modest the drop is — 6 kPa out of 150.  The v² term only becomes dramatic when the speeds get large, which is why this effect is spectacular for aircraft and subtle for plumbing.

When the pipe also rises

So far the pipe has been flat and the ρgh terms have cancelled.  Let them not cancel.  Here the pipe narrows and climbs at the same time, so the fluid gains kinetic energy and gains potential energy — and both bills land on the same account.

The narrow upper section is drawn at half the area of the lower one, so continuity puts the fluid there at twice the speed — and the upper arrow is drawn twice as long.  The dashed line is the datum the height h is measured from; only the difference in height matters, so you may put it wherever is convenient.

The same equation, grouped the way Dr. Cho writes it.  Take Bernoulli and put the kinetic terms on the left and everything else on the right:

½ρvf² − ½ρvi² = −[ (ρghf − ρghi) + (Pf − Pi) ]
ΔKE = −[ ΔPE + ΔP ]all three per unit volume

Now read the signs.  The fluid speeds up, so ΔKE is positive.  It climbs, so ΔPE is positive too.  For the bracket to come out negative overall, ΔP has to be negative and big enough to outweigh the climb:

ΔP must drop, and drop a lotit is paying for the speed and the height

Compare it with the flat pipe.  With h = 0 the pressure only had to cover the speed-up.  Here it covers the climb as well, so Pf at the top is much lower than it would be at the same speed on the flat.  This is the reason the top floor of a building has weaker water pressure than the ground floor, and the reason a pump has to work to fill a roof tank.

In your guided notes: the rising-pipe sketch with h and the datum marked, the line ΔKE = −[ΔPE + ΔP], and the sign of each of the three terms for this picture.

The wing

A wing is the famous case, and it is worth getting right rather than getting slick.

The air passing over the upper surface of a wing moves faster than the air passing underneath.  By Bernoulli, faster air is at lower pressure.  So the pressure underneath exceeds the pressure above, and that pressure difference, acting over the whole area of the wing, is lift.

The streamlines over the top are drawn crowded and the ones underneath spread out — crowded streamlines are fast-moving fluid, which is the same continuity idea as the narrow pipe.  The red arrow at the trailing edge is the other half of the story, and the caution below is about it.
Why is the air over the top moving faster?Your answer
“Because it has further to go and has to meet the air from underneath at the trailing edge.”  Is that right?

No.  That story is extremely common and it is wrong, so it is worth knowing why.  Nothing requires two parcels of air that separated at the leading edge to arrive at the trailing edge together — and measurements show they do not.  The air over the top arrives sooner, not just faster.  A wing that was symmetric top and bottom, or flying upside down, would also generate no lift on that account, and both of those fly perfectly well.

What is true: the wing's shape and its angle to the oncoming air deflect the air downward.  By Newton's third law, pushing air down pushes the wing up — that is the red arrow in the figure.  The same deflection is what sets up the faster flow over the upper surface, and Bernoulli then tells you that faster flow is at lower pressure.  So the pressure difference is real and Bernoulli's part of the argument is right; the equal-transit-time story is simply the wrong explanation for where the speed difference comes from.

Two descriptions, one wing: air pushed down (Newton) and low pressure on top (Bernoulli) are the same lift counted two ways, not two competing causes.

In your guided notes: the wing with the pressures marked, one line for the Bernoulli account and one for the Newton account, and a note to yourself that “equal transit time” is not the reason.

Back to your prediction

The pressure in the narrow section is lower.  Continuity forces the water to speed up there, and the extra kinetic energy is paid for out of pressure.  If you predicted “higher” you are in good company — the squeeze feels like it should pressurise the water — but the standpipe over the throat is the short one, and the pressure comes back up again once the pipe widens and the water slows.
