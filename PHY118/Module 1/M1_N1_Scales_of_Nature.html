Scales of Nature

Synopsis We explore objects in the universe from the unimaginably small to the unimaginably large — from quarks to the observable universe. Because the range of sizes (and masses) spanned is so vast, we describe them in terms of orders of magnitude: roughly, the power of 10 (the number of zeros) in the measurement. This lets us compare wildly different scales, and figure out how many of one thing it would take to build up another.
Performance Expectations By the end of this note, you will be able to…
  • PE1 Describe the order-of-magnitude size (in meters) of objects ranging from subatomic particles to the observable universe.
  • PE2 Describe the order-of-magnitude mass (in kilograms) of objects ranging from an electron to the entire universe.
  • PE3 Compute the ratio of scale between two quantities that differ by many powers of ten (e.g., "how many times bigger/smaller").
  • PE4 Determine how many of a smaller object would span or make up a larger one, using order-of-magnitude reasoning (e.g., how many atoms span a human, how many stars make up a galaxy).
  • PE5 Recognize why powers-of-ten notation is essential for comparing quantities across vastly different scales.
quark → atom → you → planet → galaxy → universe
💡You already know this

You already reason in orders of magnitude, even if you've never called it that:

🐜 An ant vs. a skyscraper You don't need to measure either one to know they're separated by many, many powers of ten.
🗺️ Zooming out on a map app Street → city → state → planet — each zoom level is roughly another power of ten.
🏖️ A grain of sand vs. a beach You instinctively know there's some astronomically large number of grains, even without counting.

In this note, we answer four questions

  1. What is an order of magnitude?
  2. How do sizes compare across the smallest and largest things in the universe?
  3. How many small things does it take to build up something big?
  4. Why do scientists use powers of ten instead of writing out all the zeros?

From everyday words to orders of magnitude

Half the battle is translating an everyday word for "small" or "big" into an actual power of ten:

You'd say…What's happeningRough scale
"Microscopic"Too small to see unaided10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁴ m
"Human-scale"You can hold it or see it directly10⁻² to 10¹ m
"City-sized"Measured in kilometers10³ to 10⁴ m
"Astronomical"Solar-system scale and beyond10¹¹ m and up
"Cosmic"Galaxies and beyond10²¹ m and up

Key relationships for this note

These aren't full equations yet (that starts in Note 2) — they're the reasoning patterns behind every question above.

  • 🔍 ratio = 10(a − b) compare two sizes/masses whose orders of magnitude are a and b
  • 📏 N = 10(big − small) how many of the smaller thing fit end-to-end across the bigger thing
  • 🌌 total = N × (one part) e.g., galaxy mass = (number of stars) × (mass per star) — solve for N
