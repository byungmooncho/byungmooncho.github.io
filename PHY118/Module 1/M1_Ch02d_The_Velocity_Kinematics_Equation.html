The Velocity Kinematics Equation

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea One worked example, done carefully. A runner accelerates out of the blocks and then holds a steady speed. The velocity equation v = v0 + aΔt answers “how fast at 2.0 s?” and “how fast at the end?” — but only after you list what you know, sketch the motion, and notice that the race has two segments with different accelerations.
Performance Expectations By the end of this lesson, you will be able to…
  • PE1Write down the kinematics equations.

  • PE3Determine the units in each term of the kinematics equations.

Example problem

At the starting gun, a runner accelerates at 1.9 m/s² for 5.2 s. The runner's acceleration is zero for the rest of the race.

What is the speed of the runner at (a) 2.0 s, and (b) at the end of the race?

Step 1 — List what you know

During the acceleration
a= 1.9 m/s²
t= 5.2 s
v0= 0 m/s (starts from rest)
t0= 0 s
After 5.2 s
a= 0 m/s²
Asked
v= ? m/s at t = 2.0 s, and at the end
Why the sketch matters The graph shows two different motions glued together. Part (a) happens during the acceleration, so a = 1.9 m/s² applies. Part (b) asks about the end of the race — long after the acceleration has stopped. Reading the graph tells you the speed at the end is the same as the speed at 5.2 s.

Part (a): speed at t = 2.0 s

Try each step yourself before revealing it.

v = v0 + a t

We know v0, a, and the time; we want v. This equation has exactly those four variables and nothing else.

v = 0 ms + 1.9 ms² (2.0 s − 0.0 s)

Write the units with every number. Notice the time interval is written out as t − t0, even though t0 is zero.

v = 3.8 ms² · s = 3.8 ms

(m/s²)·s: one power of seconds cancels, leaving m/s — the unit a speed must have. If it hadn't, something was wrong.

v = 3.8 m/s at t = 2.0 s

Two significant digits, matching the data (1.9 and 2.0).

Part (b): speed at the end of the race

Use the full acceleration time, t = 5.2 s.

The runner speeds up only until 5.2 s. After that, a = 0, so the velocity stops changing. Whatever speed the runner has at 5.2 s is the speed for the rest of the race.

v = 0 ms + 1.9 ms² (5.2 s − 0.0 s)
v = 9.88 ms² · s = 9.88 ms

Keep the extra digit while you work; round at the end.

v = 9.9 m/s at the end

This is the velocity at t = 5.2 s — but since a = 0 for the rest of the race, it is also the velocity at the end.

All together

(a) At t = 2.0 s
v = v0 + a t
v = 0 m/s + (1.9 m/s²)(2.0 s)
v = 3.8 (m/s²)·s
v = 3.8 m/s
(b) At the end (t ≥ 5.2 s)
v = v0 + a t
v = 0 m/s + (1.9 m/s²)(5.2 s)
v = 9.88 (m/s²)·s
v = 9.9 m/s

Concept question A student answers part (b) by plugging the whole race time, say 12 s, into v = v0 + a t with a = 1.9 m/s². What went wrong?

Looking ahead: multi-segment motion — accelerate, then coast, then brake — comes up constantly. The habit to build now: sketch the velocity graph, split it where the acceleration changes, and apply the equations to one straight segment at a time.

Review

  • Start every problem by listing the knowns with units, then sketch the motion — here a velocity–time graph with two segments.
  • v = v0 + a t applies only while a is constant. Use it segment by segment: 1.9 m/s² for the first 5.2 s, then a = 0 (speed unchanged) afterward.
  • Carry units through every line — (m/s²)·s = m/s confirms you computed a speed — and round to the data's significant digits at the end: 3.8 m/s at 2.0 s, 9.9 m/s at the end of the race.
