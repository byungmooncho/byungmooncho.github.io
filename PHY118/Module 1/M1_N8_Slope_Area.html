Slope and Area Under the Curve of Motion Graphs

Synopsis You've already seen that the slope of a distance–time graph is speed, and the slope of a velocity–time graph is acceleration. Now flip the direction: the area under the curve of an acceleration–time graph is the change in velocity, and the area under the curve of a velocity–time graph is the change in distance. Slope and area are two sides of the same coin, connecting distance, velocity, and acceleration graphs in both directions.
Performance Expectations By the end of this note, you will be able to…
  • PE1Calculate velocity change from the area under the curve of an acceleration–time graph.
  • PE2Calculate distance change from the area under the curve of a velocity–time graph.
  • PE3Recognize the shape of each area: a rectangle (a × t) under a constant acceleration–time graph, and a triangle (½ × t × vf) under a velocity–time graph starting from rest.
  • PE4Connect slope and area as inverse operations across the distance → velocity → acceleration graph chain.
distance⇄velocity⇄acceleration slope goes right, area goes left
💡You already know this

You already compute totals as "rate × time" — that's exactly what area under a rate graph means:

🚰 Filling a bucket at a steady rate Total water = (liters per minute) × minutes — the area under a flat rate graph.
💰 Getting paid by the hour Total pay = wage × hours worked — same rectangle-area idea.
🏎️ A car accelerating from a stoplight The distance gained as it speeds up builds like a triangle under the velocity–time graph.

Instructions (from your guided notes)

Your handout shows real free-fall data: an acceleration–time graph (flat at ~9.8 m/s², same for any falling object), a velocity–time graph with a line of best fit through the data points, and a distance–time graph of the same measurement. Because it's measured data, the best-fit slope won't land exactly on 9.8 m/s² — that small difference is the %error from Note 2a. Work through the area calculations on your actual graphs; the calculator above lets you check the theoretical values first.

The math behind each area:

  • Area under acceleration–time = a × t = Δv (rearranged from vf = vi + at)
  • Area under velocity–time (triangle, from rest) = ½ × t × vf = Δd

Looking ahead: notice the distance–time graph in accelerated motion is curved, so you can't (yet) take its slope directly to double-check your velocity — that requires calculus, and Note 7's secant/tangent approach is the workaround. For now, trust that slope and area are consistent: a distance–time graph's slope should match the velocity you found from the area under acceleration–time.
