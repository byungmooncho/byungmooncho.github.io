Velocities from Distance–Time Graphs: Average and Instantaneous Velocity

Synopsis Most real motion isn't constant speed forever — things speed up, slow down, and change. In free fall, velocity changes continuously with time (v = vi + at), so "velocity" at one instant is different from the next. This note teaches two ways to pull velocity out of a curved distance–time graph: the secant line (connecting two points) gives average velocity, and the tangent line (touching one point) gives instantaneous velocity.
Performance Expectations By the end of this note, you will be able to…
  • PE1Define average velocity as the slope of a secant line connecting two points on a distance–time graph.
  • PE2Define instantaneous velocity as the slope of a tangent line at a single point on a distance–time graph.
  • PE3Extract average and instantaneous velocity from a curved distance–time graph using secant and tangent lines, respectively.
  • PE4Recognize the numerical relationship: for constant acceleration starting from rest, average velocity over an interval equals half the instantaneous velocity at the end of that interval.
secant → average tangent → instantaneous same curve, two different lines
💡You already know this

You already distinguish "overall" from "right now" without thinking about it:

🚗 A road trip's average speed Total distance ÷ total time — versus what your speedometer reads at this exact moment.
📈 A stock chart's overall trend The line from open to close (secant) vs. how steeply it's moving right now (tangent).
🏃 Your race pace vs. your finish-line sprint Your average pace for the whole race is very different from your speed in the final stride.
