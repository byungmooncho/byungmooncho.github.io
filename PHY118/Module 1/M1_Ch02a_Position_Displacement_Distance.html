Position, Displacement, Distance

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Everyday English uses position, distance, and displacement almost interchangeably. Physics doesn't. Position is where something is; distance is how much ground it covered; displacement is the change in position — final minus initial. The Greek letter Δ (delta) is the shorthand for “change in,” and it shows up in every kinematics equation from here on.

Position, distance, displacement

Scientists attempt to use words with precise meanings. This doesn't always work, but we try.

Position≡Coordinate where an object is located
Distance≡Total length of travel
Displacement≡Change in position
Final position − Initial position
Displacement, written with a delta
Δx = xf − xi
Δ ≡ “change in”   — the symbol is the Greek capital letter delta
xi ≡ initial position     xf ≡ final position

Subscripts are useful for labeling variables: the letter tells you what kind of quantity (a position, x), and the subscript tells you which one (initial or final).

Disappearing deltas

You will see the same idea written several ways, depending on the book:

Four ways to write the same two ideas
Δx = xf − xi
Δx = x − xo  (“x-naught” is the starting position; plain x is the final one)
Δt = t − to
Δt = t
Warning Make sure you know what the variable means. The letter might mean different things in different parts of the book — and different books use different symbols.
Warning Textbooks almost always “reset the clock” for different segments of the motion. Thus to = 0 s, and Δt = t − 0 = t. That is why the delta on t so often “disappears.”

Keep it simple — start out with one dimension

An object starts at position −3 m and ends at position +2 m.

Displacement in one dimension
Δx = (+2 m) − (−3 m) = +5 m

The sign carries the direction: positive means the object ended up farther along the +x direction than it started. Watch the double negative — subtracting a negative position adds.

Two dimensions

Consider these two ways to get from point A to point B.

Both trips have exactly the same displacement — they start at A and end at B. But the dashed trip covers far more ground, so its distance is much larger. Displacement only cares about the endpoints; distance cares about the whole route.

Snapshots have limits A set of positions at whole seconds tells you a lot, but not everything. Between snapshots the object could speed up, slow down, or turn around. That is why physics will soon want instantaneous quantities, not just before-and-after ones.

Looking ahead: average velocity, average acceleration

Displacement is the first of a family of “change in” quantities. Divide a displacement by the time it took and you get average velocity; divide a change in velocity by the time it took and you get average acceleration.

Average velocity and average acceleration
vave = ΔxΔt = xf − xitf − ti     e.g.   x2 − x1t2 − t1   or   x3 − x2t3 − t2
aave = ΔvΔt = vb − vatb − ta

Position, velocity, and acceleration have different units (m, m/s, m/s²). Comparing their “actual size” is nearly meaningless — a displacement arrow and a velocity arrow drawn on the same picture can't be compared by length.

Looking ahead: the Δ notation you met here is the whole language of Notes 4–8. Every slope on a motion graph is a “change in something” divided by a “change in time.”

Review

  • Position and displacement are related, but not quite the same. Position is a coordinate; displacement is the change in that coordinate, Δx = xf − xi.
  • The distance traveled is always greater than or equal to the size of the displacement. They are equal only when the object never turns around.
  • Δ means “change in”: final minus initial. Know what each letter and subscript stands for in your textbook, and expect the clock to be reset so that Δt = t.
