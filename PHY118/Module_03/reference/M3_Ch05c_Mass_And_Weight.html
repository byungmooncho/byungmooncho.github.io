Mass and Weight

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea In everyday speech mass and weight are the same word. In physics they are not even the same kind of thing: mass is a property of the object and travels with it; weight is a force the object happens to be experiencing where it is standing. Take the object to the Moon and its mass is unchanged while its weight drops by a factor of six. The bridge between them is the second law, Fg = mg.

Units of force

Where the newton comes from
ΣF⃗ = m a⃗   →   [F] = [M] [L][T]2   →   [F] = [kg] [m][s]2
1 N = 1 kg m/s2

The newton is not a fundamental unit. It is a shorthand for kg m/s2, which is why unit-checking a force answer always ends by reassembling those three. N is the symbol for the newton.

Weight vs. mass

Mass— depends on the amount of matter present. A property of the object.
Weight— depends on the amount of matter present × the strength of the gravitational field. A force, and therefore a property of the object and its situation.
The bridge between them
F⃗g = m a⃗g    i.e.    Fg = mg

This is just ΣF⃗ = m a⃗ with gravity as the only force. Nothing new is being asserted — which is exactly why weight changes when g does, and mass does not.

Know mass, find weight

Fg = mg = (85 kg)(9.8 m/s2) = 833 kg m/s2 = 833 N

Know weight, find mass

m = Fgg = 700.0 N9.80 m/s2 = 71.429 kg m/s2m/s2 = 71.4 kg

Carrying the units through as kg m/s2 rather than as N is what makes the cancellation visible. Do that whenever an answer’s units are in doubt.

Converting between metric and imperial

A deliberately “bad” conversion 1 kg weighs about 2.2 pounds. Trevor flags this himself: kilograms measure mass and pounds measure weight, so the two sides of that statement are not the same kind of quantity. It works only because it silently assumes Earth’s surface gravity. Use it, but know that it stops being true the moment you leave Earth — which is the whole point of the next section. (For the record, the British unit of mass is the slug.)

Weights on different planets

To solve most of these problems you must first find the mass, because the mass is the constant. Everything else — the weight in newtons, the weight in pounds, the local ag — changes with where you are standing.

Example — the lunar rover

The lunar rover weighed 462 pounds back here on Earth. How much did it weigh, in pounds and in newtons, on the Moon? The acceleration due to gravity on the Moon is ⅓⁾ that of Earth.

m = 462 lb · 1.0 kg2.2 lb = 210 kg
Fgm = m agm = m · (1/6)ag = (210 kg)(1/6)(9.8 m/s2) = 343 kg m/s2 = 343 N
Fgm = (1/6)FgE = (1/6)(462 lb) = 77 lb
Corrected from the source deck Trevor’s slide gives the pounds answer as 72 lb. It should be 77 lb — 462 ÷ 6 = 77. The newton answer on the same slide is right, and it confirms the correction independently: 343 N ÷ 4.448 N/lb = 77.1 lb. The arithmetic slip is in the pounds line only.

Worth noticing Saturn and Neptune are enormously more massive than Earth, yet their surface ag is barely above 1 g, and Uranus’s is below it. Mass alone does not set the local gravity — the radius does too, and it enters squared. That is the next equation.

Where the local ag comes from

Newton’s law of universal gravitation, reduced to a local acceleration
FG = G m1m2r2 = mo G mpr2   and   FG = moag
  →   ag = G mpr2
SymbolMeaning
mpmass of the planet
momass of the object
rradius of the planet — more generally the distance between the objects. An object on the surface is one radius from the centre.
G6.67 × 10−11 N m2/kg2

The object’s own mass mo cancels out. That is why every object at the same place falls with the same ag — the result you have been using since Module 1 without proof.

Density ties the two planetary parameters together: ρ = m/V = m ÷ 43πr3. Reducing the mass at fixed radius lowers the density; increasing the radius at fixed mass lowers it too. Both moves lower ag, which is why the gas giants disappoint.
