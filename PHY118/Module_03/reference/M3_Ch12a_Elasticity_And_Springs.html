Elasticity and Springs

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Springs are another way to provide forces — but they differ from every push and pull so far in one respect: the force changes in a predictable manner. It is proportional to how far you have moved the spring from its natural length. That proportionality, Hooke’s law, is the same relationship that governs stretching a steel cable or compressing a concrete pillar, which is why one page carries both.

Two ways of looking at Hooke’s law

Measure the displacement, or measure the stretch — either works
Measure the displacement of the massMeasure the stretch of the spring
F = kΔx F = kΔL
F = k(x − 0) = kx  if you put the origin at the natural length —

Either method works, since ΔL = Δx — how far the block moved is how much the spring stretched. The second form F = kx is only available when you have chosen the origin at the unstretched position; that choice is not automatic, and forgetting you made it is the usual source of a wrong sign.

About the missing minus sign Trevor writes Hooke’s law without one, as F = kΔx, and his diagram has the spring’s force pointing back toward equilibrium while Δx points away. So the equation as written is a statement about magnitudes, which is all a statics problem needs. When you come to oscillations, where the direction is the whole point, you will want the vector form F = −kx — the minus sign is what makes the motion come back.

Be careful of independent vs. dependent axes

Plot the same spring data two different ways and you get two different slopes. Only one of them is k.

Force (N) on y, stretch (m) on x Stretch (m) on y, force (N) on x
slope = ΔF/ΔL slope = ΔL/ΔF
k = slope k = 1/slope
units N/m — correct for a spring constant units m/N — the reciprocal, sometimes called the compliance

Checking units will help you know whether the slope is k or not. That sentence is worth more than the table: it is a test you can apply to any graph, including ones nobody warned you about. The same trap, with the same fix, appears on the sibling page Ch05f · Verifying Newton’s Second Law, where the slope is 1/m rather than m.

Tension, compression and shear

Hooke’s law is not a fact about coiled metal. It is a fact about any solid pulled or pushed gently enough — and the “spring constant” of a rod turns out to be built from the material and the rod’s own dimensions.

From F = kΔL to stress and strain
F = kΔL   →   F ∝ ALoΔL   →   F = Y ALoΔL
FA = Y ΔLLo   i.e.   Stress = Y × Strain

Y is Young’s modulus. Dividing the force by the area and the stretch by the original length strips out the specimen’s size, leaving a number that belongs to the material — the same for a steel wire and a steel bridge. That is the whole reason for inventing stress and strain.

Tension—the external force pulls; the rod lengthens
Compression—the external force pushes; the rod shortens
Shear—the external force acts along the face rather than into it; the shape skews. Governed by S, the shear modulus, in the same form.
Why Newton’s third law is on this slide A rod only stretches if it is held at the far end. The external force on one end and the reaction at the support are equal in magnitude — that is what puts the material in tension at all. Trevor writes it as Fext = Freaction, which is true as magnitudes; as vectors they are of course opposite. Pull on a rod with nothing holding the other end and it accelerates instead of stretching.
