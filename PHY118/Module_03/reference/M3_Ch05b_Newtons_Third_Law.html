Newton’s Third Law — Action and Reaction

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea “Equal and opposite” is the most-quoted and least-understood sentence in mechanics. The trap is that plenty of force pairs are equal and opposite without being a third-law pair at all — a mug sitting on a table is the standard example. The test is not the arrows; it is which objects the arrows are attached to. Get that straight and the third law stops being a slogan and starts being the extra equation that finishes a two-body problem.
Performance Expectations By the end of this lesson, you will be able to…
  • PE1State Newton’s Third Law of Motion.
  • PE2Identify pairs of forces that are, and are not, third-law pairs.
  • PE3Use the third law as the extra relationship that closes a two-body problem.

The law

Newton’s Third Law

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

Whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the second exerts an equal and opposite force back on the first.

F⃗A on B = −F⃗B on A

The subscripts are the whole content of the law. Write every force as “what on what” and the pairs identify themselves.

Be careful of “equal and opposite”

Two forces that are equal and opposite might not be a third-law pair. A genuine N3 pair always has all three of these:

2 objects—exactly two bodies are involved
Same type—both members are the same kind of force — both gravitational, or both contact
Different objects—the two forces act on different bodies, never on the same one
The one-line test If both arrows land on the same free body diagram, they are not a third-law pair. An N3 pair can never appear together on one free body diagram.

The third law provides “extra” information

Check yourself

Two boxes are sitting side by side. You push on one box, which in turn pushes on the other box. If the boxes stay in contact…

  1. The boxes have the same acceleration.
  2. The bigger box has the larger acceleration.
  3. The smaller box has the larger acceleration.
  4. There is no relationship between the accelerations of the boxes.
Answer: 1. “Stay in contact” is the physical statement that the gap between them never changes — which is exactly the statement that their accelerations are equal. That sentence, not the third law, is what ties the two boxes together. The third law is what lets you write the force between them once and use it in both equations.

Worked example — pushing on two boxes

Two boxes sit on a frictionless surface. The larger box has a mass of 10.0 kg, the smaller has half that. You push on the larger box with a force of 20.0 N. What is the contact force between the boxes?

Treat the pair as one body first, then each box separately
Fp = (m10 + m5)a  →  a = Fpm10 + m5 = 20 N15 kg = 1.33 m/s2

Gravity and the normal force cancel, so only the horizontal forces matter.

Box 1 — 10 kg (the one you push)Box 2 — 5 kg
m10a = Fp − Fc m5a = +Fc
two forces act on it: your push forward, box 2 pushing back one horizontal force acts on it: box 1 pushing forward
— (5.0 kg)(1.33 m/s2) = 6.67 N

Box 2 is the easier one to solve, because only the contact force acts on it horizontally. That is the habit worth taking away: start from the body with fewer forces on it.

Now push from the other side

Same two boxes, same 20.0 N — but now you push the small box, which pushes the large one. The acceleration of the pair is unchanged at 1.33 m/s2, since the total mass is the same. But now the 10 kg box is the one with a single horizontal force on it:

m10a = Fc  →  Fc = (10 kg)(1.33 m/s2) = 13.3 N
The point of doing it twice The contact force is 6.67 N one way and 13.3 N the other, from the same 20 N push on the same two boxes. The third law is not what changed — at every instant, in both cases, box 1 pushes box 2 exactly as hard as box 2 pushes box 1. What changed is how much mass the contact force has to accelerate. Push the big box and the contact force only has to move 5 kg; push the small box and it has to move 10 kg.

It doesn’t always seem to apply — but it always does

A truck meets a car head-on. Everyone’s intuition says the truck hits the car harder. It does not. Newton’s third law is a given: you may simply use it.

Same force, different accelerations
F⃗t = −F⃗c   (third law)
mtat = Ft   and   Fc = mcac   (second law, each vehicle)
−mtat = mcac

The objects experience the same force, but the smaller object experiences the larger acceleration — and acceleration, not force, is what injures the occupants. The asymmetry everyone feels is real; it just lives in the mass, not in the force.
