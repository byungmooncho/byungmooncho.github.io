General Projectile Problems

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 118.

The idea Fire something at an angle and it has both a horizontal and a vertical component of initial velocity. Resolve the launch velocity into those two components, run the equations of motion on each one separately, and recombine at the end. The one new wrinkle is algebraic: the vertical equation is quadratic in time, so it hands you two values of t. Both of them are honest solutions of the equation. Only one of them is the flight time, and deciding which is part of the physics, not an afterthought.

You already know this Three things, all of them old. One: how to resolve a vector into perpendicular components with sine and cosine (03c–03e). Two: the equations of motion for constant acceleration, which you have been using since Module 1. Three: the quadratic formula from algebra. Nothing new is being invented on this page — the three are simply being used in the same problem for the first time.

What makes something a projectile

When an object is “thrown” with both a horizontal and a vertical component of initial velocity, we call it a projectile. In this course, every mathematical treatment of a projectile assumes that the only force acting on it is gravity, pulling straight down. Air resistance is neglected. That is an assumption, not a fact about the world, and it is the reason a real long-range shell does not quite follow the curve we are about to draw.

Because gravity pulls straight down, it changes only the vertical part of the motion. The horizontal part is left alone. That is the independence of motion you met in 04a: the two directions do not talk to each other except through one shared quantity — the clock.

Horizontal→ax = 0. The horizontal component of velocity never changes, so x grows at a steady rate.
Vertical→ay = −g = −9.80 m/s2 with up taken as positive. Free fall, exactly as in Module 1.
Shared≡Time. The same Δt appears in both columns, and that is what lets you solve one column and feed the answer into the other.

The full-blown treatment for projectiles

Given a launch speed and an angle — v⃗0 = (v0, θ) — the whole procedure is four steps long.

Procedure Every general projectile problem, every time
  • 1Resolve the initial velocity into components, making sure the angle is measured from the axis you think it is measured from.
  • 2Apply the equations of motion to each component of the motion separately.
  • 3Recombine the information to answer the question that was actually asked — final position, flight time, landing velocity, and so on.
  • 4Choose carefully between the two times the quadratic gives you. This step has no counterpart in 04a, and it is where most of the lost points on this topic live.
Steps 1–3 are Trevor's “full blown treatment” slide; step 4 is the line his Review slide adds once the quadratic has appeared.
Step 2 — the same three equations, run twice
Horizontal  (ax = 0)
x = x0 + v0x Δt + 12 ax Δt2   ·   vx = v0x + ax Δt   ·   vx2 = v0x2 + 2ax Δx
Vertical  (ay = −g)
y = y0 + v0y Δt + 12 ay Δt2   ·   vy = v0y + ay Δt   ·   vy2 = v0y2 + 2ay Δy

With ax = 0 the horizontal row collapses to x = x0 + v0x Δt and vx = v0x. The vertical row keeps its Δt2 term — and that term is the whole reason this page needs the quadratic formula.

Minding the angles v0x = v0 cos θ and v0y = v0 sin θ are true only when θ is measured from the horizontal. Measure the same launch direction from the vertical instead and the sine and cosine trade places. Draw the triangle before you reach for a button on the calculator.

Worked example: off the top of a cliff

A projectile is fired into the air from the top of a 200.0 m cliff above a flat valley. Its initial velocity is 40.0 m/s at 60.0° above the horizontal. Where does the projectile land?

Set the stage: origin, axes, signs

Nothing below makes sense until this paragraph is fixed and never changed again. Put the origin at the launch point, at the lip of the cliff. Take up as positive and right as positive. Then y0 = 0, the valley floor is at y = −200.0 m, x0 = 0, ax = 0, and ay = −9.80 m/s2.

Step 1 — resolve the initial velocity

Components of the initial velocity
v0x = v0 cos θ = (40.0 m/s) cos 60.0° = (40.0 m/s)(0.5000) = 20.0 m/s
v0y = v0 sin 60.0° = (40.0 m/s)(0.8660) = 34.64 m/s

Both components are positive: the projectile starts out moving up and to the right. Keep the extra digits — 34.641 m/s — in the calculator, and round only at the end.

Predict first A rock dropped from rest off this same cliff reaches the valley floor in 6.39 s. Our projectile leaves the same spot at 40.0 m/s, 60.0° above the horizontal. Its flight time will be…

Step 2 — work the vertical direction, and put it in standard form

The clock is shared, so a time found in one direction is usable in the other. The vertical direction is the one that knows about the cliff, so solve it first. Start from the position equation, substitute the sign convention, and set y equal to the height of the valley floor.

From the equation of motion to standard quadratic form
y = y0 + v0y Δt + 12 ay Δt2
y = 0 + v0y Δt + 12 (−g) Δt2 gravity points down, so ay = −g
−200.0 m = (34.64 m/s) Δt − 12 (9.80 m/s2) Δt2
0 = 12g Δt2 − v0y Δt + (y0 − y) standard form, in symbols
0 = (4.900 m/s2) Δt2 − (34.64 m/s) Δt − 200.0 m

Standard form means AΔt2 + BΔt + C = 0 with everything on one side and the Δt2 term written first. Here A = +4.900 m/s2, B = −34.64 m/s, and C = −200.0 m. Two of the three are negative, and getting those signs right is the whole job.

Where the −200 comes from The projectile does not land at y = 0; it lands 200.0 m below the origin, so y = −200.0 m. Students who write y = +200.0 m here get a negative discriminant and a calculator error, which is at least an honest warning. Students who drop the v0y Δt term instead get a plausible-looking wrong answer, which is worse.

Step 3 — apply the quadratic formula

Solving 0 = (4.900) Δt2 − (34.64) Δt − 200.0
Δt = −B ± √B2 − 4AC2A
Δt = −(−34.64) ± √(−34.64)2 − 4(4.900)(−200.0)2(4.900)
Δt = +34.64 ± √1200 + 39209.800 = +34.64 ± √51209.800 = +34.64 ± 71.559.800
Δt+ = +106.209.800 = +10.836 s  →  10.8 s   ·   Δt− = −36.919.800 = −3.7666 s  →  −3.77 s

Units are carried along and work out: m/s divided by m/s2 is seconds. The discriminant is +5120 m2/s2, comfortably positive, so both roots are real. A negative discriminant would have been physics telling you the projectile never reaches that height at all.

Step 4 — choose the time, then recombine

The launch happens at Δt = 0 and the clock runs forward from there, so the flight time is the positive root: Δt = 10.8 s. Feed it into the horizontal equation, where the component of velocity never changes.

Recombining — the question asked was where
x = x0 + v0x Δt + 12 ax Δt2
x = 0 + (20.0 m/s)(10.836 s) + 0 = 216.7 m  →  x ≈ 217 m

The ax term vanishes because there is no horizontal acceleration — no thrust, no air resistance, nothing pushing sideways. The projectile lands 217 m out from the base of the cliff, on the valley floor.

Sanity checks before you write the answer down

CheckValueDoes it hang together?
Time to the apex, v0y/g 34.641 / 9.80 = 3.53 s Yes — 3.53 s is well short of the 10.8 s flight time, so the projectile really does pass through an apex and then fall a long way.
Apex height above launch, v0y2/2g 1200 / 19.6 = 61.2 m Yes — 61.2 m above the cliff lip, so 261.2 m above the valley floor. The total fall from the apex is bigger than the cliff alone, which is why the flight lasts longer than a simple 200 m drop.
Landing velocity vy = 34.64 − 9.80(10.836) = −71.6 m/s; speed = 74.3 m/s Yes — negative means downward, and 74.3 m/s at 74.4° below the horizontal is faster than the 40.0 m/s it started with, as it must be after dropping 200.0 m.
Rough range estimate (20.0 m/s)(10.8 s) ≈ 200 m Yes — 217 m is the right order of magnitude for a 40 m/s launch given about ten seconds of flight.

Why the quadratic has two roots

The equation you solved, y = v0y Δt − ½g Δt2, describes a whole parabola — the entire curve, running from Δt = −∞ to Δt = +∞. The projectile occupies only a piece of that curve: the piece from the launch instant to the landing instant. A horizontal line drawn at y = −200.0 m cuts the whole parabola in two places, so the algebra reports two times. One of them lies inside the physical problem and one of them lies before the launch.

What the rejected root actually means

It is tempting to call Δt− = −3.77 s meaningless. It is not. It is perfectly meaningful as a statement about the parabola: a body moving on this same curve, with this same acceleration, would have been 200.0 m below the cliff lip 3.77 s before our launch instant — down in the valley on the far side, on its way up. Extend the dashed branch backwards and you can see the flight that would have produced our launch velocity if the cliff had not been in the way.

So the root is not wrong. It is simply outside the problem. The problem says the projectile is fired at Δt = 0 from the top of the cliff; nothing existed before that, so the negative root is discarded on physical grounds, not mathematical ones. Say it that way on an exam and you have earned the point.

A check worth knowing A parabola is symmetric about its vertex, so the two roots always straddle the apex time by equal amounts: (−3.7666 s + 10.8362 s) / 2 = 3.535 s, which is exactly v0y/g. If the midpoint of your two roots is not the apex time, one of your roots is arithmetically wrong.

When both roots are positive

Not every projectile problem rejects a root for being negative. Ask instead “when is the projectile 30.0 m above the launch point?” and both roots come out positive: once on the way up and once on the way down. Then neither root is unphysical, and the question itself has to tell you which one it wants. The habit to build is not “throw away the negative one” — it is read the two roots back into the story and see which one the story is asking about.

Review

  • Resolve the initial velocity into components, minding the angle: v0x = v0 cos θ, v0y = v0 sin θ when θ is measured from the horizontal.
  • Apply the equations of motion to each component separately. Horizontally ax = 0, so the horizontal component of velocity is constant; vertically ay = −g. State the origin and the sign convention first, and never drift from them.
  • Recombine to answer the question that was actually asked. The two directions share only the clock, so a time found from the vertical equation is the time to use in the horizontal one.
  • Choose the appropriate time from the two the quadratic gives you. Both roots genuinely solve the equation; the one you keep is the one that lies inside the physical problem. The rejected root describes the same mathematical parabola at a time when nothing was flying.
  • For the 200.0 m cliff at 40.0 m/s and 60.0°: v0x = 20.0 m/s, v0y = 34.64 m/s, flight time 10.8 s (rejected root −3.77 s), apex 61.2 m above the lip at 3.53 s, and the projectile lands 217 m from the base of the cliff. Air resistance neglected throughout.
