Free Fall: Measuring g from a Ticker Tape

Synopsis Every problem you solved in Module 1 used a number you were simply handed: 9.80 m/s². In this lab you measure it yourself. A ticker-tape timer drags a paper tape through a vibrating stylus and stamps a dot on it forty times a second while a mass pulls the tape toward the floor. Those dots are a position-versus-time graph that the falling mass drew for you — you just have to measure it with a ruler. You will fit a curve to that data, read g straight out of the fit, and then argue about how close your answer really is.

🔎Why a tape and not a stopwatch?

Drop something from a metre up and it lands in about 0.45 s. Your reaction time is roughly 0.2 s. A stopwatch measurement of that fall is not slightly imprecise — it is dominated by you. Timing is almost always the weakest measurement in a mechanics experiment, and the standard fix is to stop timing by hand.

The ticker-tape timer does exactly that. It ticks at a fixed rate set by the AC line frequency, so the time axis of your data is essentially perfect and free: dot number n happened at t = n/f, no human involved. That moves the entire measurement onto the one axis you can measure well — distance, with a ruler, on a tape lying flat on a table. It also hands you twenty-odd data points instead of one, which is what makes fitting possible at all.

🕑Time Set by the timer, not by you. t = n/f, exact to better than 1%.
📏Distance The measurement you actually make. A millimetre ruler, read carefully, is good to ±0.5 mm.
📊Acceleration Never measured directly. It comes out of a fit to the other two — which is why the fit has to be the right one.

Predict first — before you run a single tape. The falling mass does not just fall: it also has to drag a paper tape through the timer, past a stylus that is striking it forty times a second. If that contact resists the motion at all, what will the acceleration you measure from the tape do?

What you need

  • Ticker-tape timer. Most of our units tick at 40 Hz — check the label on the one you are using, because some run at 60 Hz and every number you compute depends on it.
  • Paper tape, a hanging mass (200–500 g works well), masking tape, and a clear drop of at least 1.5 m — a table edge or the stairwell rail.
  • A metre stick or a two-metre stick with millimetre divisions.
  • A spreadsheet program — Excel, Google Sheets, or LibreOffice Calc.
  • The Lab Template and the Writing Lab Reports module on Brightspace. Read both before you start writing.

Part I — Running the tape

A ticker-tape timer marks a paper tape at a fixed frequency while the tape is pulled through it. Because the marks are made at equal time intervals, the spacing between marks is a direct picture of the speed: evenly spaced dots mean constant speed, and dots that spread out mean the object is speeding up. A usable tape runs about 1.0–1.5 m.

  1. Thread the tape through the timer and attach the hanging mass to one end. Check that the tape runs freely and is not twisted. A twisted tape binds, and binding is the single largest source of error in this method.
  2. Hold the mass so the tape is slack-free but not under tension, with the timer clamped at the table edge and the mass free to fall clear of everything below it.
  3. Start the timer first, then release the mass. Catch the tape — or let the mass land on something soft.
  4. Lay the tape out flat on the bench and tape it down so it cannot shift while you measure.
  5. Repeat until you have three good tapes. A tape is good if the dots are distinct and separating smoothly; discard and re-run any tape with a visible skip, a smear, or a section where the spacing jumps around.

Part II — Measuring the dots

This is the whole experiment. Everything after it is arithmetic.

  1. Find your origin dot. Near the start of the tape you will find a dark blot or a cluster of dots almost on top of each other — that is the timer striking the same spot while the mass was still held, plus the first few ticks of the fall, which are only a few millimetres apart. Skip past it. The first dot you can clearly separate from its neighbours is your dot 0. Circle it and label it 0.
  2. Number the rest. Circle and number every subsequent dot: 1, 2, 3, … Keep going as far as the dots stay clean, ideally 20 or more.
  3. Measure from dot 0 to each dot. Not dot-to-dot. Measuring each gap separately and adding them up compounds every small error you make; measuring from a single origin does not. Record to the nearest millimetre, in centimetres, in your lab notebook.
  4. Measure to the dot, not to the circle you drew around it.
  5. Do all three tapes. Keep each one's data in its own column.
Your dot 0 is not the release point, and that is fine. By the time the dots are far enough apart to measure, the mass has already been falling for a tick or two and is moving at a few tenths of a metre per second. This is unavoidable — the timer is running before you let go, the first gaps are smaller than the dots themselves, and you do not release in sync with the ticks. The analysis below is built so that this costs you nothing. In fact, the fit will tell you how fast the mass was already moving at dot 0, and you will use that number in your discussion.

Part III — Fitting the data

You already know the equation that describes this motion. It is the one from Module 1:

x = x0 + v0 Δt + ½ a (Δt)² position under constant acceleration

And a spreadsheet's order-2 polynomial trendline fits this:

y = A x² + B x + C generic parabola — what the trendline reports

These are the same equation written with different letters. That is the whole trick of this lab. Line them up term by term, with your time column playing the role of x and your position column the role of y:

Table 2 — what each fit parameter is, physically
Fit reportsPhysics calls itSo…
A½aa = 2A — this is your measured acceleration, in m/s²
Bv0the speed the mass already had at dot 0, in m/s
Cx0the position of dot 0 in your own coordinate system — should come out near zero

Notice that you get all three for free. You are not assuming the mass started from rest, and you are not assuming dot 0 is anywhere in particular — the fit works those out and hands them to you.

Doing it in the spreadsheet

  1. Select your time column and your position-in-metres column and insert a Scatter (XY) chart. Time goes on the horizontal axis — the independent variable always does.
  2. Right-click any data point and choose Add Trendline.
  3. In the trendline options, select Polynomial and set the Order to 2.
  4. Check both Display Equation on chart and Display R-squared value on chart. You may need to scroll down in the options panel to find them.
  5. Read A, B, C and R² off the chart. Double A. That is your measured acceleration for that tape.
  6. Repeat for all three tapes. Each tape gets its own graph in your report, with the equation and R² visible.
Widen the equation label before you trust it. Excel displays trendline coefficients with very few significant digits by default, and for A that can round your answer by a percent or more. Click the equation label, open Format Trendline Label, and set the number format to Scientific with 4 decimal places — or at minimum confirm you are reading four significant digits out of A.
⚠Why we are not plotting x against t²

You may have seen free fall handled by plotting position against time squared, so that x = ½gt² becomes a straight line whose slope is g/2. That is a legitimate trick, and it works — as long as the object starts from rest at the point you call zero.

On a ticker tape, it does not. Your dot 0 is a tick or two into the fall, so v0 is not zero, and the real relationship is x = v0t + ½gt². Plotted against t², that is not a straight line — the v0t term becomes v0√(t²), which curves. Fitting a line to it anyway does not simply shift the intercept; it quietly folds part of the velocity term into the slope and pushes your g upward, typically by 10–15% on a real tape.

What makes this worth a paragraph is that the wrong answer looks better. Because the tape also drags, which pushes g down, the two errors partly cancel and the linearized fit lands suspiciously close to 9.8 — for the wrong reason, with an R² of 0.999 reassuring you the whole time. The order-2 fit avoids the trade entirely: it models v0 instead of wishing it away.

The statistics you need

You have three tapes, so you can report a mean and a spread.

ḡ = (1/N) Σ gi     s = √[ Σ(gi − ḡ)² / (N − 1) ] In Excel: =AVERAGE(range) and =STDEV.S(range)

Quote your result as ḡ ± s, rounded so the uncertainty carries one significant digit and the value is rounded to the same decimal place. A mean of 8.6127 with a standard deviation of 0.3142 is reported as 8.6 ± 0.3 m/s² — never as 8.6127 ± 0.3142.

Three tapes is a small sample, and you should say so. A standard deviation from N = 3 is itself very uncertain. It tells you roughly how much one tape differs from another; it does not license a precise claim about how well you know the average. Use it, quote it, and note the limitation in your discussion rather than pretending it is more than it is.

Percent error

% error = | measured − accepted | / accepted × 100% accepted value: g = 9.80 m/s²

Then apply the rule from the lab-writing guidelines. Under 10%, you may attribute the difference to ordinary experimental uncertainty and say so. Over 10%, that is no longer an acceptable explanation: you must name a specific physical mechanism and put a number on it. Expect to be in the second case. The tape method has a real, identifiable systematic error in it, and finding it is the point of the lab.

OptionalA second route to the same number — up to 3 bonus points

You do not need this section to finish the lab or to earn full marks. Everything above is the complete assignment. What follows is a second, independent way to get g out of the same tape, for anyone who wants to try it — it is worth up to three bonus points if you do it correctly and write it up properly.

The fit in Part III found the acceleration from the curvature of a parabola. You can also get it from the slope of a straight line, by turning your positions into velocities first.

The dot spacing is already velocity information: over one tick the mass moves some distance, so dividing that distance by the tick time gives a velocity. The cleanest version uses the dots on either side of the one you are interested in:

vn = ( xn+1 − xn−1 ) / (2Δt) central-difference velocity at dot n, where Δt = 1/f

This is the average velocity across the two ticks straddling dot n, and because the motion has constant acceleration, that average is exactly the instantaneous velocity at dot n — not an approximation. You can compute it for every dot except the first and the last. Then, from Module 1, v = v0 + aΔt, which is a straight line.

If you want to try it

  1. Add a velocity column next to one tape's data: =(x_next − x_prev)/(2*Δt), in m/s. Leave the first and last rows blank — those dots have no neighbour on one side.
  2. Plot velocity against time as a Scatter (XY) chart.
  3. Add a trendline — this time Linear — with the equation and R² displayed.
  4. The slope is your acceleration directly, in m/s². No doubling. The intercept is v0, and it should come out close to the B from your order-2 fit.

To earn the bonus, include the velocity-versus-time graph as a numbered figure with its fit equation and R², show one sample calculation of a central-difference velocity with units, and add two or three sentences to your discussion comparing the two accelerations — how close are they, and what does it mean that two different reductions of the same measurements agree or disagree?

One tape is enough for the bonus. You do not have to do all three.

Questions for your discussion

Work these into your Discussion and Additional Questions sections, in sentences and paragraphs — never as a numbered list, and never by restating the question before answering it. Do not limit yourself to these; add any other insight you have.

  1. Your fit reported a value for B. Convert it into a time: if the mass had been falling freely from rest, how long had it been falling when it reached dot 0? Compare that to one tick of the timer. Does the number you get match the size of the blot you skipped past at the start of the tape?
  2. State which direction your measured g missed 9.80, and propose a physical mechanism that is consistent with that direction. A mechanism that would push the answer the wrong way is evidence against itself.
  3. Treat the tape's drag as a constant retarding force and estimate its size per unit mass from your own deficit: if you measured gmeas, then the drag contributes (9.80 − gmeas) in m/s². Multiply by your hanging mass to get a force in newtons. Is that a plausible amount of friction for paper sliding past a vibrating stylus? Say why or why not.
  4. Your R² values will be very close to 1. Say precisely what that does and does not tell you. Can a data set be shifted by a systematic error and still produce R² = 0.9999?
  5. The time axis of this experiment carries almost no uncertainty, while the distance axis carries all of it. Explain why, and say which of the two is limiting how well you know your answer.
  6. Suppose your three tapes agree with each other to within 0.1 m/s² while sitting a full 1 m/s² away from 9.80. Explain in your own words why that combination is more troubling than a large scatter would be, not less.
  7. Name one change to the apparatus or the procedure that would reduce the dominant error here, and say roughly how much of the deficit you would expect it to remove.
One thing the rubric will not accept: "human error." It explains nothing, and it is not a category of error in experimental physics. If your percent error is under 10%, attribute it to ordinary experimental uncertainty and say so. If it is over 10%, you must identify specific error sources and quantify them — a named mechanism with a number attached.
