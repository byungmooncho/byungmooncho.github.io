The Diffraction Grating

SynopsisTwo slits give broad, faint fringes.  Ten thousand slits per centimetre give maxima that are narrow, bright and far apart — far enough apart that each wavelength in a beam lands in its own place.  The equation is the one from Note 10, unchanged.  What changes is what it is good for.
Before you start — predict

The condition is still d sin θ = mλ — identical to the double slit.  So what does adding thousands more slits actually buy you, if the equation does not change?

Performance expectations
  1. Compute the slit spacing d of a grating from its ruling in lines per centimetre.
  2. Apply d sin θ = mλ to find the angle of a given wavelength in a given order.
  3. Explain dispersion: why a grating separates white light into spectra, one per order.
  4. Locate a line on a screen a known distance away, using tan θ = y/D.

Diffraction grating

d sin θ = mλ

where d is the distance between the “lines” — the slits.  A grating is specified by how many lines are ruled per centimetre, so d is whatever is left when you divide:

Worked, in one line

10 000 lines per cm  →  d = 1 cm / 10 000 = 1 × 10−6 m

Figure 2.  The orders, schematically.  No angles are marked here on purpose: for the grating in the worked example there is no second-order red at all, because sin θ would have to be 1.52.  How many orders you get depends on d.

Dispersion

Rearranged, the condition reads

sin θ = mλ / d

so within one order the angle depends on λ.  Every wavelength goes somewhere different, and white light arriving at the grating leaves as a fan of spectra — one for each order, with the zeroth order going straight through undeviated and staying white.

Worked example

Using the 10 000 lines/cm grating above, a screen is placed 2 m away.  For first order (m = 1), take violet at λV = 380 nm and red at λR = 760 nm.  Find the two angles, then yV and yR.

Figure 1.  First order, drawn to scale.  A grating does not just bend light — it bends each wavelength by a different amount, and by a lot.  Red leaves at more than twice the angle of violet and lands nearly three times as far down the screen.
The angles.  sin θV = (1)(380 × 10−9) / 1 × 10−6 = 0.380, so θV = 22.3°. sin θR = (1)(760 × 10−9) / 1 × 10−6 = 0.760, so θR = 49.5°. On the screen.  yV/2 m = tan θV, so yV = 2 tan 22.3° = 0.815 m. yR/2 m = tan θR, so yR = 2 tan 49.5° = 2.328 m. The visible spectrum, which spans one octave of wavelength, is spread over more than a metre and a half of screen in first order alone.  That is what a grating is for.
The punchline sin θ = mλ/d  —  one angle per wavelength

Back to the prediction: the extra slits do not change where the maxima are.  They change how sharp they are — with thousands of slits, light at the wrong angle is cancelled by some other slit, so each maximum narrows to a line.  The equation locates them; the number of slits is what makes them worth locating.
