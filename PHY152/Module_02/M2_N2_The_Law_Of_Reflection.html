The Law of Reflection

SynopsisLight bounces off a shiny surface, and it does so by a rule so simple it can be written in three symbols.  The whole of this note is that rule, the convention it is measured in, and why that convention is the part people get wrong.
Before you start — predict

A ray of light strikes a flat mirror, making an angle of 30° with the mirror surface.  At what angle does it leave, measured from the normal?  Write down a number before you read on.  If you answered 30°, the figure below is for you.

Performance expectations
  1. State the law of reflection, and say what each angle in it is measured from.
  2. Draw the normal at a point of incidence, and mark θi and θr correctly on a ray diagram.
  3. Convert between an angle given from the surface and the same angle measured from the normal.
  4. Predict the direction of a reflected ray given the direction of the incident ray.

3.  Law of reflection

Light bounces off a shiny surface.

Figure 1.  Both angles are measured from the normal — the dashed line standing at right angles to the surface — and never from the surface itself.  That convention is the single most common place to go wrong, and every equation in this module assumes it.
θi = θr

The angle the light arrives at equals the angle it leaves at.  Nothing in that statement depends on the colour of the light, on how bright it is, or on what the mirror is made of.

The punchline θi = θr, both measured from the normal

Back to the prediction: 30° from the surface is 60° from the normal, so the ray leaves at 60°, not 30°.  Three symbols and one convention — and the convention is the half that costs marks.  Read every angle in this module from the normal, and keep doing it when the surface stops being flat and starts being a lens.

Looking ahead

A shiny surface sends the light back.  A transparent one lets it through instead — but not along the same line it arrived on.  Note 3 is the law of refraction, and the bending that goes with it.
