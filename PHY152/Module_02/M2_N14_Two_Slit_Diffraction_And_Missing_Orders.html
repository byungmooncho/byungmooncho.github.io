Two-Slit Diffraction and Missing Orders

SynopsisNote 10 treated the slits as points and Note 11 treated one slit as wide.  Real slits are both.  The pattern you actually see is the two-slit interference pattern multiplied by the single-slit envelope — and where the envelope happens to be zero, a bright fringe that Note 10 promised you simply is not there.
Before you start — predict

You count the bright fringes on the screen and find … the third one on each side is missing.  Orders 1, 2, 4, 5 are all present.  Nothing is wrong with the apparatus.  What could put a hole in an otherwise regular row of fringes?

Performance expectations
  1. State the two-slit pattern as a product of an interference term and a diffraction envelope.
  2. Derive β = mπD/d at the position of the mth interference maximum.
  3. Predict which orders are missing from the ratio d/D.
  4. Count how many interference fringes fall inside the central diffraction peak.

The two effects together

The rule The diffraction pattern of two slits of width D separated by a distance d
is the interference pattern of two point sources separated by d
multiplied by the diffraction pattern of a slit of width D
Figure 1.  The two patterns, and their product.  Every curve here is sampled from its own formula — the missing order is missing because the arithmetic kills it, not because it was left out of the drawing.

As D grows the envelope narrows — a wide slit diffracts less — so fewer and fewer fringes survive underneath it.  Shrink D and the envelope opens out, letting more through.

Why an order goes missing

Take the two conditions and put them together.  Interference gives a maximum at

d sin θ = mλ   so    sin θ = mλ/d

and the diffraction intensity at that angle is I = I0(sin β/β)2 with β = πD sin θ/λ.  Substituting one into the other:

β = (πD/λ)(mλ/d) β = mπD / d

and the wavelength has cancelled.  Whether an order survives depends only on the ratio of the slit width to the slit spacing.

  1. Show that m = 3 is the missing order when D = 2λ and d = 6λ, and find the intensity of the m = 1 fringe.
β = mπD/d = mπ(2λ/6λ) = mπ(⅓) = mπ/3. When m = 3: β = (3)π(⅓) = π. sin π = 0, so I = I0(0/π)2 = 0.  The interference condition says “bright here” and the diffraction envelope says “nothing gets out at this angle”.  The envelope wins. For m = 1: β = π/3, so I(m=1) = I0(sin(π/3)/(π/3))2 = 0.684 I0 — already down by a third, and that is only the first fringe out.

How many fringes fit inside the central peak?

Young’s two-slit interference.  Slit width D = 0.02 mm, separated by d = 0.2 mm, with λ = 500 nm.  How many fringes are observed in the central peak of the diffraction pattern?

Hint

Find the first dark diffraction — the edge of the central peak — and then ask how many interference orders fit inside that angle.

Figure 2.  Narrow the slits relative to their spacing and the envelope opens out, letting many fringes through.  The ratio d/D = 10 is exactly the order that goes missing — which is the worked example below, drawn.
Diffraction — the first dark fringe, m = 1: D sin θ = mλ D sin θ = λ:  (0.02 × 10−3) sin θ = 500 × 10−9, so θ = 0.025 rad. Interference at that same angle:  d sin θ = mλ, so m = d sin θ / λ m = (0.2 × 10−3)(sin 0.025) / (500 × 10−9) = 10. And notice that 10 is exactly d/D = 0.2/0.02.  The ratio of the slit spacing to the slit width is the order that lands on the edge of the central peak — which is the same statement as β = mπD/d, read backwards.
The punchline β = mπD/d  —  the wavelength cancels

Back to the prediction: a hole in a regular row of fringes means an interference maximum has landed on a diffraction minimum.  The missing order is d/D, and it depends on the geometry of the slits alone — change the colour of the light and the same order is still missing.

Looking ahead

Two slits give fringes you have to look hard at.  Note 15 uses ten thousand of them per centimetre, which makes the maxima so sharp and so far apart that each wavelength in a beam separates out on its own.
