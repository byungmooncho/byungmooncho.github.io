Thin-Film Interference

SynopsisSoap bubbles and oil slicks show colours no pigment put there.  Two rays leave the film — one off the front surface, one off the back — and interfere.  But the obvious calculation gives the wrong answer for a very thin film, and fixing it needs something new: reflection can flip a wave over.
Before you start — predict

A soap bubble gets thinner and thinner just before it bursts, so the two rays travel almost exactly the same distance.  The path difference goes to zero.  So the thinnest part should be brightest.  In fact it goes black.  Why?

Performance expectations
  1. Identify the two interfering rays in a thin film and say which surface each reflected from.
  2. Determine whether a reflection inverts the wave, from the indices on either side of it.
  3. Combine the path difference 2t with the phase changes on reflection to get the correct condition.
  4. Compute film thicknesses for constructive or destructive interference, using λn = λ0/n.

3.  Further topics — thin-film interference

Figure 1.  Two rays reach your eye from a thin film: one bounced off the top surface, one that went through, bounced off the bottom surface and came back out.  They are parallel, they overlap, and they have travelled different distances — so they interfere.  That is the whole mechanism.

In a soap bubble, or any very thin layer, the dark spots are where the bubble is thinnest — and that is where it breaks.

The question this raises

Q.  If t is very small — a very thin film — the path-length difference between Ray 1 and Ray 2 is small.  So shouldn’t there be constructive interference?

A.  Phase change upon reflection!!

Reflection can flip the wave over

Specifically, reflection causes a 180° phase change.  We observed this clearly in mechanical waves — a pulse on a string reflecting off a fixed end comes back upside down.  For a light wave, the deciding parameter is n, the refractive index.

  • Low to high n → 180°, or π rad, phase change.
  • No phase change when reflecting from a lower index.
  • Refraction causes no phase change at all — only reflection does this.

And the useful part: a 180° phase change is equivalent to ±λ/2 of distance travelled, so it can be added straight into the path-length bookkeeping rather than tracked separately.

Figure 2.  Identical geometry, different materials — and opposite rules.  Count the inversions before you write anything down: two inversions or none and they cancel; exactly one and the film behaves as though it were half a wavelength thicker than it is.

Putting the two together

If the thickness t is not negligible, you must consider the path-length difference as well as the phase change on reflection.  Remember that a path-length difference of mλ is constructive and (m + ½)λ is destructive — and inside the film the wavelength is not the one you started with:

λn = λ0 / n

The ray goes down and back, so the extra path is 2t.  Which condition that satisfies depends on how many inversions happened:

InversionsConstructiveDestructive
Two, or none
they cancel out		 2t = λn, 2λn, 3λn… 2t = λn/2, 3λn/2, 5λn/2…
Exactly one
a net λ/2 shift		 2t = λn/2, 3λn/2, 5λn/2… 2t = 0, λn, 2λn, 3λn…
The two rows are each other’s opposites.  Nothing else in this note is worth memorising if you can count inversions and read the right row.

Worked example 1 — an anti-reflection coating

A layer of MgF2 (magnesium fluoride, n = 1.38) is deposited on glass (n = 1.53) in air.  What is the thinnest MgF2 layer that will limit the reflection of 550 nm light?

Count the inversions.  1.00 → 1.38 at the top: low to high, inverted.  1.38 → 1.53 at the bottom: low to high again, inverted.  Both rays inverted, so the inversions cancel — first row of the table. We want destructive (that is what “limit the reflection” means), and the thinnest, so take the first term: 2t = λn/2. With λn = λ/n2, that is t = λ / 4n2 = 550 nm / (4 × 1.38) = 99.6 nm. A hundred nanometres of coating, and the reflection off your glasses largely disappears.  This calculation is on every camera lens you have ever used.

Worked example 2 — a soap bubble

A soap bubble is water (n = 1.33) with air on both sides.  Find the three smallest thicknesses that give constructive interference for 650 nm light, and the three smallest that give destructive interference.

  1. Count the inversions first.  This film is not the coating above, and the answer changes because of it.
Count the inversions.  1.00 → 1.33 at the top: inverted.  1.33 → 1.00 at the bottom: high to low, no inversion.  Exactly one — second row of the table, the opposite of the coating. Constructive:  2tc = λn/2, 3λn/2, 5λn/2 tc1 = 650 / (4 × 1.33) = 122 nm  ·   tc2 = 3(650) / (4 × 1.33) = 366 nm  ·   tc3 = 5(650) / (4 × 1.33) = 610 nm Destructive:  2td = 0, λn, 2λn, 3λn td0 = 0  ·   td1 = 650 / (2 × 1.33) = 244 nm  ·   td2 = 2(650) / (2 × 1.33) = 488 nm  ·   td3 = 3(650) / (2 × 1.33) = 733 nm Look at that first destructive answer: t = 0.  Zero thickness is a dark condition for this film — which is the prediction at the top of the note, falling straight out of the arithmetic.
The punchline Count the inversions, then pick the row

Back to the prediction: the thinnest part of the bubble goes black because one reflection inverts and the other does not.  With t → 0 the two rays are still half a wavelength out of step, and they cancel.  The blackness just before a bubble bursts is direct evidence of the phase change on reflection.
