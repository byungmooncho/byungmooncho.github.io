Diffraction and Huygens’ Principle

SynopsisWaves bend around corners.  Huygens’ principle says why, in one sentence, and that sentence then does something strange: it makes a single slit produce bright and dark bands — with the roles of mλ and (m + ½)λ exactly reversed from Note 10.
Before you start — predict

In Note 10, d sin θ = mλ gave a bright fringe.  In this note, a sin θ = mλ gives a dark one.  Same-looking equation, opposite answer.  What is different about the physical situation?  Guess now; Figure 2 settles it.

2.  Diffraction

Waves bend around a corner.  Ocean swell arriving at the end of a breakwater does not stop at the line of the wall and leave flat water behind it — the crests curl round into the sheltered area.  That bending is diffraction.

Huygens’ principle

Huygens’ principle Every point of an advancing wavefront is a new centre of disturbance
from which emanate independent waves in all directions

Each point serves as a source of secondary wavelets, and the new wavefront is the tangential surface of all those secondary wavelets.

Figure 1.  Huygens’ principle.  Every point of an advancing wavefront is a new centre of disturbance from which independent waves emanate in all directions — it serves as a source of secondary wavelets, and the new wavefront is the tangential surface of all of them.  In the middle the tangent surface is flat, so the wave carries straight on.  At the edges it curves round, and that is diffraction.

One slit, and why it goes dark

Huygens says every point across the opening is itself a source.  So the light arriving at one spot on the screen is the sum of contributions from every point of the slit — and those contributions can cancel each other.

Figure 2.  Pairwise cancellation.  At the angle where a sin θ = λ, the rays spread evenly across the red segment.  Pair each ray in the top half with the one directly below it in the bottom half: every pair is λ/2 apart, so every pair cancels, and nothing at all reaches the screen at that angle.  That is why a sin θ = mλ gives darkness here, and brightness in Note 10.

Walk along the four cases and the pattern appears:

ConditionWhat happens across the slitResult
a sin θ = 0λevery ray in step with every otherBright — the central maximum
a sin θ = 1λpairwise cancellation for all raysDark
a sin θ = 3⁄2λsome rays survive and do not cancelBright — a side peak
a sin θ = 2λpairwise cancellation for all raysDark
The single-slit minima a sin θ = mλ   sin θ = mλ/a   m = ±1, ±2, ±3…

and these are DARK.  Note that m = 0 is excluded: the centre is the brightest point of the whole pattern.

Worked example

A laser of λ = 550 nm falls on a single slit.  The second diffraction minimum is at θ = 45°.

  1. (1) What is the width of the slit?  (2) At what angle is the first minimum?
(1)  A minimum, so a sin θ = mλ — the dark condition.  The second one, so m = 2. a = 2(550 × 10−9) / sin 45° = 1.56 × 10−6 m. (2)  Same equation, m = 1: θ1 = sin−1[ (1)(550 × 10−9) / 1.56 × 10−6 ] = 20.6°. Notice that 20.6° is not half of 45°.  The minima are evenly spaced in sin θ, not in θ — which is exactly what Figure 3’s horizontal axis is telling you.

How bright, not just where

The conditions above say where the pattern is dark.  The full intensity is

I = I0 (sin β / β)2,    β = πD sin θ / λ

where D is the slit width.  At θ = 0 the ratio (sin β/β) → 1, so I = I0: the central bright fringe.  At β = π and β = 2π, sin β = 0 and so I = 0 — the dark fringes again, from the other direction.

Figure 3.  The single-slit intensity, sampled from I = I0(sin β/β)2.  D marks each dark fringe: the curve touches zero at every whole multiple of π.  The first side peak reaches 4.5 % of the centre — which is why the central maximum is the only part of a single-slit pattern that is easy to see.

The first side peak sits at β = 3π/2, midway between the first two zeros:

I1 = I0(sin(3π/2) / (3π/2))2 = 0.045 I0

and because β carries D in the numerator, widening the slit narrows the pattern.  Squeeze the slit and the light spreads out further — which is the opposite of what a ray picture would predict, and is the clearest everyday sign that light is a wave.

The punchline Two slits: mλ is bright.  One slit: mλ is dark.

Back to the prediction: in Note 10, d is the gap between two sources and mλ means they arrive in step.  Here, a is the width of a single source, mλ is the spread across it, and it means every ray finds a partner half a wavelength away.  Same algebra, opposite physics.
