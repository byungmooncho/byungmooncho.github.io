Converging Lenses: Two Routes to a Focal Length, and a Two-Lens System

SynopsisYou will find the focal length of the same lens two independent ways — once by focusing a very distant object, once from the intercept of a straight line fitted to 1/di against 1/do — and then put two lenses in series and check the magnification you measure against the one the thin lens equation predicts. The data come from the lab videos; the analysis and the report are yours.

Fig. 1  The optical bench. The light source is the object; the screen is where a real image is found. Each of the three positions is read off the bench scale.

The optical bench carries the light source, one or two lenses and the screen, and every component slides along it. The bench is marked in centimetres, so what you read off it is always a position. Distances come later, as differences.

Part I — focal length from the theory of converging lenses

The focal length of a lens is the distance at which parallel rays come into focus.

Fig. 2  Rays from a distant object arrive parallel and are brought together at the focal point, which is what makes Part I work: with the object far enough away, the image distance IS the focal length.

In practice, if an object is much farther away than the focal length, its image is in focus at the focal point — and this is the one situation where the image distance and the focal length are the same number. That gives a quick way to measure a focal length: hold the lens in one hand and a piece of paper in the other and move them until something very far away comes into focus. The gap between lens and paper is the focal length. It works best in a shadowed area with a brightly lit subject; in the video the lab is darkened and the “object” is a stand of trees well outside the window.

Procedure

  1. Mount the 10 cm converging lens on the bench at about the 140 cm mark. Record its position as xL in Table 3.
  2. Point the optical bench at some distant object.
  3. Place the screen at about the 150 cm mark.
  4. Move the screen toward or away from the lens until a sharp image forms on it. Watch the image sharpen — not the screen’s position.
  5. Record the screen’s position as xi in Table 3.
  6. Repeat steps 1–5 twice more with the lens at different positions.
  7. From the raw data, calculate the lens-to-image distance for each trial and record it in Table 4.
  8. Average the three. That average is the focal length from the theory of converging lenses. Record it as f1.
  9. Calculate the percent accuracy of f1 against the lens’s stated focal length of 10 cm.

Part II — focal length from the thin lens equation

The thin lens equation ties the focal length to the object and image distances:

1/f  =  1/do  +  1/di

Written that way it is already the equation of a straight line. Plot 1/di against 1/do and the line has slope −1 and an intercept of 1/f on the vertical axis — so it is the intercept that gives you the focal length.

Positions are not distances.  The object distance do is the gap between the object and the lens; the image distance di is the gap between the lens and the image. Both are differences of two bench readings. Carrying a position into a calculation that wants a distance is the single most common error in this lab.
Fig. 5  Positions against distances. The bench gives you positions; both distances are differences you work out from them. Confusing the two is the most common way to lose marks here.

Procedure

  1. Place the 10 cm lens at the 100 cm position and record it as xL. Leave the lens there for the rest of Part II.
  2. Place the lighted object at the 40 cm position and record it as xo in Table 6.
  3. Place the screen beyond the lens, past the 100 cm mark.
  4. Move the screen until a sharp image appears.
  5. Record the screen position as xi in Table 6.
  6. Repeat steps 2–5 for xo = 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, 70, 75, 80 and 85 cm.
  7. From the raw data, calculate do and di in cm and record them in Table 7.
  8. Calculate 1/do and 1/di and record them in Table 7.
  9. Plot 1/di on the vertical axis against 1/do on the horizontal axis as a scatter plot in Excel.
  10. Fit a linear trendline and display its equation on the chart.
  11. Take the vertical-axis intercept from that equation and, using the thin lens equation, work out the focal length. Record it as f2.
  12. Calculate the percent accuracy of f2 against the stated 10 cm.

Part III — the image formed by two converging lenses

With more than one lens the algebra could get heavy, but it does not have to: take the lenses one at a time and apply the thin lens equation twice. The trick is that the image from the first lens is the object of the second. Measure every distance from the lens in play to the object or image that belongs to it. Here you record the positions of each lens and each image, then take differences.

Procedure

  1. Prepare a raw data table like Table 8.
  2. Place the object at the 30 cm mark and record it as xo.
  3. Place the 10 cm lens at the 50 cm mark. Call it lens 1 and record its position as xL1.
  4. Find the image position for this lens with the screen and record it as xi1.
  5. Place the 20 cm lens at the 115 cm position. Call it lens 2 and record its position as xL2.
  6. The image due to lens 1 is now the object of lens 2.
  7. Move the screen beyond lens 2 and find the image it forms. Record that position as xi2.
  8. With the vernier caliper, measure the size of the object and of the final image. Record them as ho and hi2.
  9. Determine the magnification from the heights, M = hi2 / ho. Call it the experimental magnification 1, Me1.
  10. From the raw data, calculate the object distance do1 (lens 1 to the object) and the image distance di1 (lens 1 to the first image).
  11. Calculate the magnification of the first lens, M1 = −di1 / do1.
  12. Again from the raw data, calculate do2 (lens 2 to the first image) and di2 (lens 2 to the second image).
  13. Calculate the magnification of the second lens, M2 = −di2 / do2.
  14. Calculate the overall magnification M = (M1)(M2). Call it the experimental magnification 2, Me2.
  15. Draw a ray diagram for this two-lens case, using the nominal focal lengths, the original lens positions xL1 and xL2, and the object position xo. Use the Ray Tracing Template that comes with this lab — print it and use a straight edge to draw the parallel ray, the focal ray and the middle ray, with the techniques from the lecture notes and the textbook.
  16. Now work out the image distances and the magnifications theoretically, from the lens equation and the magnification equation. Call that magnification Mt.
  17. Calculate the percent difference between Me1 and Mt, and between Me2 and Mt. Base both percentages on the theoretical magnification Mt.

Questions for your discussion

  1. Part I treats the image distance as the focal length. What has to be true of the object for that to hold, and roughly how far off would you be if the “distant” object were only ten focal lengths away?
  2. Your two focal lengths f1 and f2 came from the same lens. Which do you trust more, and what in your own data supports that?
  3. The fitted line should have slope −1. What does yours have, and what would a slope noticeably different from −1 tell you about the measurements?
  4. Why does the intercept give the focal length while the slope does not depend on it at all?
  5. In Part III you found the magnification twice, once from heights and once as (M1)(M2). If those disagree, which measurement would you suspect first, and why?
  6. The image of lens 1 is the object of lens 2 even though nothing physical sits there. Explain in a sentence or two why the second lens cannot tell the difference.
