Refraction and Snell’s Law

SynopsisA shiny surface sends light back; a transparent one lets it through, but not along the line it arrived on.  The bending has one equation, and that equation comes out of something already in hand from Note 1 — that light travels at a different speed in a different medium.
Before you start — predict

A ray goes from air into water.  Does it bend toward the normal or away from it — and which quantity in v = c/n decides?  Write both down.  Figure 1 is drawn to scale, so you can check yourself against it.

Performance expectations
  1. State Snell’s law and identify every symbol in it, including which angle is measured from what.
  2. Predict which way a ray bends at an interface from the two refractive indices alone.
  3. Calculate an unknown angle or an unknown index from the other three quantities.
  4. Derive Snell’s law from the wavelength change, using that the frequency is the same in both media.

4.  Law of refraction, Snell’s law

Bending of a light “ray” at an interface between two media of different refractive index.

Figure 1.  Drawn to scale from the equation itself: θi = 50° in air gives θr = 36.1° in water.  Going into the denser medium the ray bends toward the normal.  Both angles are measured from the normal — the same convention as Note 2, and the one the equation is written in.
ni sin θi = nr sin θr

Where that equation comes from

Note 1 left us with v = c/n: light goes slower in a denser medium.  The frequency f does not change when light crosses a boundary — the wave arriving and the wave leaving are the same wave — so if the speed drops, the wavelength must drop with it.  That is the whole mechanism, and the geometry below turns it into the equation.

Figure 2.  The construction behind the equation.  Two parallel rays strike the surface a distance L apart.  λi and λr are legs of two right triangles that share the same hypotenuse L, so sin θi = λi/L and sin θr = λr/L.
  1. Get ni sin θi = nr sin θr from Figure 2, using v = fλ and v = c/n.
From the two right triangles on the shared hypotenuse L:     sin θi = λi/L ⇒ L = λi/sin θi   and    sin θr = λr/L ⇒ L = λr/sin θr It is the same L, so   λi/sin θi = λr/sin θr Now the wavelengths.  v = fλ and v = c/n give vi = c/ni = fλi, so λi = c/(f ni) — and likewise λr = c/(f nr).  The same f in both, which is the step the whole derivation rests on. Substituting:    c/(f ni) · 1/sin θi = c/(f nr) · 1/sin θr c and f cancel from both sides, leaving    ni sin θi = nr sin θr
The punchline ni sin θi = nr sin θr

Bigger n means smaller angle from the normal.  Nothing in the derivation used anything but the speed change from Note 1 and the fact that frequency survives a boundary — which is why the same equation holds for every pair of transparent media, not just air and water.

Looking ahead

Run the light the other way — out of the dense medium instead of into it — and the angle on the far side grows instead of shrinking.  Push it far enough and there is no far side left.  Note 4 is total internal reflection.
