Two-Lens Systems

SynopsisEvery optical instrument worth owning has more than one lens in it, and not one of them needs an equation beyond the two you already have.  You solve the first lens, take its image, and hand that image to the second lens as its object.  That one sentence is the entire method.
Before you start — predict

Lens 1 inverts the object.  Lens 2 then inverts what it is given.  So is the final image upright or inverted compared with the original object?  And what does that tell you about the sign of the total magnification?

Performance expectations
  1. State the rule that links the two stages of a multi-lens problem.
  2. Compute the object distance for the second lens from the image distance of the first and the separation.
  3. Combine the two magnifications into the magnification of the system.
  4. Recognise when the intermediate image falls beyond the second lens, and say what its sign then means.

5.  Two-lens system

The rule The image of the first becomes the object of the second
Figure 1.  Two converging lenses, solved one at a time.  Lens 1 forms a real inverted image between the lenses (red).  That image is then treated as an ordinary object for lens 2 (purple) — nothing about the second calculation knows there was ever a first one.

Work the lenses strictly in order.  For lens 1 use the object you were given and find di1.  Then, if the lenses are a distance L apart,

do2 = L − di1

and feed that into the same thin lens equation for lens 2.  The magnification of the whole system is the product

m = m1 × m2
Watch the sign of do2.  If lens 1 would have formed its image past lens 2, then di1 > L and do2 comes out negative.  That is the virtual object of the table in Note 8 — not an error, and the equation handles it without any special case.

Worked example — carry the construction through

  1. In Figure 1, lens 1 has already formed its image between the two lenses.  Carry the construction through lens 2 by hand.  What do you use as the object, which rays do you draw, and what happens to the magnification?
Use the first image as the object.  The image of the first becomes the object of the second.  It does not matter that no object is really sitting there — the light leaving that point is heading towards lens 2 exactly as if one were. Forget lens 1 completely.  Rub it out of the picture if it helps.  What is left is a single lens with a single object in front of it, which is Note 7 again, unchanged. Draw the same two rays.  From the tip of that first image: one parallel to the axis, which bends through the focus beyond lens 2; one straight through the centre of lens 2, undeviated.  Where they cross is the final image. The magnifications multiply.  Lens 1 inverted the object; lens 2 inverts that image again, so the final image comes out upright — two inversions undo each other — and its size is the first magnification times the second, not the sum. The second construction is the case worth watching.  If lens 1 would have formed its image beyond lens 2, the light reaches lens 2 still converging and there is no first image to point at.  You use the point where it would have formed anyway: that is the virtual object of the table in Note 8, and nothing else about the method changes.
The punchline do2 = L − di1    m = m1m2

Back to the prediction: two inversions give an upright final image, and the two negative magnifications multiply to a positive one.  No new physics has entered this module since Note 6 — only the discipline of doing one lens at a time.

Looking ahead

That closes geometric optics: light treated strictly as rays that travel in straight lines and bend at surfaces.  From Note 10 the ray model runs out.  Two beams of light will be made to arrive at the same place and produce darkness, which no amount of ray tracing can account for.
