Image Formation With Lenses and Spherical Mirrors

SynopsisA curved mirror needs no new equation.  The thin lens equation and the magnification equation both carry over unchanged — all that changes is which way the signs point, and one short relation between the radius of a mirror and its focal length.  This note is that relation and those sign tables, and it is the note to keep open during every problem that follows.
Before you start — predict

A concave mirror is ground to a radius of curvature of 40 cm.  Where is its focal point?  Write a number down.  Most people’s first answer is 40.

Performance expectations
  1. Relate the radius of curvature of a spherical mirror to its focal length.
  2. Apply the mirror and thin lens equation 1/do + 1/di = 1/f to a mirror.
  3. Read the sign convention table correctly for concave and convex mirrors and for converging and diverging lenses.
  4. Predict whether a given arrangement gives a real or virtual, upright or inverted image, before computing anything.

4.  Image formation with lenses and spherical mirrors

R = 2f

The centre of curvature sits at twice the focal length.  Grind a mirror to radius R and you have fixed its focal length at R/2 — there is no further choice in the matter.

Figure 1.  A concave mirror, object outside C.  The arc is a real piece of a circle of radius R centred on C, and F sits exactly halfway along it.  The three reflected rays cross between F and C because 1/do + 1/di = 1/f says they must.

Sign convention for spherical mirrors and thin lenses

Applies to the mirror and thin lens equation 1/do + 1/di = 1/f and to the magnification equation hi/ho = −di/do.

∗  An optical system using several mirrors or lenses sometimes uses the image formed by the first as the object for the second.  When that image falls on the far side, the object distance is negative and the object is called virtual.  That is exactly the situation Note 9 is about.

The two columns with + / − in them are the only ones where the answer depends on where you put the object; everywhere else the sign is fixed by the optic alone.  The convex lens is the one to know cold — it is the case the rest of this module keeps coming back to, and the one with the most room to go wrong.

Worked example

  1. A light bulb stands 0.75 m in front of a converging lens of focal length 0.50 m.  A screen is moved back and forth behind the lens until the bulb comes into focus on it.  Where does the screen have to be, and what does the picture on it look like?
Figure 2.  The bulb, the lens and the screen.  The bulb and the focal length are given; where the screen goes is what the equation decides.
1/do + 1/di = 1/f, so 1/0.75 + 1/di = 1/0.50. 1/di = 2.000 − 1.333 = 0.667 m−1, so di = +1.50 m.  The screen goes a metre and a half behind the lens. m = −di/do = −1.50/0.75 = −2. Positive di means real — and it had better be, because a virtual image cannot land on a screen.  Negative m means inverted, and |m| = 2 means twice the size.  That is why the bulb on the screen is upside down.
The punchline R = 2f, and every equation from Note 6 still holds

Back to the prediction: a 40 cm radius gives a 20 cm focal length, not 40.  Mirrors bring no new physics into this module — only new signs, and one factor of two.

Looking ahead

Everything so far has used one optic at a time.  Note 9 puts two in a row, and the whole trick is in the footnote to the table above: the image of the first becomes the object of the second.
