Reflection and Refraction: Verifying Two Laws with Pins and Paper

SynopsisTwo laws, measured rather than quoted.  You will locate the image in a plane mirror by sighting on it from four directions and show that it sits as far behind the glass as the object sits in front, then send light through a plastic block and get the index of refraction out of Snell’s law twice — going in, and coming out.  Everything is done with pins, paper and a protractor, which is the point: the laws fall out of the geometry, not out of an instrument.

What you need

  • Plane mirror
  • Refraction block (the clear plastic rectangle)
  • Unlined paper, several sheets
  • Metric ruler
  • Protractor
  • Straight pins
  • Two pencils
  • Corrugated cardboard, to take the pins
  • Rubber band, to hold the mirror upright

The mirror stands vertically against a small wooden block or a piece of corrugated cardboard held with the rubber band.  The paper goes on top of the cardboard mat so the pins will stand up in it.  Keep the mirror perpendicular to the paper — if it leans, every distance you measure afterwards is wrong by a factor you cannot recover.

Theory

Parallax

Hold two pencils upright at arm’s length, one behind the other, and move your head from side to side.  They appear to shift relative to one another.  That apparent shift is parallax, and it shrinks as the two pencils get closer together.  When it vanishes, they are at the same place.  That is the whole basis of Part II: you find where the image is by moving a second pencil until the parallax between it and the image disappears.

The law of reflection

An object in front of a plane mirror produces an image that is upright, virtual, the same size as the object, and the same distance behind the mirror surface as the object is in front of it.  The law itself is about angles:

θi = θr

both measured from the normal — the line perpendicular to the mirror at the point where the ray strikes it.  Not from the mirror surface.  That is the single most common way to lose marks on this lab.

Fig. 1  The law of reflection. Both angles are measured from the normal, not from the mirror.

Snell’s law

When light crosses from one transparent medium into another it changes direction:

n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2

where n1 and n2 are the indices of refraction of the two media, θ1 is the angle of incidence and θ2 the angle of refraction — again both from the normal.  For air, n = 1.00 to three figures, which is why the air side of every calculation in this lab is easy.

Fig. 2  Snell’s law at an air-to-glass boundary, drawn to scale for n2 = 1.52, the value you will be comparing your own result against.  The faint dashed line shows where the ray would have gone if it had not bent.
Before you take any data.  Read step 5 of Part I and watch the second video.  The entire lab rests on one skill — lining your eye up along the edge of a ruler with the image of a pin — and if you are sighting carelessly, no amount of careful protractor work afterwards will rescue the numbers.  Your dashed construction lines in step 10 are the diagnostic: if they fail to meet anywhere near a single point, your sightings were bad, and the fix is to do them again rather than to average harder.

Procedure

Part I — the law of reflection

  1. Put a clean sheet of paper on the cardboard and draw a straight line across the centre.  Label it LM.
  2. Mark a dot about 5 cm in front of that line, near the middle of the paper.  Label it O.  This is the object.
  3. Stand the mirror on the paper with the silvered surface exactly on LM, held upright by the block and rubber band, perpendicular to the paper.
  4. Put a straight pin at O, vertical.
  5. Lay the ruler on the paper about 5 cm to the left of the pin and sight along its edge at the image of the pin in the mirror.  When the edge of the ruler and the image line up, draw a line along the ruler and label it AB.
  6. AB is a reflected ray.  Put an arrowhead on the end pointing back toward you.
  7. Move the ruler another 3–4 cm to the left and sight the image again.  Draw and label that line CD.  A different coloured pencil for each pair of lines will save you confusion later.
  8. Repeat steps 5–7 on the right-hand side, labelling the lines EF and GH.
  9. Remove the pin and the mirror.  Extend AB, CD, EF and GH back to LM.
  10. Using dashed lines, extend AB and CD, and EF and GH, until they intersect behind LM.  That intersection is the position of the image, I.
  11. In a real drawing the dashed lines will not meet at a perfect point.  Take the average position.  If the spread is large, repeat steps 4–11.
  12. Two incident rays are enough to locate the image.  Four were used here only to show that sightings from either side give the same answer; any two of them will do.
  13. Measure the perpendicular distance from I back to LM, and from O back to LM.  Record both.

Part II — parallax

  1. Stand the mirror back on LM.
  2. Stand a pencil, taller than the mirror, at the old object position O, and look at its image.
  3. Hold a second pencil behind the mirror so that the part showing above the mirror looks like a continuation of the image of the first.
  4. Move your head side to side and adjust the second pencil until the parallax between it and the image is gone.
  5. Where, in general, does that put the second pencil relative to the mirror?
  6. Mark the position.
  7. Compare it with the intersection of the dashed lines you constructed in step 10 of Part I.  How does the size of the image pencil compare with the object?

Part III — image orientation

  1. Set the mirror up again on a line across a fresh sheet, perpendicular to the paper.
  2. Draw an object triangle in front of the mirror and label the vertices A, B, C.  Two constraints: no side of the triangle at right angles to the mirror, and not equilateral or isosceles.  Both exist so that a reversed image is distinguishable from an unreversed one.
  3. Put a pin at vertex A and sight two lines from its image along the ruler.  Label them A1 and A2.  This is exactly the technique from Part I.
  4. Do the same at B, labelling B1 and B2.
  5. Do the same at C.
  6. Extend each pair backwards to find A′, B′ and C′.
  7. Join A′, B′ and C′ to construct the image triangle.
  8. If your constructed triangle does not closely resemble the original, repeat steps 1–5.
  9. Measure and record the distance from each of A, B, C, A′, B′, C′ to the mirror line.

Part IVa — refraction, air into glass

  1. Draw a set of perpendicular lines on a fresh sheet and set the refraction block on them.
  2. Put a pin at point B, on the normal, against the side of the block.
  3. Put pin A1 about 4 cm from the block, to one side of the normal, so that the line A1B makes a 30° angle with the normal.
  4. Looking through the plastic from the other side, line up A1 and B with a third pin C1 against the far side of the block.
  5. Remove the block and join A1 to B and B to C1 with straight lines.
  6. Measure the angle of incidence between the normal and BC1.  Record it.
  7. Repeat steps 3–6 with pin A2 at 45°, labelling the new points A2 and C2.
  8. Calculate the index of refraction from Snell’s law, taking n = 1.00 for air, then average your values.

Part IVb — refraction, glass into air

  1. Fresh sheet, perpendicular lines again, block in place.
  2. Put a pin at B as before.
  3. Put pin C1 at an arbitrary point on the far side of the block, against the glass.
  4. View from the side where B sits on the normal.  The light now travels from C1 through the glass and out into the air, so the angle of incidence is the one inside the glass and the angle of refraction is in the air.
  5. With your eye lined up with C1 and B, place a third pin A1 about 4 cm from the block along that line of sight.
  6. Remove the block and join C1B and BA1.
  7. Measure the angle of incidence between the normal and C1B, and the angle of refraction between the normal and BA1.  Record them.
  8. Repeat steps 3–7 with pin C2 at a different angle, labelling the new points A2 and C2.
  9. Now move pin C3 further and further from the normal and see whether you can find a position that gives an angle of refraction of 90°.  That angle of incidence is the critical angle, θc.
  10. Remove the block and join C3B and BA3.  BA3 should come out along the surface, since the refraction angle is 90°.
  11. Measure that critical angle and record it.
  12. Calculate the index of refraction from your angles, again with n = 1.00 for air, and average.
  13. Take the grand average of the indices from this section.
  14. Work out the percent error of that grand average against the accepted value for this glass, n = 1.52.

Questions for your discussion

  1. Your dashed construction lines in Part I almost certainly did not meet at a single point.  What physically causes that spread, and does it bias your image distance in a particular direction or just scatter it?
  2. Compare di and d0 from Fig. 4.  How close are they, and what would a systematic difference between them tell you about how the mirror was standing?
  3. In Part III, the image triangle is the same size as the object but reversed.  Explain why the handout forbids an isosceles triangle, in terms of what you would and would not be able to detect.
  4. You measured n twice, once going into the glass and once coming out.  Should those two agree in principle?  Did yours?  If they differ, which direction do you trust more, and why?
  5. A critical angle exists going from glass to air but not from air to glass.  Say why in one sentence, using Snell’s law.
