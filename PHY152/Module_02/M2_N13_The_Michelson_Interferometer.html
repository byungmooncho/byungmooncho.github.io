The Michelson Interferometer

SynopsisSplit a beam in two, send the halves down separate arms, bring them back together and let them interfere.  Because the path difference is now adjustable, the fringe pattern becomes a ruler — one whose smallest division is a fraction of a wavelength of light.
Before you start — predict

You move one mirror by 315 nm.  That is about a three-hundredth of the thickness of a human hair.  What would you have to see for that movement to be measurable — and could you count it by eye?

Performance expectations
  1. Describe the path of each half-beam through a Michelson interferometer.
  2. Explain why moving a mirror by λ/2 shifts the pattern by a whole fringe.
  3. Compute a mirror displacement from a fringe count, and a fringe count from a displacement.
  4. Apply the same fringe-counting idea to a refractive index measurement with a gas cell.

Michelson interferometer

Figure 1.  The beam is split in two, the halves travel down separate arms, and they are recombined.  The compensator plate puts the same thickness of glass in both arms so that the only difference left is the one you control.  Move M1 and the path difference changes — by twice the distance you moved it, because the light goes down the arm and back.

The two half-beams travel 2d1 and 2d2 — down each arm and back — so the path difference is

Δd = 2d1 − 2d2
  • Δd = mλ0 → constructive
  • Δd = (m + ½)λ0 → destructive
Figure 2.  The fringes are circles, and they are not evenly spaced — the bright rings sit at radii proportional to √m, so they bunch up toward the edge.  Pick one point, mark it, and count rings as they sweep past.

When M1 is moved a distance Δd = λ0/2, the path difference changes by λ0 — the factor of two from the round trip — and each fringe moves to the position previously occupied by an adjacent fringe.

The consequence By counting the number of fringes m passing a single point,
we can measure minute displacement accurate to a fraction of a wavelength

Worked example 1 — how far did the mirror move?

λ = 630 nm.  M1 is moved and a single fringe shifts.  How much did the mirror move?

One fringe, so m = 1 and the path difference changed by one whole wavelength. The mirror moves half that, because the light makes the trip twice: Δd = (1)λ0/2 = 630 nm / 2 = 315 nm. Back to the prediction: you do not need to see 315 nm.  You need to see one ring move over by one, which is centimetres of movement on the screen.  That is the whole trick of the instrument — it converts a distance you cannot resolve into one you can.

Worked example 2 — the refractive index of a gas

A cell of length t = 2 cm is placed in one arm and slowly filled with gas.  With λ = 632.8 nm, 122 fringes shift as it fills.  What is the refractive index of the gas?

  1. Nothing moved.  So what changed the path difference?  Answer that first and the algebra is two lines.
The light crosses the cell twice, so the optical path through it is 2tn with gas and 2t with vacuum.  The change is the difference: 2tn − 2t = mλ  →   2t(n − 1) = mλ n − 1 = mλ / 2t  →   n = 1 + mλ / 2t n = 1 + 122(632.8 × 10−9) / [2(2 × 10−2)] = 1.00193 A refractive index that differs from vacuum in the third decimal place, measured by counting rings in an eyepiece.  No other technique in this module comes close to that.
The punchline Move the mirror λ0/2 → one fringe passes

Everything else about this instrument follows from the factor of two.  A displacement too small to see becomes a count you can make by eye, and the same count — with nothing moving at all — weighs the optical thickness of a gas.
