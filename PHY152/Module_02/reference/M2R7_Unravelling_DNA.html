Unravelling DNA: how physics helped solve the double helix

Web version of the public lecture by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

What this page is This one is not a lesson — it is a talk, and it is kept whole because it is worth keeping whole. Trevor gave it as a public lecture, with his own apparatus and his own photographs, and it runs a single argument from the first slide to the last: the structure of DNA was read off a diffraction pattern, using exactly the physics in the rest of this module and nothing more. Read the other reference pages to learn the equations; read this one to see what they were once used for.
You already know this Diffraction and interference — that a wave bends round a barrier, and that two waves arriving out of step can cancel. The one equation this talk turns on, mλ = d sin θ, is derived here from scratch.

Two words, kept apart

Diffractionthe “bending” of a wave when it encounters a barrier.
Interferencethe “addition” of two or more waves.

Both happen at once in every experiment on this page. The narrow beam is spread out by diffraction — a 0.04 mm beam spreads to 10 cm or more — and the rays from each slit then interfere, adding together to make brighter and darker regions. The pattern you see is the product of the two.

The experiment

Trevor’s setup is a laser, a lens, a slide holding the pattern, and a wall. The camera is off to one side and the laser is just outside the frame. Everything in the talk after this point is a photograph of what landed on that wall.

Where mλ = d sin θ comes from

Constructive:  mλ = d sin θ       Destructive:  (m + ½)λ = d sin θ
morder of the spot
λwavelength of the wave
ddistance between the centres of the openings
θangle between the centre and the maximum spot

Read the equation backwards and you have the whole method of the talk. Measure the angles, know the wavelength, and you get d — the spacing of the thing that did the diffracting, even if it is far too small to see.

Many colours, many spacings

Shine white light through a grating and each wavelength leaves at its own angle, because θ depends on λ. Trevor’s photographs of a point source through crossed gratings show it directly — and his question to the audience is a good one to answer before reading on: which colour has the longest wavelength? The one bent furthest out, which is the red.

The same logic is why a spectrum is a measurement and not a decoration. Wavelengths are chemical signatures, and a grating is what separates them.

Down to the scale of atoms

Visible light has wavelengths of a few hundred nanometres, and that sets a floor: you cannot use it to measure a spacing much smaller than that. Atoms are about 0.1 nm apart.

X-rays used in labs have wavelengths of around one ångström: 1 Å = 10−10 m

That is the whole reason X-rays are the tool. To measure a spacing you need a wavelength comparable to it, and an ångström is the size of the gaps between atoms in a molecule. Shine X-rays at a crystal and the diffraction pattern is a direct readout of how its atoms are arranged.

The optical transform

The talk’s central trick is that you do not need X-rays to demonstrate the method. The International Chemical Education optical transform slide carries patterns of tiny marks — rows of dashes, slanted lines, crossed hatchings — printed at a scale where a red laser diffracts off them the way an X-ray diffracts off a molecule. Shine the laser through each one and the pattern on the wall tells you what the marks were, without ever looking at them.

Trevor works through the masks in order: single rows, double rows, slants, then the ones built from a helix. A helical arrangement of marks gives a diffraction pattern in the shape of an X — two arms crossing, with the layer lines along them — and the angle of that X and the spacing of the dots on it give you the pitch and the radius of the helix that made it.

Photo 51

Which is what Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling had. Their X-ray diffraction image of hydrated DNA fibres — the famous Photo 51 — is an X, with its layer lines, and the structure of DNA is read out of it the same way the optical transform patterns are read: a double helix, 2 nm across, with a 3.4 nm pitch and bases stacked 0.34 nm apart.

Humidity changes the pattern, and Trevor shows the pair side by side. The crystalline A form and the hydrated B form give visibly different images, and it was the B form — the wetter one — that showed the helix cleanly. Getting the sample condition right was part of the measurement, not a detail around it.

Franklin’s own photographs of the two forms are on the slides, alongside the laser patterns from the optical transform masks that reproduce them. Put side by side, they are the same picture.

In short

  • Diffraction spreads a wave out at a barrier; interference adds the spread waves back together. Every pattern here is both at once.
  • mλ = d sin θ connects an angle you can measure to a spacing you cannot see.
  • Because θ depends on λ, a grating separates colours — and wavelengths are chemical signatures.
  • To resolve a spacing you need a comparable wavelength, which is why atomic-scale structure needs X-rays at about one ångström.
  • A helix diffracts into an X. That one fact, plus a photograph, is how the structure of DNA was found.
