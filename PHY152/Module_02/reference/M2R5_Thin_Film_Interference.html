Thin-Film Interference

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea A soap bubble is colourless soapy water, and it comes out rainbow. The reason is that two rays reach your eye from every point of it — one off the front surface, one that went through the film and came back — and whether they add or cancel depends on the thickness, the wavelength, and one thing that catches almost everybody: whether each reflection flipped the wave over on its way.
You already know this That waves add, and that a path difference of a whole wavelength puts them back in step. The new ingredient is the phase change on reflection, and everything hard about this topic is bookkeeping around that one fact.

Two waves, added

Where the second ray comes from

Soap bubbles have a range of colours due to the interference of light with its own reflection. The thickness of the film varies a little as the liquid starts to pool at the bottom, which is why the colours move and band.

Three things decide the path difference, and you must account for all of them: (1) the thickness of the film, (2) the wavelength of the light in the film, and (3) any phase changes at the boundaries.

(1) Thickness

(2) The wavelength changes inside the film

The speed of light changes as a wave travels into a different medium, but the frequency does not. So the wavelength must:

n = c/v = c/(fλ)  ⇒  nλ = c/f
n1λ1 = n2λ2   ⇒   λ2 = n1λ1/n2

This is useful because we are usually given the wavelength in the first medium — the colour of the light in air — and the interference happens in the second.

(3) Phase changes at the interfaces

This is the part that decides the answer, and it is the part most often skipped.

n1 > n2 reflecting off a less dense medium: no phase change. No modification is needed.
n1 < n2 reflecting off a more dense medium: a phase change of 180°, which is essentially an extra ½λ of path difference.

Trevor demonstrates this with the free-barrier and fixed-barrier wave simulations at ophysics.com — a pulse on a string reflects upright off a free end and inverted off a fixed end, and the optical case is the same statement.

Putting it together

For a soap film in air — inverted at the top, not at the bottom — the path difference is 2t + ½λf, and the two conditions come out as:

Bright bands (constructive), k = 1, 2, 3:
2t + ½λf = kλf  ⇒  t = ½(k − ½) nairλair/nf
Dark bands (destructive), k = 1, 3, 5:
2t + ½λf = ½kλf  ⇒  t = ¼(k − 1) nairλair/nf
The dark condition includes t = 0.  Put k = 1 into the second result and the thickness is zero — which is why the very top of a draining soap film, where it has thinned almost to nothing, goes black just before it bursts.  That is the observation the whole topic is worth making for.

Multi-layer films

Stack a second film on top and nothing about the method changes — but the inversions have to be counted at every boundary, and the two half-wavelengths no longer cancel so tidily. Trevor's own note on the slide is the one to carry: the path difference only involves phase. One wavelength is “back in step”.

Where you have seen this

Trevor's own photograph on slide 3 shows two microscope slide covers pressed together, with bands of light and dark running across them and a faint rainbow where the gap varies. The same effect is on every anti-reflection-coated lens you own, on oil films on wet roads, and on the bubble itself.

In short

  • Bands of light and dark appear when light interferes with itself in a thin film.
  • You must account for the distance travelled and any phase changes due to reflection.
  • Reflecting off a more dense medium inverts the wave — worth an extra ½λ. Off a less dense medium, nothing happens.
  • Use the wavelength in the film: λf = nairλair/nf.
  • When the path difference is λ or a whole multiple, bright bands appear; at ½λ or odd multiples of it, dark bands.
