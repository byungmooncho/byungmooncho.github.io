The Lens Maker’s and Thin Lens Equations

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea Two equations, and they answer different questions. The lens maker’s equation is about the piece of glass: grind these two curves into this material and you get that focal length. The thin lens equation never mentions the glass at all — it takes the focal length as given and tells you where the image goes. Almost everything that goes wrong in between is a sign, and the signs are all in one table.
You already know this Snell’s law, and that light bends towards the normal going into a denser medium. A lens is just two refracting surfaces close together, and nothing in either equation is doing anything Snell’s law was not already doing.

A curved surface, and the circle behind it

A ray crossing a curved surface bends by Snell’s law exactly as it would at a flat one — the only new thing is that the normal points in a different direction at every point of the surface. Send in a bundle of parallel rays and they cross the axis at one place.

Radius of curvaturethe radius of the circle that describes the edge of a refracting surface.
Focal pointthe location where parallel beams of light converge. The image is most likely not at the focal point.

That last half-sentence is Trevor’s, and it is worth the emphasis he gives it. The focal point is where parallel light converges — light from infinitely far away. An object at a finite distance forms its image somewhere else.

The lens maker’s equation

In full, for a lens of thickness d made of material of index n2 sitting in a medium of index n1:

1/f = (n2/n1 − 1) [ 1/R1 − 1/R2 + (n2 − 1)d / (n2R1R2) ]
ffocal length
R1radius of curvature, lens side 1
R2radius of curvature, lens side 2
n2index of refraction of the lens
n1index of refraction of the medium
dthickness of the lens

For a thin lens in air — n1 ≈ 1 and d ≪ R — the whole last term goes away and what is left is the form you will actually use:

1/f ≈ (n − 1) [ 1/R1 − 1/R2 ]

The thickness dropped out. Two lenses ground from the same glass to the same curves have the same focal length however fat they are through the middle — as long as both are thin enough for d ≪ R to hold. Make one thick enough and the term comes back.

The thin lens equation

Now forget what the lens is made of. Given its focal length, where does the image go?

1/do + 1/di = 1/f       m = −di/do

Signs, which is where the marks go

Focal length+f converging (convex) · −f diverging (concave)
Object distance+do real object, on the side the light comes from · −do virtual object, usually handed over by another lens
Image distance+di real image, on the far side · −di virtual image, on the object’s own side
Magnification+m upright · −m inverted

In the lab: a position is not a distance

Trevor puts this warning on its own slide, and it is the single most common way the converging lens lab goes wrong. Everything on the optical bench is read off a metre stick, so every number you write down is a position. The equation wants distances.

And the focal length is not where the image is.  Those two coincide only when the object is very, very far away — which is exactly the parallel-light case that defines the focal point in the first place.

Solving it for the thing you want

The equation is almost always handed to you in the form that does not have your unknown on its own. Rearranging it once, carefully, is worth more than rearranging it badly five times:

1/di = 1/f − 1/do
1/di = do/(f do) − f/(f do) = (do − f)/(f do)
di = f do / (do − f)
Don’t forget to flip it over.  Stopping one line early leaves you holding 1/di and calling it di. Trevor flags this one on the slide, which is usually a sign he has watched a room full of people do it.

A worked case: the object inside the focal length

A converging lens of focal length 2.00 cm has an object 1.23 cm in front of it — closer than the focal point. Where is the image, and what does it look like?

di = f do/(do − f) = (2.00)(1.23)/(1.23 − 2.00) = 2.46/(−0.77) = −3.19 cm
m = −di/do = −(−3.19)/1.23 = +2.60

Negative di, so the image is virtual and on the same side as the object. Positive m, so it is upright, and |m| > 1, so it is magnified. That is a magnifying glass, and this is the only arrangement of a single converging lens that behaves like one.

One number on the slides is a slip, and it is fixed here.  The Thin Lens Equation deck works these same values and writes di = 2.46/−0.77 = −3.48 cm and m = +2.83. The Ray Tracing deck works them again and gets −3.1948 cm and +2.597. Since 2.46/0.77 = 3.1948, the later deck is the correct one and this page carries its values throughout — including in Figure 5, which is drawn to scale and would not close up otherwise.

In short

  • The lens maker’s equation turns two radii and an index into a focal length; for a thin lens in air the thickness term vanishes.
  • The radius of curvature is the radius of the circle the surface is a piece of, and the focal point is where parallel light converges — not where the image is.
  • The thin lens equation takes f as given and places the image; the magnification comes from the same two distances.
  • Every sign in the problem is in one table, and the one that catches people is a converging lens producing a negative image distance when the object is inside the focal length.
  • In the lab, a position on the bench is not a distance. Subtract.
