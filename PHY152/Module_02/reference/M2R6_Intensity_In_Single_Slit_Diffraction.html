Intensity in Single-Slit Diffraction

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea Knowing where the dark fringes fall is one question; knowing how bright the pattern is at some arbitrary angle is another, and it needs a different equation. The derivation is a bit involved, but it boils down to three lines and one quantity, β, which is half the phase difference across the slit. The whole of the difficulty in using it is keeping track of whether that angle is in degrees or radians.
You already know this The single-slit minima condition, a sin θ = mλ. That equation locates the dark fringes and says nothing about intensity; this page is what fills the gap between them.

The pattern

Destructive interference (the dark fringes):   a sin θ = mλ
awidth of the opening, in metres
θangle from the centre, in degrees
morder number, unitless
λwavelength, in metres

Where the intensity equation comes from

φ = (2π/λ) a sin θ
β = φ/2 = πa sin θ/λ
I = I0 (sin β / β)2
Iintensity at angle θ, W/m2
I0intensity at 0°, W/m2
φphase difference, in radians
βhalf the phase difference, in radians
The tricky part of this set of equations is that the location angle θ is measured in degrees, while φ and β come out in radians.  One expression, two angle modes.

Worked example

  1. Coherent red light of wavelength 630 nm is aimed at a single slit of width 0.400 mm.  What is the ratio of intensity I/I0 at an angle of 5.00 degrees?
Step 1 — find β, with the calculator in DEGREE mode, because sin θ needs it:
β = πa sin θ/λ = π(4.00 × 10−4 m)(sin 5.00°) / (6.30 × 10−7 m) = 173.846 rad
This is in radians, since m/m is a pure number and the quantity is a phase.
Step 2 — find the intensity ratio, with the calculator in RADIAN mode:
sin(173.846 rad) = -0.871696
I/I0 = (-0.871696 / 173.846)2 = 2.51e-05 ≈ 2.51 × 10−5
This page does not carry the number printed on the slide, and here is why.
Trevor’s slide finishes at I/I0 = 3.8022 × 10−7. That is the value you get if the sine is taken in degree mode: sin(173.846°) = +0.1072, against sin(173.846 rad) = −0.8717. The two answers differ by a factor of about 66.
What makes this worth flagging rather than quietly fixing is the note he writes immediately underneath it: “some calculators may re-evaluate β when you switch modes. It may be a good idea to write down the value with several extra decimal places, then type it in manually after you change modes.” He names the trap on the slide and then falls into it. Every number above has been recomputed; the build asserts that it reproduces β = 173.846 exactly, and that the degree-mode route reproduces his printed 3.8022 × 10−7, which is how the cause was identified.

Sanity check it against Figure 1. At β = 173.8 rad we are about 55 orders out from the centre, far down the tail, where the envelope has fallen roughly as 1/β2. An intensity around 10−5 of the centre is what that tail gives; 10−7 would be two orders too faint.

In short

  • The diffraction equation a sin θ = mλ determines the location of local minima, not maxima.
  • The intensity at any angle is I = I0(sin β/β)2.
  • β = πa sin θ/λ, and it is in radians.
  • Be careful of radian versus degree mode. It is the single most common way to get this calculation wrong, and the error is large enough to be invisible as a “small slip”.
