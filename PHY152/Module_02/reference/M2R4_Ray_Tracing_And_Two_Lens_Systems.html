Ray Tracing and Two-Lens Systems

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea Three rays behave so simply that you can draw them without calculating anything, and where they cross is where the image is. That is the whole of ray tracing. Once you can do it for one lens you can do it for two, because the second lens does not care where its object came from — the image made by the first is the object for the second, and nothing else about the method changes.
You already know this The thin lens equation and the sign conventions. Ray tracing and the equation are two ways of saying the same thing, and a construction that disagrees with the arithmetic means one of them was done wrong.

The three “special” rays

Any ray leaving the object is bent by the lens, but three of them are bent in ways you can state in a sentence, which is what makes them drawable:

P-raystravelling parallel to the principal axis — they all go through the focal point on the other side of the lens.
M-raystravelling through the centre (middle) of the lens — they are not deflected.
F-raystravelling through the focal point before the lens — they exit parallel to the principal axis on the other side.
Where the bending actually happens.  The refraction occurs at the two surfaces, where the glass meets the air. We draw it as a single kink at the centre plane of the lens because that is a very good approximation for a thin lens, and because drawing both surfaces makes the picture unreadable.

Tracing one lens

Two rays are enough to fix the image — two lines cross at one point. The third is worth drawing anyway as a check: if it does not arrive at the same place, something is wrong, and it is usually a focal point on the wrong side.

For the lab:  use the template Trevor provides, a ruler, and a few different coloured pencils. One colour per ray is the difference between a diagram you can read afterwards and a tangle.

When the rays refuse to cross

Move the object inside the focal length and the three rules still hold, but the refracted rays come out diverging. They never meet, so there is no real image — but their backward extensions do meet, and that is where the eye sees the image:

di = f do/(do − f) = (2.00)(1.23)/(1.23 − 2.00) = −3.19 cm      m = −di/do = +2.60

Negative di, so virtual and on the object’s own side; positive m, so upright; |m| > 1, so magnified. The construction and the equation agree, which is the point of doing both.

Two lenses: the image of the first is the object of the second

Many optical instruments use more than one lens, and the strategy is to treat the system as a series of single lenses rather than as something new. The image from the first lens becomes the object for the second lens. Solve lens 1 completely, hand its image over, and solve lens 2 the way you would solve any single lens.

do2 = L − di1       mt = m1 × m2

The total magnification is the product, not the sum. Two inversions undo each other; a doubling followed by a halving leaves the size alone.

His worked example

A converging lens has a focal length of 8.0 cm. An object is 4.0 cm to the left of it. A second converging lens of the same focal length sits 12.0 cm to the right. Relative to the second lens, where is the final image, and what is the total magnification?

Lens 1.  di1 = (8.0)(4.0)/(4.0 − 8.0) = 32/(−4.0) = −8.0 cm     m1 = −(−8.0)/4.0 = +2.0
Hand it over.  do2 = L − di1 = 12.0 − (−8.0) = +20.0 cm
Lens 2.  di2 = (8.0)(20.0)/(20.0 − 8.0) = 160/12 = 13.3 cm     m2 = −13.3/20.0 = −0.665
The system.  mt = (2.0)(−0.665) = −1.33

Watch the sign of do2. Here lens 1 made a virtual image, 8 cm on its own left, so that image is 20 cm in front of lens 2 and do2 is comfortably positive. Had lens 1 formed its image past lens 2, do2 would have come out negative — a virtual object — and the equation would have handled it with no special case at all.

In short

  • Three rays are drawable without calculating anything: parallel in leaves through the far focus, through the middle carries straight on, through the near focus leaves parallel.
  • Where they cross is the image. Two fix it; the third is the check.
  • If they diverge instead of crossing, extend them backwards — the image is virtual, upright and on the object’s own side.
  • For two lenses, solve them one at a time: the image of the first is the object of the second, with do2 = L − di1.
  • Magnifications multiply.
