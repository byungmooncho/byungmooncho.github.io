The Nature of Light and the Law of Reflection

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea Nobody can quite say what light is — that argument runs into quantum mechanics and does not come out. What can be said is how it behaves, and for all of geometric optics two facts are enough: it travels in straight lines within a medium, and it slows down in matter by a factor we call the index of refraction. The law of reflection falls straight out of the second one, and everything in this module is built on top of it.
You already know this Waves — wavelength, frequency, speed and v = fλ. Light is a wave, and the only genuinely new thing here is that it needs no medium to travel through.

What light is, as far as anyone can say

The clues came first from electricity and magnetism — Faraday's experiments and the idea of fields permeating and propagating through space, made rigorous by Maxwell. Light turned out to be a solution of Maxwell's equations: an electromagnetic wave travelling at a fixed speed.

c = 1/√(ε0μ0) = 3 × 108 m/s

That number is the whole argument, and the order it happened in matters. Light speed was measured before anyone thought light was an electromagnetic wave. The electromagnetic nature of light was inferred from the fact that 1/√(ε0μ0) — a combination of two constants from electricity and magnetism, with nothing to do with optics — happened to match the then-known value of the speed of light.

Counter-intuitively, such disturbances are self-sustaining and require no medium. Light propagates through vacuum at c, the maximal speed.

Wave fronts, rays, and how little of it reaches you

Light in matter: the index of refraction

Light can also travel through transparent media such as water or glass. At what speed, compared with c, will light travel in a transparent medium?

v = c/n       n ≥ 1
n ≡ c/v = (speed of light in vacuum) / (speed of light in the material)

Since n is never less than 1, light in matter is always slower than light in vacuum. Vacuum is the case n = 1, and nothing beats it.

The law of reflection

Light bounces off a shiny surface, and the rule is as simple as a rule gets:

θi = θr
In optics we measure every angle from the normal — the line perpendicular to the surface — not from the surface itself. A ray making 30° with the surface makes 60° with the normal, and reading that wrong costs the whole question. The habit starts here and never stops mattering: it is still the convention when the surface stops being flat and starts being a lens.

Images in a plane mirror

Solving problems with plane mirrors means setting up the angles and then applying some geometry. The toolkit is small and entirely from high school:

Parallel lines and transversalsalternate interior angles, alternate exterior angles.

Be sure you know which way the mirror is facing.  More plane-mirror problems are lost to a mirror drawn facing the wrong way than to any geometry.

Corner reflectors

In short

  • Light is an electromagnetic wave, self-sustaining and needing no medium, travelling through vacuum at c — the fastest anything goes.
  • Rays of light travel in straight lines within a particular medium.
  • The index of refraction is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to the speed in the medium, and it is never less than 1.
  • The law of reflection: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
  • Measure every optical angle from the surface normal.
  • A plane mirror puts the image as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.
