Snell’s Law and Total Internal Reflection

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea A ray crossing from one medium into another bends, and the amount it bends by is fixed entirely by the two indices of refraction. That one relation — Snell’s law — is the whole of refraction, and it is derived here from nothing more than two right triangles standing on the same hypotenuse. Run it the other way, from dense to thin, and it eventually asks for a sine greater than one; what the light does instead is the second half of this page.
You already know this That light slows in matter by the factor n, and that wave fronts and rays are perpendicular to each other. Both are on the Nature of Light page, and Snell’s law is just those two facts applied at a boundary.

Why a ray bends at all

Nothing bends the ray directly. What happens is that one edge of the wavefront enters the slow medium before the other edge does, so that edge falls behind, and the front pivots. The ray — perpendicular to the front — pivots with it.

The ray diagram

n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2

Where Snell’s law comes from

This is Trevor’s derivation, and it is worth following once because it explains the shape of the result rather than just asserting it. Start with the two wavelengths and one shared length:

sin θi = λi/L  ⇒  L = λi/sin θi       sin θr = λr/L  ⇒  L = λr/sin θr
It is the same L, so   λi/sin θi = λr/sin θr
and since v = fλ with f the same in both media, λ = c/(f n):
(c/f ni)/sin θi = (c/f nr)/sin θr
∴  ni sin θi = nr sin θr

The frequency is the one quantity that cannot change at the boundary — the far side is driven by the near side, cycle for cycle — so c and f cancel out of both sides and only the indices survive. That is why Snell’s law mentions no speeds and no wavelengths.

Total internal reflection

Run it the other way. Rearranged, Snell’s law says

sin θr = (ni/nr) sin θi

For ni < nr the factor is less than one and there is always an answer. But for ni > nr the factor exceeds one, and past some incident angle the right-hand side is greater than 1. That is a problem, because sin φ only ever lies between −1 and 1 — so sin−1 has no answer to give.

What the light does instead is reflect. All of it. When the entire wave is reflected back into the medium it came from, that is total internal reflection.

The boundary case is the one worth memorising. Set the refracted ray grazing along the surface, θr = 90°, and solve:

ni sin θc = nr sin 90° = nr
θc = sin−1(nr/ni)
Which way is the ray going?  We usually draw this with the ray coming up from below, because the index of air is lower than the index of water or glass — so total internal reflection is what happens looking out of the denser medium, not into it. Get the two indices the wrong way round and the formula hands you a sine bigger than one for every angle.

In short

  • A ray bends at an interface because one edge of the wavefront slows before the other.
  • Snell’s law: n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2, every angle measured from the normal.
  • It follows from two right triangles on the same hypotenuse, plus the fact that the frequency is unchanged across the boundary.
  • Going into a denser medium the ray bends towards the normal; coming out, away from it. Some light reflects at the interface either way.
  • From dense to thin there is a critical angle, θc = sin−1(nr/ni), beyond which none of the light gets out at all.
