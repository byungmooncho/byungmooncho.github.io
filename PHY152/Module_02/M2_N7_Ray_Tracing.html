Ray Tracing

SynopsisNote 6 found the image by arithmetic.  This note finds it with a straightedge.  Three rules — one for a ray parallel to the axis, one for a ray through the middle, one for a ray through the focus — are enough to locate any image a thin lens can make, and to see at a glance whether it is real or virtual.
Before you start — predict

You trace two rays carefully and they spread apart after the lens instead of crossing.  Have you made a mistake, or has the lens told you something?  Decide now.

Performance expectations
  1. State all three ray rules without looking them up.
  2. Construct an image by tracing at least two rays from the tip of the object.
  3. Distinguish a real image from a virtual one by whether the refracted rays themselves cross, or only their backward extensions do.
  4. Check a construction against 1/do + 1/di = 1/f, and say what it means when they disagree.

3.  Ray tracing

Two names for the same line, and both are used: the optic axis and the principal axis.

Figure 1.  Three rules, and that is the whole of ray tracing.  Each one says what a lens does to one particular ray — and every one of the three is reversible, which is why you may trace in either direction.
  • Rays parallel to the principal axis ⇒ go through the focus on the other side of the lens.
  • Rays going through the centre of the lens ⇒ nothing happens; they keep going without bending.
  • Rays travelling through the focus before the lens ⇒ come out parallel to the optic axis on the other side.

Any two of the three locate the image.  The third is the check — if it does not arrive at the same point, something in the construction is wrong, and it is nearly always the focal point that has been put on the wrong side.

Worked example — trace it with no numbers at all

  1. An object stands outside the focal length of a converging lens.  Using nothing but the three rules above — no distances, no equation — locate its image, and say whether it is real or virtual, upright or inverted, before you measure anything.
Draw the P-ray.  From the tip of the object, parallel to the axis.  At the lens it bends and goes through the focus on the other side of the lens, and it keeps going. Draw the M-ray.  From the same tip, straight at the centre of the lens.  Nothing happens — it keeps going without bending. Read off where they cross.  The P-ray is heading down across the axis and the M-ray is heading down too but less steeply, so the two meet on the far side of the lens, below the axis.  The light itself arrives there, so the image is real, and it is below the axis, so it is inverted. Check with the third rule.  The F-ray leaves the tip through the near focus and comes out parallel to the axis.  It arrives at the same point.  If it does not, the focal point has been put on the wrong side — that is nearly always the mistake. No arithmetic was used anywhere above.  The construction and the equation are the same statement: the point where the rays cross is the solution of 1/do + 1/di = 1/f.

When the rays refuse to cross

Move the object inside the focal length and the construction changes character.  The refracted rays still obey all three rules, but they diverge.

Figure 2.  Object inside the focal length.  The two refracted rays never cross, so they are traced backwards (dashed) until they do.  That meeting point is the virtual image — upright, magnified, and on the same side as the object.  The arithmetic agrees: di = −225 px and m = +2.5.

Trace them backwards and they meet.  That meeting point is a virtual image: no light actually passes through it, which is why you cannot catch it on a screen — but your eye, which only ever sees the directions rays arrive from, sees an object standing there.

The punchline Parallel → through F  ·  through the middle → straight on  ·  through F → parallel

Back to the prediction: rays that spread apart are not a mistake.  They are the lens telling you the image is virtual — the same message the equation sends as a negative di.
