Total Internal Reflection

SynopsisRun the light the other way — out of glass into air instead of in — and Snell’s law gives a refracted angle that is bigger than the one you started with.  Push far enough and it reaches 90°.  Past that there is no refracted ray at all, and the surface behaves like a perfect mirror.
Before you start — predict

Light inside glass meets the surface with air above it.  As you tilt the ray further from the normal, the refracted ray in the air tilts faster.  What happens when the refracted ray reaches 90°, and what can possibly happen after that?  Write down a guess before you read on.

5.  Total internal reflection

Figure 1.  Drawn to scale.  The blue ray leaves at θi = 30° and refracts to 48.6° — bending away from the normal, because it is going into the less dense medium.  Push θi up and θr runs ahead of it until it hits 90°.  The angle at which that happens is θc, and that is the whole of the critical angle.
  1. Find the angle θc at which the refracted ray just grazes the surface, in terms of ni and nr.  Then put the numbers in for glass and air.
Start from Snell’s law:   ni sin θi = nr sin θr “Grazing the surface” means θr = 90°, and sin 90° = 1:    ni sin θc = nr sin 90° = nr    sin θc = nr/ni = nair/nglass For glass and air: sin θc = 1.00/1.50 = 0.667, so θc = 41.8°. Note what the equation requires: sin θc cannot exceed 1, so there is only a critical angle when nr < ni.  Dense to less dense, or it does not happen at all.
The punchline sin θc = nr/ni

Below θc some light leaves and some reflects.  At θc the refracted ray lies flat along the surface.  Above it, none of the light gets out — it is all reflected back inside, which is why the equation is the whole working principle of an optical fibre.

Why light bends at all: the principle of least time

Figure 2 — the principle of least time.  Running is fast, swimming is slow, so the quickest route is not the straight line and not the one with the least water in it, but a bent one — more sand, less sea.  Light does the same thing at a boundary, and the angle it chooses is the one Snell’s law gives.
