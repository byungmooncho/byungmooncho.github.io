Circular Apertures and Resolution

SynopsisEvery lens, mirror and pupil is a hole, and a hole diffracts.  So no instrument ever makes a point image of a point object — it makes a small blur.  When two objects’ blurs overlap, the instrument cannot tell them apart, and that limit is set by physics, not by workmanship.
Before you start — predict

You are told a telescope mirror has been ground and polished perfectly — no flaw anywhere.  Can it now resolve two stars that are arbitrarily close together?  Answer yes or no, and say what decides it.

Circular aperture and resolution

If there were no diffraction, light through a round hole would throw a shadow with a sharp edge — a clean disc, not “fuzzy”.  It does not.  The edge is blurred, and the blurring is diffraction at the rim of the aperture.

Now put two point sources in front of that aperture.  Each makes its own blur.  If the blurs are far apart you see two objects; if they overlap enough, you see one.

Figure 1.  Each curve is a real Airy pattern — (2J1(x)/x)2 — and the pale curves are the two sources taken separately.  On the left they sit exactly one first-zero apart, and the dip in their sum is the actual dip.  Push them closer and it fills in.

The dividing line is the angle at which the peak of one pattern falls on the first dark ring of the other.  For a circular aperture that angle is

θm = 1.22 λ / D

where D is the diameter of the aperture.  θm is the angle for just about resolving the two.  Closer than that and they are not resolved — no amount of magnification recovers them, because the information is not in the light any more.

Worked example — the Hubble Space Telescope

(1)  The Hubble mirror has D = 2.4 m.  At λ = 550 nm, what is θm?

θm = 1.22λ/D = 1.22(550 × 10−9) / 2.4 m = 2.8 × 10−7 rad. In degrees, using 360° = 2π so that π = 3.14 rad = 180°: 1.6 × 10−5 degrees.

(2)  Two stars sit in a galaxy 2 million light years away.  How far apart must they be for Hubble to see them as two?

Figure 2.  Once you have the smallest angle a telescope can resolve, the smallest separation it can resolve at a known distance is one line of geometry.  The answer scales with how far away the objects are, which is why resolving a pair of stars in another galaxy is a far harder problem than resolving two headlights.
  1. You have an angle and a distance.  The rest is one line — but check what a light year is before you write it.
s = rθ = (2 × 106 ly)(2.8 × 10−7) = 0.56 light years. A light year is the distance light travels in one year, so converting: s = (0.56)(3 × 108 m/s × 365 × 24 × 60 × 60) = 5.3 × 1015 m. For comparison, the Earth–Sun distance is 149 × 106 km = 149 × 109 m.  So two stars in that galaxy must be about thirty-five thousand times further apart than the Earth is from the Sun before Hubble can split them.
The punchline θm = 1.22 λ / D

Back to the prediction: no.  A perfect mirror still cannot resolve two arbitrarily close stars, because the limit is set by the wavelength of the light and the size of the hole, not by the quality of the glass.  To do better you need a bigger aperture or a shorter wavelength — which is exactly why telescopes keep getting larger.
