Interference

SynopsisSend light through two narrow slits and the screen does not show two bright patches.  It shows a row of bright and dark bands — including dark bands where light from both slits arrives.  No amount of ray tracing accounts for that.  This note is the geometry that does, and it comes down to one length: the difference in how far the two waves have travelled.
Before you start — predict

Light arrives at a point on the screen from both slits.  Twice the light.  Under what circumstance could that point be completely dark?  Answer in one sentence before you read on.

Performance expectations
  1. Distinguish constructive from destructive interference by the phase relationship and by the path difference.
  2. Derive Δl = d sin θ from the geometry of two distant parallel rays.
  3. Apply d sin θ = mλ and d sin θ = (m + ½)λ to locate bright and dark fringes.
  4. Use the small-angle result ym = mλD/d, and say when it is allowed.

1.  Interference

Figure 1.  Two waves arriving in phase.  The resultant is drawn from the sum of the two above it, so its amplitude really is 2X — nothing about the picture was adjusted to make that come out.
Figure 2.  The same two waves, one shifted by half a wavelength.  The resultant is again the sum, and it is flat: the light has gone.  That is the thing a ray picture cannot do, and it is why this note exists.

So the condition is a statement about how far apart the two waves are when they meet:

  • Constructive — the waves are mλ apart, m = 0, ±1, ±2, ±3…
  • Destructive — the waves are (m + ½)λ apart, m = 0, ±1, ±2, ±3…

Two slits

Figure 3.  Why Δl = d sin θ.  The screen is so far away compared with d that the two rays heading for the same point are effectively parallel.  Drop a perpendicular from S1 onto the other ray and the bit left over — drawn in red — is the extra distance the bottom ray travels.  It is the opposite side of a right triangle with hypotenuse d, so it is d sin θ exactly.

Δl is the difference in path lengths between the top and bottom rays.  Feed it into the two conditions above and the whole of two-slit interference falls out:

The two conditions d sin θ = mλ  (constructive)
d sin θ = (m + ½)λ  (destructive)

m = 0, ±1, ±2, ±3…

m is the order of the interference.  m = 0 is the zeroth order — the central bright fringe; m = ±1 is first order, m = ±2 second order, and so on outward in both directions.

Where the fringe lands on the screen

A bright fringe is at d sin θ = mλ.  For small angles sin θ ≈ θ ≈ tan θ, and tan θ = y/D, so

d sin θ = mλ →  d (ym/D) = mλ ym = mλD / d

which says the bright fringes are evenly spaced on the screen — as long as the angle stays small enough for the approximation to hold.

Worked example

A HeNe laser shines on a double slit with d = 0.01 mm.  The third bright fringe appears at θ3 = 10.95°.  What is the wavelength of the laser?

  1. Work it before opening the solution.  Two decisions settle it: which of the two conditions applies, and what m is.
A bright fringe, so d sin θ = mλ.  The third one, so m = 3. λ = d sin θ / m = (0.01 × 10−3 m)(sin 10.95°) / 3 = (1.0 × 10−5)(0.1900) / 3 = 633 nm. Which is exactly the red line of a helium–neon laser — so the measurement has identified the source, not merely described it.  This is how wavelengths are actually measured: an angle and a ruler.
The punchline Δl = d sin θ, and everything follows from it

Back to the prediction: the point is dark when the two waves arrive half a wavelength out of step — light plus light giving no light.  A ray cannot be half a wavelength out of step with another ray, because a ray has no wavelength.  This is the first result in the module that requires light to be a wave.

Looking ahead

Two slits give a row of evenly spaced fringes.  Note 11 closes one of the slits — and finds that a single slit still produces a pattern of bright and dark bands, for a reason that turns the rule of this note upside down.
