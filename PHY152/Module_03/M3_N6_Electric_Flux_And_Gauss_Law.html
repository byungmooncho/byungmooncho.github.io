Electric Flux and Gauss’s Law

Count the lines through a closed surface, and the charge inside tells you how many there are

SynopsisBack in Note 3 a phrase went past almost in passing — flux is line density.  This note collects on it.  Draw any closed surface you like around some charge, count the field lines crossing out of it, and that count depends on nothing but the charge inside — not the shape of the surface, not its size, not where the charge sits within it.  That is Gauss’s law, and where a problem has enough symmetry it hands you the field in two lines of algebra where the direct integral takes two pages.  We will do it the hard way first, so that you can see what is being saved.

1.  Electric Flux

Flux is the simplest idea in this note and it is worth stating in his own shorthand before any integral signs appear:

ΦE = {electric field} × {area}

But which area, and how much of the field?  Take the same patch of area twice and put it in two different places:

Figure 1.  The same 2 m² patch in two orientations, and more flux through the left one — because more lines go through it.  This is the picture behind the line he wrote in Note 3 and left hanging: flux is line density.

Now close the surface up, so it wraps the charge entirely.  Two things can happen, and he labels both:

Figure 2.  Close the surface up so it wraps the charge entirely, and two things can happen.  Lines out: flux positive.  Lines in: flux negative.  The sign of the flux is the sign of the charge, and nothing else.

“Closed” is doing real work in that sentence, and his page tests it before going on:

Figure 3.  “Closed” is doing real work in that sentence.  The first two surfaces have an edge you could walk off; only the third separates an inside from an outside.  Gauss’s law has nothing to say about the first two.

The one subtlety is that a field skimming along a surface carries nothing through it.  Only the part along the outward normal counts, which is what the dot product is doing in every formula below:

Figure 4.  Only the part of E along the outward normal n̂ carries anything through the patch — a field lying flat in the surface carries no flux at all.

Put those together and you have the law, in the two forms he writes it:

ε0Φ = qenclosed   for a closed surface!!

Φ = ∮S E · n̂ dA = ∮S E · dA = qenc / ε0

What we are interested in, in his wordsΦ = ∮S E · dA = qenc / ε0

He boxes this one and writes “what we are interested!!” beside it, with “Gauss’ Law” underneath.  Everything else in the note is this equation being used.

2.  Solving the Electric Field with Gauss’s Law

Before trusting it on anything hard, check it on something whose answer is already known.  His note at the top of this page: “We know this is solution.”

E = (1 / 4πε0) (1 / r2)  r̂

Figure 5.  The whole content of the law in one picture.  Nine charges, three of them inside, and only those three appear in the flux.  The other six still make a field everywhere on the surface — what they do not do is change the total through it.
Figure 6.  A point charge at the centre of a spherical surface.  Two things make this work, and they are the two to look for in every problem below: n̂ = r̂ everywhere, so the dot product is just a product; and E is the same size everywhere on the surface, so it comes out of the integral.

His heading for what follows is “Demonstrate Gauss Law”:

dΦ = E · n̂ dA= (1 / 4πε0) (q / R2)  r̂ · r̂ dA= (1 / 4πε0) (q / R2)  dA

Φ = ∮S dΦ = (1 / 4πε0) (q / R2) ∮S dA= (1 / 4πε0) (q / R2) (4πr2)Φ = q / ε0   for r = R

The ∮dA is just the area of the sphere — his own note beside it — and the R2 underneath cancels against it.  That cancellation is the whole trick, and it is why spheres are the easy case.

He then works the law on a set of cases where nothing has to be integrated at all — the whole job is deciding which charges are inside:

Figure 7.  Five surfaces, and the whole job is deciding which charges are inside.  (e) is the one worth pausing on: +4, +6 and −10 all sit inside, and they cancel.

Where this is goingε0Φ = qenclosed
