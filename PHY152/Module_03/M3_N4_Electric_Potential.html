Electric Potential Is The Energy Of a Unit Positive Charge In An E-Field

E-Field PhET Simulation

SynopsisWe explored the electric field in the last PhET simulation lab.  You should recognize that a field is a cue for force.  Specifically, the field lines tell you how an object — a mass in a gravitational field, a charge in an electric field — will accelerate, because it knows the force acting on it.  This note is about the other way of looking at the same situation.

Two world’s views

Remember in mechanics that physicists have two “world’s views”?  One is the force perspective.  The other is the energy perspective.  We can analyze the behavior of an object using either, or both.  However, I told you that they prefer the energy perspective if they have the choice, because the math is simpler: force is a vector, requiring three equations for three dimensions, whereas energy is a scalar, so it is just one equation for an object.

So now, let’s talk about the energy of a charge in a field!  But first, here is a reminder about work (energy), W (J): W = Fd, where F is the force (N) and d (m) is the distance over which the force is applied.  So work done in a gravitational field is WG = Fgd = mgh, where Fg = mg, m is the mass (kg), g is the acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²), and h is the distance over which the gravity force held sway.

A new term — potential

At this point, I need to introduce a new term, potential.  This is not a new concept!  Just like the field is a force-like quantity (when you see “field,” think “force”), the potential is an energy-like quantity, so when you see potential, you should think “energy.”

The field appears in the force equation: Fg = mg, where g is the gravitational field, and FE = qE, where E is the electrostatic field.  The potential appears in the energy equation.  So for gravity,

WG = Fgd = mgh = mUG

where WG is the gravitational potential energy (as opposed to just potential), and UG is the gravitational potential, which is just gh, the field multiplied by distance!  For electrostatic force,

WE = FEd = qEd = qVE

where WE is the electrostatic potential energy and VE is the electric potential, which is just Ed, the electric field multiplied by distance.  We will just drop the subscript E in VE and call this potential.

You are already very familiar with V: it has its own unit, the volt (V), and, yes, it’s the same as “voltage” volts as you see in batteries!  So you have been using the electrostatic potential to gauge your battery level — voltage goes down as batteries wear out, or as their energy content decreases over time.  It’s not the actual energy measured in J, but a very good indication of how much energy you have left in the battery.  Refer to your Reference Table for “V” on p. 4; it is denoted as “potential difference.”  This is completely equivalent to electrostatic potential or voltage.

How physicists define it

Finally, I want to describe how physicists define electric potential:

V = W/q

the same equation as WE = qVE, where I just drop the subscript E and rearrange for V.  In other words, the electrostatic potential, or voltage, is the energy per charge — the energy in each coulomb of charge (J/C, joules per coulomb).

Try the arithmetic on it

So if you have 2 V of potential and 0.5 C, you have 1 J.  If there is 4 J of energy in the charge in a field, and there is 2 C, then you have 2 V of potential.

We could do this with gravitation, too, but we just didn’t — perhaps there were no compelling reasons for us.  Why for electrostatics?  Here, it’s really useful for electricity!  In electricity, we have voltages and other parameters to describe electric circuits, and electrostatic potential or potential difference (completely equivalent to voltage, V) is a crucial quantity, rather than energies in joules or forces in newtons.  More on that soon.

For completeness, let me add the definition of the electric field: E = FE/q, which reads as force per charge, or force experienced by each coulomb of charge.

  2. Use the voltmeter to measure the electric potential along locations approximately equal distances from the charges.  What can you say, generally, about the value of the electric potential equal distance from the opposite charges?
  3. Draw the line of zero potential on your field map below.

Zero.  Anywhere equidistant from the two charges, the potential is zero — and that set of points is a straight line running midway between them.

Figure 2.  Equipotential “contour lines.”  Exactly like the contour lines on a map — every point on one of them is at the same potential, and the value is written on it.  They crowd together close to a charge, where the potential changes fast, and spread out as you go away.  The 0 V line is dead straight, exactly midway.  And look at the green arrow: the field crosses the equipotential at right angles, pointing from the + charge toward the − one — downhill, always.  The faint dashed curves are the field lines, and they cut every contour square.
  1. Grab the sensor and move it around to find a location or locations where the field is zero.  Can you find it?
  2. How do you explain the existence of zero electric potential where the electric field is not zero?

NO.  There is nowhere the field is zero.

And yet the potential is zero all along that middle line.  How can both be true?

Because fields add like vectors, while potentials add like numbers.  On that line the two field vectors point the same way rather than opposite ways, so they do not cancel — but the two potentials are a positive number and an equal negative number, and numbers like that always cancel.

So: field ≠ 0 there, potential = 0 there.  Nothing contradictory about it at all, once you remember which of the two is a vector.

  1. How does the value of the potential change with the distance from the charges?
  2. With the charges close enough to touch each other, place the voltmeter nearer the edge of the field (making it easier to see the pattern).  What happens to the voltmeter reading as you are at the edge of the field, and what does this reading imply about how the potentials from positive and negative charges add?

Decrease.  From the + charge it runs from a large positive value down toward zero; from the − charge, from a large negative value up toward zero.

At the edge, with the charges touching, the reading goes to zero.  They cancel.  Positive and negative potentials add like numbers, and like numbers they cancel.

Part III — potential in two like charges (two negative or two positive charges in close proximity)

  1. Place two like charges on the grid vertically aligned with a reasonable distance between them.  Use a voltmeter and find the locations of zero potential.  Can you find it?  Explain the result.
  2. Draw the electric field lines.  Grab a sensor and move it around.  Where is the location of the zero field?  Indicate it on the diagram.  Explain the result.

Zero potential: NO.  Two like charges both contribute potential of the same sign, and two numbers of the same sign can never add to zero.

Zero field: YES — between the two charges, at the midpoint, where the forces point in opposite directions and have the same magnitude, so they cancel (vectors).

The plot just below shows it: two same-sign curves never reach the axis.

Which is the exact mirror image of the dipole.  There: field never zero, potential zero on a line.  Here: potential never zero, field zero at a point.  Same reason both times — one of them is a vector and the other is not.

The same three cases, as graphs

Here is the potential plotted along the line that runs straight through the two charges.  V1 is the potential from charge 1 alone, V2 from charge 2 alone, and what the voltmeter reads is the two of them added — added as numbers, which is the whole point.

Figure 3.  Up means positive potential; the horizontal line is V = 0.  Each curve runs away to infinity at its own charge and decays toward zero far away.  Case 1: both curves sit entirely above the line.  Case 2: both entirely below.  Two numbers of the same sign cannot add to zero, so on neither of those plots can the total ever reach the line — which is exactly the answer to the question above.  Only Case 3, the dipole, crosses zero, and it crosses midway between the charges.
The thick black line is the one that matters: V1 + V2, which is what the voltmeter actually reads.  The thin curves are what each charge would do on its own.  In Cases 1 and 2 the thick line dips toward the axis between the charges and then pulls away again without ever reaching it — you can watch two same-sign numbers failing to cancel.  In Case 3 it goes straight through, at the ⊗.

Part IV — potential in two line charges of opposite signs

  1. Make two lines of charges, one consisting of positive and one negative.  Grab a sensor and map the electric field between the line charges and outside the line charges.  How does the field between the line charges differ from that outside them?
Figure 4.  Two line charges.  Between them the lines are straight, parallel and evenly spaced, running + to − — that is what a constant field looks like, and it is the one picture to carry into Module 4, where this geometry comes back as the capacitor.
Now follow a line past the rows.  The field above the + row and below the − row is not something separate: it is the same lines carrying on, up off the top, out round the ends, and back underneath.  Notice they point up in both places — away from the + row above it, toward the − row below it — and that they are sparse out there compared with the crowd between the rows, which is the field being weak.

Inside: constant E-field magnitude.  The field lines are straight lines pointing from + to −, and E = constant.

Outside: the E-field decreases with distance.

In his own words on the sheet: “Field constant between the line charges.”

  1. Use the voltmeter and map the potential between the line charges and outside the line charges.  How does the potential between the line charges differ from outside the line charges?

Inside: goes from +max at the + line charges, decreases to zero at mid-distance, and reaches −max at the − line charges.  A straight ramp, because the field is constant.

Outside: the + side decreases to 0 at infinite distance; the − side increases to 0 at infinite distance.

The punchline One is a vector.  The other is just a number.

Every answer in this note comes from that.  A dipole has no zero-field point but a whole line of zero potential.  Two like charges have no zero potential anywhere but a zero-field point dead in the middle.  You do not have to remember the four cases — you have to remember which quantity is a vector.
