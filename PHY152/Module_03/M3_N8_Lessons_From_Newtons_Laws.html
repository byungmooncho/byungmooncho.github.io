Electric Field and Electric Potential: Lessons From Newton’s Laws

SynopsisYou have heard me referring and drawing parallels to Newton’s equations to get a feel for the electric field, E, and electric potential, V.  The reasons are apparent.  Physics phenomena are pretty distinct between gravitation and electrostatic, for sure.  Still, the forms of equations are identical:  If you swap out the mass for the charge and G for k, then g turns into E, and Newton’s law of gravitation turns into Coulomb’s law.  Moreover, the weight Fg = mg in Newton’s equation becomes the attraction/repulse FE = qE in Coulomb’s equation!  Gravitational field strength (or simply gravitational field) g = Fg/m, the force in each kg of mass becomes electric field E = FE/q, the force in each C of charge.  Finally, the work in the gravitational field Wg = mgh = mUg turns to the work in the electrostatic field WE = qEd = qVE, where “gh = Ug” is analogous to “Ed = VE” which are known as gravitational potential, U and electrostatic potential V, respectively (I dropped the subscripts g and E).  The gravitational potential U = Wg/m is the energy in each kg of mass in a gravitational field, and the electrostatic potential V = WE/q is the energy in each C of charge in an electrostatic field.

The underlying reason for the isomorphism in equations is that both laws fundamentally seek to find the acceleration so that we can describe the motion, whether it’s a mass or charge.  A mass or charge will respond to its respective field, the mass in a gravitational field and the charge in an electrostatic field.  Remember, the fields are “spheres of influence” created by other masses or charges whose motions are not interesting to us; only the mass or the charge that feels/experiences the field (or influence and the mass or the charge will accelerate because of the generated field) are.

As we move into the electricity topic, we will use V, the electrostatic potential, better known as the potential difference or voltage, with the unit volt (V, yes, the uppercase v is the symbol for electrostatic potential/potential difference/voltage and the unit of voltage).  I summarize the equations in the table below.

Finally, let me introduce a convenient energy unit for electrostatics: electronvolt, eV.  The way you should think about the eV unit is (unit elementary charge) × (Voltage).  Remember, voltage is the electrostatic potential or the energy per charge, so 1 electron or proton traversing 1 volt of potential has 1 eV of energy.  This energy is equivalent to 1.6 × 10−19 J, i.e., numerically the same as the elementary charge (1.6 × 10−19 C).

One ladder, two names

Before the questions, here is the table drawn instead of written.  A 2 C charge sitting in a uniform 3 N/C field, beside a 2 kg mass sitting in a uniform 9.8 m/s² field.  Climb either ladder a metre at a time and read off the potential and the work.

Figure 1.  This is his own sheet, redrawn.  Each rung is one metre.  On the left the potential climbs V = Ed = 3 V per metre and the work climbs WE = qV; on the right it is U = gh = 9.8 m²/s² per metre (that is 9.8 J/kg) and WG = mU.  The 4 m rung on the electric side is blank because he left it blank — the gravitational side is filled in all the way, so you have a worked column to copy the pattern from.

12 V,  24 J

V = Ed = (3 N/C)(4 m) = 12 V, and WE = qV = (2 C)(12 V) = 24 J.

Which is the same arithmetic as the rung beside it: U = gh = (9.8 m/s²)(4 m) = 39.2 m²/s² and WG = mU = (2 kg)(39.2 m²/s²) = 78.4 J.
