Electric Force: Coulomb’s Law

The same shape as gravity, with a constant twenty orders of magnitude bigger

Synopsis

Like charges repel and opposite charges attract.  Repulsion and attraction are force descriptions.  Remember what a force — an “agent of change” — does: when we say force, we mean acceleration (Newton’s second law).  Gravity accelerates a mass.  Repulsive or attractive force accelerates a charge.

This force is known as electrostatic force and is described by Coulomb’s Law.  Coulomb discovered in the 18th century that repulsive or attractive force is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance.

FCoulomb = k CA CB / r2

… where FCoulomb is the electrostatic force in newtons (N), CA and CB are the two charges in coulombs (C), r is the distance between them in metres (m), and k is the force constant, 8.99 × 109 N m2/C2.

Look at the shape of it

When you look at this equation for the force between two charges separated by a distance, you notice that another equation looks just like this.  If you exchange C’s for M’s and k for G, doesn’t that look like Newton’s law of gravitation?  The answer is yes, and there was a reason why I said in the earlier Notes that you should think about charges as you think about masses.  As far as forces go, charges behave just like masses: the larger the charge, the larger the force; the larger the distance, the smaller the force.  So it turns out that you can just import all you know about gravitation into the electrostatic forces.  There is no new math.

1.  The two constants, side by side

Remember that doubling a charge doubles the force, and doubling the distance quarters the force.  Note the electrostatic constant, k = 8.99 × 109 N m2/C2, is a very large number, in the order of 109.  This contrasts the gravitational constant, G = 6.674 × 10−11 N m2/kg2, a very small number, in the order of 10−11.

This enormous difference in these constants is crucial, as you will discover that electrostatic force can easily completely overwhelm the force of gravity between the masses of small objects — because of the different magnitudes of the numbers.

2.  Both charges feel the same force

Figure 1.  Two unequal charges, and the forces they exert on each other.  His answer, written across this pair on the master: “Equal and opposite!!  Newton’s 3rd law pair!!”

Compare the forces each charged object feels from the other.  Same magnitude, opposite directions — and the reason is not electrostatics at all.  It is Newton’s third law.

Look at the equation and you can see why it has to be so: q1q2 is one product.  There is no way to write a bigger force for the bigger charge, because neither charge appears on its own.  The 6 μC does not push harder than the 2 μC.  It never did.

The 6 μC has three times the charge, so you might expect three times the force.  But the force on either one is kq1q2/r2 — the same expression, because it contains both charges and nothing else.  What differs is the acceleration: if the 6 μC object is also more massive, it accelerates less, which is exactly the situation of the Earth and a dropped ball.

4.  An iron nucleus and its closest electron

Q1.  What is the magnitude of the electric force of attraction between an iron nucleus (q = +26e) and its closest electron, if the distance between them is 1.5 × 10−13 m?  If you assume the electron is orbiting the nucleus, determine how fast that electron is going.

F = k q1q2 / r2= (8.99 × 109)(26)(1.6 × 10−19)2 / (1.5 × 10−13)2= 0.27 N

Then the electron is held in its orbit by that force, so the electric force is the centripetal force:

FCoul = Fc = me v2 / r →  v = √[ rF / me ]

v = √[ (1.5 × 10−13)(0.27) / (9.11 × 10−31) ] →  v = 2.09 × 108 m/s

That is 0.7c.  Seventy per cent of the speed of light, from a force of about a quarter of a newton — the weight of a small apple — acting on something as light as an electron.  This is why the inner electrons of heavy atoms have to be treated relativistically, and it is a result you can reach with nothing but this note and F = mv2/r.

5.  Three charges at once: superposition

Nothing new is needed to handle more than two charges.  Work out each force separately and add them.  That is superposition, and it is the same rule you already use for gravity.

Figure 3.  Q2.  In each panel three charges are fixed on a grid and a point P is marked.  All the charges are the same size Q, but they can be either positive or negative.  Rank the strength of the net force on a positive charge +q placed at P.

Take the force from a charge one square away as 1.  Two squares away is ¼, three squares is ⅛ — the inverse square, counted in grid squares.  Then sign each one by whether it pushes P right or pulls it left, and add.

the three contributionsnet
A+⅛ +¼ +149/36 = 1.361
B+¼ +1 −11/4 = 0.25
C+¼ −1 −1−7/4 = −1.75
D+⅛ −¼ +131/36 = 0.861

Ranked by strength, greatest first: C, A, D, B.

Two things worth noticing.  C is the strongest even though it has a negative charge in it — because the negative charge at one square pulls P the same way the far positive one pushes it, so they add.  And B is the weakest because its two nearest charges are on opposite sides and nearly cancel.  Geometry decides this, not the number of charges.

6.  Which way does the net force point?

Figure 4.  Q3.  Three charges at the vertices of each equilateral triangle, all the same magnitude.  In the first, only charge 1 is positive; in the second, all three carry the same sign.  Give the direction of the net force on each charge as a letter from the compass.

Adding forces as vectors is the whole exercise here.  Each charge feels two forces of equal size — same charges, same separation — so the answer is always the direction that bisects or opposes them, and you can get every one of these without a calculator.

the two forces on itnet direction
Charge 1 (+)pulled towards 2 and towards 3, symmetricallyG — straight down
Charge 2 (−)pulled towards 1 (up-right), pushed away from 3 (left)L
Charge 3 (−)pulled towards 1 (up-left), pushed away from 2 (right)B
Charge 4pushed away from both, symmetricallyA — straight up
Charge 5pushed away from 4 and from 6I
Charge 6pushed away from 4 and from 5E

Charges 2 and 3 must be mirror images of each other, because the whole picture is symmetric about the vertical through charge 1.  L and B are mirror images.  Use that as your check: if your two answers are not mirrored, one of them is wrong.

Doing charge 3 properly, with the side length as the unit and the force between any two as F: the pull towards 1 points up and to the left at 120°, and the push away from 2 points straight right at 0°.  Adding, F(−0.5, 0.866) + F(1, 0) = F(0.5, 0.866), which is 60° — up and to the right.  That is B.

A correction, and why it is here

Two of these answers are corrected from the master.  On the FLCC sheet charges 2 and 3 are answered K and C; on his own earlier Regents key they are answered L and B.  L and B are right, and the vector sum above shows why.  K and C are each one compass step too far round.

It is worth seeing rather than hiding, because the symmetry check catches it in seconds and K/C pass that check too — they are mirror images of each other as well.  A consistency test can only tell you two answers agree with each other.  Getting the direction right still takes the vector sum.

Figure 5.  His answer, drawn on the master with “a ∝ 1/r2” written beside it.  The bead starts at rest, is pushed away, and its acceleration drops as it goes — steeply at first, then hardly at all.

The shape is the force law itself, divided by a constant mass.  Acceleration is not constant here, which means none of the constant-acceleration kinematics from Module 1 applies — a point worth making out loud, because the graph looks inviting and the equations do not work.

8.  Doing it properly: Coulomb’s law as a vector sum

Ranking forces by eye works while the charges sit on a line or a symmetric triangle.  Off those cases you need components, and the law has to be written as a vector:

F31 = k q3q1 / r312   r̂31

… where r̂31 is the unit vector that carries the direction and r312 the distance.  Writing it this way, the sum over several charges is just F = ΣFi, component by component.

Figure 6.  His own worked setup, from a sheet of his own from July 2021 — the one place in this corpus where Coulomb’s law is done as a vector sum.  The signs are not on that page (it is part (c) of a longer problem), but both of his force arrows point towards the other charges, which settles that charge 3 carries the opposite sign to 1 and 2.

His convention, written on the sheet: r31 “points from 3 to 1”, and F31 is the force of 1 on 3.

r31 = −1î − 0.5ĵ  r31 = √(12 + 0.52)

F31 = k (8 × 10−6)(6 × 10−9) / (12 + 0.52)3/2 (−î − 0.5ĵ)

= 3.088 × 10−4 (−î − 0.5ĵ)

Note the 3/2 power: dividing by r2 and then by r again to normalise the direction vector is what produces it.  That is the single most common slip in this calculation.

r32 = −0.5ĵ  F32 = k (15 × 10−6)(6 × 10−9) / (0.5)2 (−ĵ) = −3.236 × 10−3 ĵ

F31 + F32 = −3.088 × 10−4 î − 3.39 × 10−3 ĵ

|F31 + F32| = 3.40 × 10−3 N  at  θ = 84.8° (+180°) = 264.8°

Why the +180°.  The arctangent of the ratio of components cannot tell which of two opposite directions you are in — it returns the same angle for (−, −) as for (+, +).  Both components here are negative, so the vector points down and to the left, into the third quadrant, and the half turn has to be put back by hand.  His tick is next to that step on the sheet.

What this section buys you

Every other force problem in this course is now the same problem.  Charges on a line, charges on a triangle, a charge near a whole sheet of other charges — all of it is this calculation, repeated and added.  The electric field in the next note is nothing but this sum, done once and divided by the test charge, so that you do not have to do it again for every new charge you bring in.

Looking ahead

You now have the force between any two charges, and the rule for adding up as many of them as you like.  The next note stops asking about the force and starts asking about the space itself.

The move is small and it changes everything: divide the force by the charge you brought in to feel it.  What is left does not depend on that charge at all — it is a property of the arrangement, there whether or not anything is present to be pushed.  That is the electric field, and your first clue that it is worth defining is the thing you have just done four times: the same tedious sum, with a different test charge each time.

Watch for

The 1/r2 shape survives into the field, the potential and Gauss’s law.  Everything in this module is this law, looked at from a different angle.  When something later looks new, try writing it back as a sum of Coulomb forces and see whether it still is.
