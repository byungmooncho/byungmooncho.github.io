Charged Objects Have Excess Electrons or Protons

Only electrons move between objects

Synopsis

It’s really all about electric charges.  Remember the four fundamental quantities in Nature that are always conserved?  Electric charge is the last one to explore, as matter, energy and momentum are now familiar to us.

You should think about charge the way you think about mass: a fundamental property of matter, with an amount (in coulombs, like mass in kg) and a type — negative or positive, unlike mass, which doesn’t have types.

All matter is made of atoms, and all atoms are made of electrons, protons and neutrons.  They all have electric charge as one of their defining properties: an electron has one negative charge, a proton one positive, and a neutron zero.  All “naturally occurring” things are neutral, meaning there must be a balance between the numbers of electrons and protons.  In fact, Nature abhors charge imbalance and will do everything to neutralise excess charge.  This tendency is much at the heart of chemistry.

There are only two types of charge, and all we need to care about is that like charges repel and opposite charges attract.  That is really it.  The rest of this module is just describing the forces and the energies in a collection of charges — mostly just two — using equations.

A correction to the master

The printed note gives the proton mass as 1.27 × 10−27 kg.  It is 1.673 × 10−27, and the sentence that follows it on the same page is what gives the slip away: an electron is “around 2000 times lighter than a proton”, and 1.673 × 10−27 ÷ 9.11 × 10−31 = 1836.  The wrong figure would give 1394.  Corrected here.

2.  Making a charged object: two strips of sticky tape

The quickest way to see all of this is to make charge yourself.  Stick two strips of tape together and rip them apart abruptly.

Figure 1.  His procedure.  A base strip stuck to the desk, a bottom strip on top of it, a top strip on that, with the handles all folded to the same side so you can pull them without touching the sticky face.

When two stuck strips are pulled apart abruptly, the electrons do not divide evenly between them.  The side that gained electrons — so that electron numbers exceed proton numbers — is negatively charged.  The side that lost them is positively charged.

The honest problem

You won’t know a priori which side is positive and which is negative.  Ripping tape tells you the two strips are opposite; it cannot tell you which is which.  To settle that you need an object whose charge you already know.

The ebonite rod is known negative, and it repels the bottom strip.  Like charges repel, so the bottom strip is negative.

Everything else in the table agrees with that and is worth checking as a test of the method: top repels top and bottom repels bottom (like charges); top attracts bottom (opposite); paper and foil show N against each other and A against every charged thing; and your finger behaves exactly like the paper.

His answers on the sheet: “Is there a force between neutral and charged objects?” — Attractive.  “Did you confirm the top and bottom strips are oppositely charged?” — yes, they attract.  Top: +  Bottom: −

Notice what the N entries are doing.  Neutral paper does nothing to neutral foil, but is attracted by everything charged, whichever sign.  A neutral object being pulled towards both a positive and a negative rod is the thing section 5 has to explain.

3.  Only electrons move between objects

Rubbing, ripping, touching — every method in this note moves electrons, and never protons.  The reason is structural rather than electrical.  Protons and neutrons sit in the nucleus, bound far too tightly to be shifted by rubbing.  Electrons surround the nucleus, are far lighter, and the outermost ones are not tightly bound at all — those can be made to jump from one object to another.

A positively charged object is not one that gained protons.  It is one that lost electrons.

That is worth saying out loud because the arithmetic is identical either way, so nothing in a calculation will ever catch you believing the wrong mechanism.

4.  Charges share out when objects touch

Nature’s abhorrence of charge imbalance applies to different amounts of like charge too.  Two metal objects brought into contact behave like one continuous object; separate them again and each carries half the total.

Figure 2.  His three worked cases.  Add the charges, divide by the number of objects.  The third is the one to look at twice — a positive and a negative object end up both negative.

This only works where electrons can move freely, which is what metals have and other materials do not:

Conductors (metals)free electrons, so charge redistributes equally
Insulatorsno free electrons, so charge sticks where it is put and the imbalance survives contact and separation — not energetically ideal, but there is no route to fix it
Semiconductorsconductor at high temperature, insulator at low

So the tape strips keep their charge after you separate them, and two touching metal spheres do not.  Same physics, different material.

Four to check for yourself

(a)  An object with an excess of 6.0 × 106 electrons has what net charge?
(b)  An alpha particle is two protons and two neutrons.  What is its charge?
(c)  Metal sphere A carries −2 units, identical sphere B carries −4.  They touch, then separate.  What is on B?
(d)  A conductor carrying 8 elementary charges touches an identical neutral conductor.  What is on each, in coulombs, after they are parted?

(a)  charge = e × (6 × 106) = (1.6 × 10−19)(6 × 106) = 9.6 × 10−13 C, negative.

(b)  +2e = 2(1.6 × 10−19) = 3.2 × 10−19 C.  The neutrons contribute nothing.

(c)  Total −6 units over two spheres, so −3 units each.

(d)  8 elementary charges shared is 4 each, and 4(1.6 × 10−19) = 6.4 × 10−19 C on each.

5.  The two ways to charge an object

Figure 3.  Conduction needs contact and is permanent; induction needs no contact and is temporary.  Note the signs: conduction leaves the object with the same sign as the charger, induction with the opposite.

Charging by conduction.  Rub two materials together and electrons jump.  His own caution on the word: “conduction” here has nothing to do with metal.  It simply means there was contact, and electrons went across.  A negatively charged object charges a neutral one negatively, and the charge stays.

Charging by induction.  This one needs no contact at all, and you have already seen it: in the tape experiment, a charged strip attracted a neutral paper strip it never touched.  Bring a charged object near a neutral one and the neutral one’s own charges separate — the opposite sign gathers at the near face, the like sign is pushed to the far one.  The near face wins, because it is closer, so the net force is attraction.

This is why a neutral object is attracted to a charged one whichever sign the charged one carries — and why every N row in his table has an A in it the moment a charged object is involved.

Induction is temporary.  Take the charging object away and the separated charges relax back; the neutral object is no longer “charged”.

Three spheres and a rod

Figure 4.  Three neutral metal spheres, touching, on insulating stands, with a positively charged rod held near x and not touching it.  The charge marks are the answer.

Because the spheres touch, they are one conductor, and electrons are free to move the whole length of it.  The positive rod pulls electrons towards x; they come from as far away as z.

x ends up negative and z positive, with y in the middle largely unchanged.  Nothing has been added or removed — the three spheres together are still neutral, and separating them while the rod is held in place is what would make the charge permanent.

Also worth having: a positively charged glass rod attracts object X.  What is X’s charge?  His answer: it may be zero or negative.  Attraction alone does not prove a charge, because a neutral object is attracted too.  Only repulsion proves a charge, which is why his table settles the tape strips by the two R entries and not by any of the As.

The electroscope

A simple tool for detecting small charges.  A conducting rod carries two thin conducting leaves, isolated inside a flask.  The rod and leaves form one conducting path, so charge spreads evenly over all of it; the leaves then carry like charge, repel each other, and a bigger charge gives a bigger separation.

Figure 5.  His three-step sequence, with his notes on each.  This is how you leave a permanent charge by induction — and the sign you end up with is opposite to the rod you never touched it with.

1  Rod brought near.  Electrons are pulled up to the knob, leaving the leaves positive.  They repel and open.  His note: “negative charges held in place” — held there by the rod.

2  Ground it, rod still in place.  The leaves collapse.  His note gives both readings of the same event: “positive charges discharge via ground”, or “excess electrons from ground neutralise the + in the leaf”.  Only electrons actually move — the second phrasing is the literal one, and the first is the bookkeeping.

3  Break the ground, then remove the rod.  The electrons that were held at the knob are now free and in excess.  His note: “remove rod, electrons redistribute evenly” — and the leaves, now both negative, “repel!”

The electroscope is left permanently negative by a rod that was permanently positive, and the two never touched.

6.  How much charge, and how many electrons

Charge does not come in arbitrary amounts.  It comes in whole electrons.  Any charge you can put on an object is

q = n e  with n a whole number and e = 1.60 × 10−19 C

His own note on this, from section 4: a coulomb holds an enormous number of electrons, so a total charge of −1.5 C is in no way a problem — it is still a whole number of them.  What cannot happen is a fractional number of electrons, and at these sizes you would never notice the difference.

So a charge of 4.80 × 10−19 C could sit on an object — it is exactly 3e — while 6.25 × 10−19 C could not.

How you would discover a quantum from data

Quantization is not something you have to take on faith.  You can find the quantum from measurements alone, and a question he marked in his own archive shows the method with nothing more than loose change.

Five identical containers, each holding a different number of pennies, are weighed:

Container12345
Mass (g)35.264.048.038.441.6

What is the most likely mass of one penny?

Not the smallest measurement, and not the differences between them.  The quantum is the largest number that divides every reading a whole number of times.

Try 3.2 g: 35.2/3.2 = 11 · 64.0/3.2 = 20 · 48.0/3.2 = 15 · 38.4/3.2 = 12 · 41.6/3.2 = 13.  Every one a whole number of pennies.

Try 6.4 g and the first container gives 5.5 — half a penny, which does not exist.  So the answer is 3.2 g.

This is exactly how the elementary charge was found.  Millikan measured the charge on many oil drops, found no drop carried less than a certain amount, and found every drop carried a whole-number multiple of it.  Same reasoning, pennies for electrons — and you never have to see a penny or an electron to do it.

The number that makes it concrete

Back to the question his own preamble asks: roughly how much charge is on a woolly sweater that gives you a shock, and how many excess electrons is that?

sweater ≈ 10−6 C  one electron = −1.6 × 10−19 C

n = 10−6 / (1.6 × 10−19) ≈ 6 × 1012 →  about 1013 electrons

Ten trillion electrons — and that is the number that makes the whole note land.  Set it against the 1024 charged particles in the object.  A shock you can feel across a room is an imbalance of about one part in a hundred billion.

That is what “Nature abhors charge imbalance” means quantitatively.  Matter is held so close to neutral that the largest departure you meet in daily life is invisible against the total.

Looking ahead

You can now make a charged object, say what sign it carries and say how many electrons that took.  What you cannot yet do is say how hard two of them push on each other.

That is the next note, and the shape of the answer is already familiar: the force grows with both charges and falls off with distance squared — which is Newton’s law of gravitation with the masses swapped for charges.  There is no new mathematics, only a new constant, and that constant is about twenty orders of magnitude bigger than G.

Watch for

Keep the 1013 electrons in mind.  That much imbalance, on a balloon, holds it against a wall while the entire Earth pulls it down.  The next note is where that stops being surprising and becomes a number.
