FLCC PhysicsModule 3 · ElectricityNote 12

Applications: Nuclei and Accelerators

Four problems, one ledger

Synopsis

Everything in this module so far has been forces and fields: how hard charges push, and what the push per coulomb is at a given place.  This note spends that machinery on four real objects — a nucleus coming apart, two nuclei being driven together, and a beam of particles crossing a pair of charged plates.

There is no new physics in it at all.  Every one of the four is the same bookkeeping line, written out and then crossed down:

U1 + K1 + Wext = U2 + K2

Nothing external does work in any of them, so Wext goes.  In each problem two more terms are zero because something starts at rest, or ends at rest, or is far enough away that its potential energy is nothing.  What survives is a one-line equation, and the whole skill is knowing which terms to cross out.

The two expressions for U you already have:

U = kq1q2 / r   (two point charges)     U = q∆V   (a charge crossing a potential difference)

Where the other applications are

Two applications you might expect here are already elsewhere in this module, and are not repeated.  The electric force against gravity is Note 8 — the whole isomorphism table, plus his ladder sheet with the 2 C charge beside the 2 kg mass.  Millikan is Note 9, where the oil drop hangs motionless and qE = mg gives 4.8 × 10−19 C, and the method of finding a quantum from measurements alone is the five containers of pennies in Charged Objects.

Performance expectation
  1. Solve problems involving electric potential energy, using conservation of energy to find speeds, closest approaches and accelerating voltages for charged particles.

1.  Alpha decay: where does the speed come from?

A radon nucleus (atomic number 86) transforms spontaneously into a polonium nucleus (atomic number 84) and an alpha particle — two protons and two neutrons bound together.  Immediately afterwards both pieces are at rest, about 50 × 10−15 m apart.

Figure 1.  His sketch of the instant after the transformation.  The polonium carries +84e, the alpha +2e, and neither is moving.  Every joule the alpha ends up with is already in the diagram, stored as potential energy in that separation.

(a)  The potential energy it starts with

Work it in two steps, the way he does: first the potential the polonium creates at the alpha’s position, then the energy that potential gives to the alpha’s charge.

VPo = kqPo / r1 =  (9 × 109 N·m2/C2)(84)(1.6 × 10−19 C) / (50 × 10−15 m)

U = qαVPo = kqPoqα / r1 =  (9 × 109)(84)(2)(1.6 × 10−19)2 / (50 × 10−15)

U = 7.74 × 10−13 J

Note what the two charges did: 84 and 2 in units of e, so the product carries 168 e2.  That factor is why a nucleus coming apart releases so much more energy than anything chemical — chemistry moves one or two elementary charges at a time, across a distance ten thousand times larger.

(b)  How fast the alpha is going when it is clear

Far away, the potential energy is gone.  The polonium is about fifty times heavier than the alpha, so it barely moves, and the alpha takes essentially all of the kinetic energy.

His ledger, with the dead terms struck down:

K1,Po + K1,α + U1 + Wext  =  K2,Po + K2,α + U2

Three of the five terms are zero because both pieces start at rest, and U2 is zero because r → ∞.  What is left is one line:

U1 = ½mαv2 →  v = √(2U1 / mα)

The alpha is two protons and two neutrons, so mα = 2(1.67 × 10−27) + 2(1.68 × 10−27) = 6.70 × 10−27 kg.

v = √[ 2(7.74 × 10−13) / (6.70 × 10−27) ] =  1.52 × 107 m/s

About 5% of the speed of light, out of a nucleus that was sitting still.

(c)  Where is it when it has 90% of that speed?

This is the part worth slowing down on, and it is also the one place on his sheet where the arithmetic goes wrong.  The set-up is right:

U1 = ½mα(0.9v)2 + kqPoqα / r′ →  r′ = kqPoqα / [ U1 − ½mα(0.9v)2 ]

The denominator is what is left over as potential energy — the total minus the kinetic.  Before substituting anything, notice that you can read it off without a calculator.

Figure 2.  U = kqPoqα / r, computed from his own numbers, with his 7.74 × 10−13 J as the dashed total.  The curve and the dashed line are never further apart than the total energy, so the whole of part (c) is the question of where the gap reaches 81% of the height.  The open grey circle is where his sheet’s answer falls.

Kinetic energy goes as the square of the speed, so at 0.9v the alpha already holds 0.92 = 81% of the total.  That leaves 19% as potential energy.  And U goes as 1/r, so cutting U to 19% of its starting value means multiplying r by 1/0.19:

r′ = r1 / 0.19 = (50 × 10−15 m) / 0.19 =  2.6 × 10−13 m

Or the long way, with his numbers, which must agree:

kqPoqα = 3.871 × 10−26

U1 − ½mα(0.9v)2 = 7.74 × 10−13 − 6.27 × 10−13 =  1.47 × 10−13 J

r′ = (3.871 × 10−26) / (1.47 × 10−13) =  2.63 × 10−13 m

Both routes give the same thing, which is the point of doing it twice.  The alpha has travelled more than five times its starting separation.

A correction to his sheet

His own line reads 3.871 × 10−26 ÷ 6.177 × 10−13 = 6.266 × 10−14 m.  The division is right; the number he divided by is not.  6.18 × 10−13 is the ½m(0.9v)2 term itself, not U1 − ½m(0.9v)2 — the subtraction in the line immediately above never got carried down.

It is caught by the sanity check, not by redoing the arithmetic.  A particle that has 90% of its final speed is nearly done accelerating and must be far out; 6.27 × 10−14 m is only 1.25 times the starting separation, which would leave 80% of the energy still stored.  Look at Figure 2: at 63 fm the curve has barely come down.  Corrected here to 2.63 × 10−13 m.

2.  Fusion: the same sum, run backwards

Alpha decay is two charges being pushed apart by their own repulsion.  Fusion is the problem of driving two of them together against it, and it is the identical equation with the start and the finish swapped.

A proton is fired at a fixed deuterium nucleus — an isotope of hydrogen with one proton and one neutron.  If it gets within about 1.0 × 10−15 m, the two have a good chance of fusing.

Figure 3.  His sketch.  The proton is launched at v1 from far away and comes to a stop at r2, the distance of closest approach.  The deuterium is held fixed, so none of the energy goes into moving it.  Both carry +e; the extra neutron adds mass and no charge.

(a)  The launch speed that just reaches

Far away, U1 = 0.  At closest approach, the proton has momentarily stopped, so K2 = 0.  The ledger keeps two terms:

K1 = U2 →  ½mpv12 = kqDqp / r2

v1 = √(2kqDqp / mpr2) =  √[ 2(9 × 109)(1.6 × 10−19)2  /  (1.67 × 10−27)(1.0 × 10−15) ]

v1 = 1.66 × 107 m/s

Which is the same order as the alpha’s exit speed in section 1, and for the same reason: it is the same barrier, climbed instead of descended.

(b)  Fire the deuterium at the protons instead

His part (b) swaps the roles.  Nothing in the physics changes — the barrier is the same height, because it depends on the two charges and the distance, and neither has moved.  Only the mass in the kinetic-energy term is different.

v1D = √(2kqDqp / mDr2)  with  mD = (1.67 + 1.68) × 10−27 = 3.35 × 10−27 kg

v1D = 1.17 × 107 m/s

Slower, by a factor of √2 — because the deuteron is twice the mass and needs the same kinetic energy.  Check that against the numbers: 1.66 × 107 ÷ √2 = 1.17 × 107.  It is the energy that has to match the barrier, never the speed.

(c)  Launch at half the speed — how close now?

Same two-term ledger, with 0.5v1 in place of v1:

r2′ = 2kqDqp / mp(0.5v1)2 =  (4.608 × 10−28) / (1.150 × 10−13)

r2′ = 4.0 × 10−15 m

Four times as far out, for half the speed — and you did not need the calculator to know that.  r sits on the bottom of an expression whose top is fixed and whose other factor is v2, so r ∝ 1/v2.  Halve v and r quadruples: 1.0 fm becomes 4.0 fm, and no fusion.

That steepness is the whole engineering problem with fusion.  A beam at 50% of the design speed does not get you 50% of the way there; it stops four times too far out.

3.  The accelerator: plates with holes in them

The last two problems are the same ledger with the other expression for U.  Between parallel plates there is no 1/r anywhere — the field is uniform, and the energy a charge gains is simply q∆V.

Figure 4.  His sketch of the device.  Two oppositely charged plates 5 cm apart, with small holes cut in them so a beam can pass straight through.  The particle enters at v1 and leaves at v2; the only thing that happened in between is that the field did work on it.

(a)  An electron speeds up — what is the voltage?

An electron is injected at 2.0 × 107 m/s and leaves at 2.5 × 107 m/s.  Run the ledger the other way round: the speeds are given and the potential difference is the unknown.

q∆V = ½me(v12 − v22) →  ∆V = me(v12 − v22) / 2q

∆V = −(9.11 × 10−31)[ (2 × 107)2 − (2.5 × 107)2 ] / 2(1.6 × 10−19)

∆V = 640.5 V

The minus sign in front is the electron’s own charge, −e.  Keep it.  Dropping it is the commonest way to come out of this problem with a plausible number and the wrong sign, which here would mean a device that slows the beam down instead of speeding it up.

(b)  Now send a proton through the same device

Same plates, same 640.5 V, same entry speed.  Before calculating, predict.

v2 = √(v12 − 2qp∆V / mp) =  √[ (2.0 × 107)2 − 2(1.6 × 10−19)(640.5) / (1.67 × 10−27) ]

v2 = 1.99969 × 107 m/s

His own answer, to all those figures, and the figures are the answer: the proton enters at 2.0 × 107 and leaves at 1.99969 × 107.  It has changed speed by about one part in six and a half thousand — 0.015%.

Two things did that.  The proton is 1836 times heavier, so the same energy buys far less speed — and it carries the opposite sign, so this device decelerates it rather than speeding it up.  A set of plates built to accelerate electrons barely touches a proton.

(c)  The field between the plates

The field follows from the voltage and the gap, and his line for it is worth copying out because it shows where V = Ed comes from rather than just using it:

V12 = V2 − V1 = −∫ E · dr = −∫ E dr cos(180°) =  E ∫ dr  =  Er

Walking against the field is what makes the cosine −1, and the two minus signs cancel.  With a uniform field E comes out of the integral and the rest is just the gap width.

E = V12 / r = (640.5 V) / (5 × 10−2 m) =  12,810 V/m

Which is 12,810 N/C — the same quantity in the units Note 9 used.  Volts per metre and newtons per coulomb are the same thing, and this is the problem where that stops being a piece of trivia.

4.  A 20 kV accelerator, and the question about the gap

His fourth problem is the same device driven much harder: a proton injected at 2.0 × 106 m/s across 20 kV, with the left plate at the higher potential and the plates 5 cm apart.

Going from the high plate to the low one, ∆V = V2 − V1 = −20,000 V, and the proton is positive, so it gains energy.

v2 = √(v12 − 2qp∆V / mp) =  √[ (2.0 × 106)2 − 2(1.6 × 10−19)(−2.0 × 104) / (1.67 × 10−27) ]

v2 = 2.80 × 106 m/s

Check it in electronvolts instead, which is faster: a proton crossing 20 kV picks up 20 keV however you dress it up, and 20,000(1.6 × 10−19) = 3.2 × 10−15 J.  It started with ½(1.67 × 10−27)(2.0 × 106)2 = 3.34 × 10−15 J, so it ends with 6.54 × 10−15 J — and that gives the same 2.80 × 106 m/s.

Now widen the gap to 10 cm, holding 20 kV

His answer to this one is written out in prose, and it is the best line on the sheet:

In his words

“Change in separation of the plates doesn’t come into play given the equation for v2 above.”

No separation appears in it.  The energy a charge picks up is q∆V, and that is the whole story — the proton leaves at the same 2.80 × 106 m/s whether the plates are 5 cm or 10 cm apart.  It takes longer to cross, and the force on it is weaker, and those two exactly compensate.

But something must have changed.  What?

Figure 5.  Your job.  Both pairs are across the same 20 kV source and the right-hand gap is twice the left-hand one.  Mark the field between each pair and the charge sitting on each plate, then say in one sentence what is the same on the two sides and what is not.

Start from the same line as part (c) of section 3, which for a uniform field is just ∆V = Er:

∆V = Er  ∆V fixed, r doubled  →  E′ = E / 2

And for charged plates the field is set by how densely the charge sits on them, so halving the field means halving that density:

E = σ / ε0  and  E′ = σ′ / ε0 →  σ′ / ε0 = σ / 2ε0  →  σ′ = σ / 2

His conclusion, in his hand: “charge density has to decrease by factor of 2.”  Half the field, half the surface charge — which is why Figure 5 has four field lines and four charges a side on the left and two on the right.

So the exit speed is unchanged and the plates are carrying half as much charge.  Widening the gap did not change what the device does to the beam; it changed what the source has to supply to hold the voltage up.

Looking ahead

That is the electricity module finished.  Everything in it came from two equations for the force and two for the energy, and the last four problems were those same equations handed to a nucleus and a particle beam.

Notice what the accelerator could not do.  It changed the beam’s speed and left its direction alone — the holes had to line up, because the particle goes where it was already going.  An electric field cannot steer.

Watch for

The next module is magnetism, and it starts exactly where this one stops: a particle is accelerated through a potential difference, and then it is bent.  On the same sheet these four problems came from, the next two pages do that — a proton accelerated through ∆V into a field B, turning on a circle of radius R = (1/B)√(2m∆V/q).
