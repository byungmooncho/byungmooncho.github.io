Gauss’s Law

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The ideaAdding up the contributions of a million pieces of charge is correct and slow. Gauss’s law replaces the whole integral with one counting argument: the number of field lines leaving a closed surface depends only on the charge inside it. Where the geometry is symmetric enough, that turns a hard integral into one line of algebra.

Flux

Flux is the amount of something flowing through an area. For the electric field nothing actually flows, but the bookkeeping is identical.

Φ = E · A = EA cos θ
Flux is a scalar, but we give it a sign.  Out of a closed surface counts positive, in counts negative. Trevor’s own phrasing is the honest one: flux doesn’t really have a direction mathematically, but we assign it one.

For a surface that bends, or a field that varies over it, chop the surface into patches dA small enough that neither changes across them, and add:

Φ = ∮ E · dA

The circle on the integral sign means the surface is closed — it has an inside. That is not decoration; everything below depends on it.

Gauss’s law

∮ E · dA = qencε0
Read the law carefully, because it says less than it looks like it says.
  •  The flux depends only on the enclosed charge. Charge outside the surface contributes nothing to it.
  •  The field at a point on the surface depends on everything, inside and out. Moving an outside charge changes E all over the surface — it just rearranges the flux without changing the total.
  •  So the law is always true, and only sometimes useful: you can pull E out of the integral only when symmetry says it is constant over the surface.

Choosing the surface: the line of charge

The wire of Fields by Integration took a trigonometric substitution and most of a page. Trevor puts the alternative plainly: two options — set up the integral using small pieces and play with the angles, or set up Gauss’s law and integrate.

E (2πsℓ) = λℓε0  ⇒  E = λ2πε0 s
The length cancels, and it had to.  Nothing in the problem picks out a length along an infinite wire, so no answer may depend on the one you chose for your cylinder. If ℓ survives to the end, something went wrong.

Compare the two routes and the pattern is clear: Gauss does the work when there is enough symmetry to make E constant on a surface you can name, and that is spherical, cylindrical or planar symmetry — nothing else. For a finite wire, a ring or a disc, there is no such surface and the integral is the only way.
