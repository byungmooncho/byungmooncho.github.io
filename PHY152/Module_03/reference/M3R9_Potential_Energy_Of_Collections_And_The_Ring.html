Potential Energy of Collections, and the Ring

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The ideaPotential is a scalar, so the potential of several charges is their potentials added up — no components, no angles. That one fact does two jobs on this page: it gives the energy stored in a collection of point charges, and it turns the field of a ring from a vector integral into a derivative.

The energy of a collection

How much work does it take to assemble a set of charges, bringing each one in from infinitely far away? Build it up one at a time.

U = k q1q2r12 + k q1q3r13 + k q2q3r23
One term per PAIR, and each pair exactly once.  Three charges give three pairs, four give six, five give ten. The commonest mistake is counting q1q2 and then q2q1 as well, which doubles the answer. Trevor’s own wording is the thing to hold on to: make sure to get all pairs, and be careful to not count any twice.

The order you build them in does not matter, and it is worth checking that for yourself on three charges. If it did matter, the energy would not be a property of the arrangement, and the whole idea would collapse.

The signs carry real meaning here. A negative total energy means the collection is bound — it would take work to pull it apart. A positive total means it is straining to fly apart, and would give that energy up if released.

The ring, by potential

Every piece of a ring of radius R is the same distance √(z² + R²) from a point on the axis. Since V is a scalar there is nothing to resolve into components — the integral is simply the total charge over that one distance:

V = kQ√(z² + R²)
Compare that with the field.  Getting E directly meant arguing about which components cancel and carrying a cos θ through the integral. Getting V meant noticing that one distance is the same for every piece. Where you can do either, do the potential.

And back to the field

The field is the rate at which the potential falls off — so having V everywhere, you can differentiate rather than integrate:

Ez = −dVdz    E = −∇V

In three dimensions each component is a partial derivative, Ex = −∂V/∂x and so on. For the ring on its axis there is only one variable to differentiate:

Ez = −ddz kQ(z² + R²)1/2 = kQz(z² + R²)3/2
That is the same result the direct integral gives.  Two independent routes to one answer, which is the best check there is.

This only works on the axis.  The expression for V above is the potential at points on the axis, so differentiating it gives you the rate of change along the axis and nothing else. The off-axis components are real; you simply cannot get them from a formula that never left the axis.
