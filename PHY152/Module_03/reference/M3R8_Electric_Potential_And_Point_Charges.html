Electric Potential, and the Potential of Point Charges

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The ideaForces and fields are vectors, and adding them is work. Potential energy and potential are scalars — they add like numbers, with no components and no angles. That is the whole reason this chapter exists, and it is why almost every problem gets easier the moment you stop tracking directions.

The gravitational version first

Lift a brick and you do work against gravity; let it fall and you get that work back. Gravity is a conservative force, so the work depends only on where the brick started and finished, never on the path — and that is what makes it storable as energy.

W = ∫ F · ds = ∫ F cos θ ds

Coulomb’s law has the same 1/r² shape as Newton’s, so the electric force is conservative too, and the whole apparatus carries over unchanged.

Potential energy, and then potential

ΔU = −W = −∫ qE · ds

That still depends on how much charge you happened to carry. Counting out a specific amount of charge is awkward, so divide it out — exactly the move that turned force into field:

ΔV = ΔUq    units: J/C = volt
Potential and potential energy are related and they are not the same thing.
  •  U is in joules and belongs to the charge and its surroundings together.
  •  V is in volts and belongs to the place. It is there whether or not any charge is.
  •  U = qV. Multiply by the charge you actually put there.
The practical difference is that potential difference is easy to measure — that is what a voltmeter reads — while the energy of a charge is not. For masses it is the other way round, which is why nobody bothers with “gravitational potential” in first-semester physics.

Equipotentials, and downhill

An equipotential is a line or surface joining points at the same potential. The contour lines on a walking map are exactly the same idea for height.

Equipotentials and field lines cross at right angles, always.  Moving along an equipotential takes no work, so the force has no component along it — which means the field is perpendicular to it everywhere. Like field lines, equipotentials never cross each other: a point cannot be at two potentials at once.

A uniform field

ΔV = −∫ E · ds = Ed    (uniform field)

Rearranged, E = V/d — so the volt per metre is a unit of electric field, and it is the same thing as the newton per coulomb. Both are correct and V/m is what you will see in practice.

The potential of a point charge

Integrate kQ/r² in from infinity and the exponent drops by one:

V = kQr = 14πε0 Qr
Zero is wherever you say it is — but for a point charge, say infinity.  Only differences in potential mean anything, so the zero is arbitrary, just as it was for gravitational potential energy last semester. Putting it infinitely far away is what makes the formula above come out with no additive constant, and it is the convention every textbook uses.

Signs are where this goes wrong. V can be positive or negative, and so can U = qV — and the two signs multiply. A negative charge in a negative potential has positive energy. The deck’s advice is the right habit: look at the context of the situation every time. It is also why pulling an electron off an atom costs energy: it sits in the well of a positive nucleus.
