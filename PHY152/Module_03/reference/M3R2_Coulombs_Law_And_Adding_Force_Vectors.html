Coulomb’s Law and Adding Force Vectors

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The ideaCoulomb’s law gives the force between two charges. Everything hard about using it comes from there being more than two, because forces are vectors and have to be added as vectors. This page is the law, the comparison with gravity that shows how absurdly strong it is, and two problems worked all the way through — one with three charges in a plane, one with two charged balls hanging on strings.

The law

F = k q1q2r² r̂
kCoulomb constant8.99 × 109 N·m²/C²
k = 1/4πε0the same constant, written with the permittivity
ε0Permittivity of free space8.85 × 10−12 C²/(N·m²)
The method, in the order that works.
  1.  Use the formula with magnitudes only — put the signs aside.
  2.  Put the direction in afterwards, from a sketch: along the line joining the two charges, toward for opposite signs and away for like signs.
  3.  Break each force into components and add.
Feeding signed charges into the formula and hoping the sign of the answer means something is the commonest way to get a direction backwards.

“Point charges” is an assumption about distance, not about size. The law as written needs the charges to be small compared with the separation between them. Two charged spheres a centimetre apart are not point charges if they are themselves a centimetre across.

Against gravity, for the same two particles

Take a proton and an electron one Bohr radius apart, r = 5.29 × 10−11 m, and work out both forces.

FG = G mempr² = 3.63 × 10−47 N
FE = k qeqpr² = 8.22 × 10−8 N
The electric force is about 2 × 1039 times the gravitational one — nearly forty orders of magnitude. Trevor’s own comment on the slide is the right one: there is no way to draw them to scale and be able to see both vectors.
This is why gravity is simply ignored in every electrostatics problem in this module, and why the one place it is not ignored — a charged drop held up against its own weight — needs a charge of only 10−19 C to do it.

Worked: three charges in a plane

Three charged spheres are arranged as shown. q1 = +5.00 μC, q2 = −6.31 μC, q3 = +2.34 μC. What is the net electrical force on q3?

Trevor’s four steps, and they are worth following in this order.
  1.  Find the separation of each pair.
  2.  Find the magnitude of each force.
  3.  Determine the direction of each force.
  4.  Add the force vectors.

Step 3 is the one that is actually physics.  q1 and q3 are both positive, so F13 pushes q3 away from q1 — up and to the right, along the line joining them, 36.0° above the horizontal. q2 is negative, so F23 pulls q3 toward q2 — straight down.

F13 = 76.501 N  at 36.0° above the horizontal  ·  F23 = 279.313 N  straight down

An answer of −88° or −89° would mean the x-component had all but vanished, and it plainly has not. The sketch is the cheapest error check in the whole module.

Worked: two charged balls on strings

Two small conducting balls hang from insulating strings of length L from the same point. Each has mass m and charge q. What angle do the strings make?

vertical:  T cos θ = mg    horizontal:  T sin θ = k q²(2L sin θ)²
Divide the second by the first and the tension disappears.  You never knew it and you never need it:

    sin²θ tan θ = kq² / 4mgL²

which has to be solved numerically for θ. The angle between the strings is then 2θ.

This is the shape of almost every hanging-charge problem: two force balances, one unknown tension, and a separation written in terms of the angle you are solving for. Getting to the line above is the whole of the physics; what follows is a calculator.
