Gauss Applications: Distributions and Conductors

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The ideaGauss’s law earns its keep on three shapes and in one material. This page is the shapes — how charge is described when it is spread out, and what the law gives for a solid sphere, inside and out — and then the material: a conductor, where the field inside is zero and everything else follows from that one fact.

Point-like is an assumption about distance, not about size.  A charged sphere counts as point-like when you look at it from far enough away compared with its own radius — and as the deck says, that is usually the case, which is why it is usually safe.

None of these has to be uniform. Charge implanted in an insulator can be densest at the centre and fade outwards, or the other way about, and then the density is a function:

Q = ∫ ρ(r) dV = ∫ ρ(r) 4πr² dr
There are two integrals in a Gauss problem and they are not the same integral.  One runs over the Gaussian surface and gives the flux; the other runs over the charge and gives qenc. Each needs its own limits, and the charge integral stops at the edge of the charge — Trevor’s warning is worth repeating: don’t integrate the charge past its outer edge.

A uniformly charged sphere

Total charge Q spread evenly through a ball of radius R. By symmetry E is radial and depends only on r, so the Gaussian surface is a sphere of radius r and the flux is always E(4πr²). Only qenc changes.

That last point is the one worth carrying: a spherical shell of charge produces no field anywhere inside itself. It is why only the charge closer in than r appears in qenc, and it is the same result Newton needed for gravity inside the Earth.

Conductors

A conductor is full of charge, but only its free electrons can move. Add an excess charge and nothing happens — add a second and they repel, and keep moving until they are as far apart as they can get. That motion is over in far less time than it takes to ask about it, and we only ever look at the after.

The one fact, and the three things that follow from it.
  E = 0 everywhere inside the metal. If it were not, the free charges would still be moving, and they are not.
  •  Excess charge lives on the surface, never in the interior — a Gaussian surface drawn anywhere in the metal has zero flux, so it encloses no net charge.
  •  The field just outside is perpendicular to the surface. A sideways component would push the surface charge along, and it has stopped moving.
  •  A nearby charge rearranges the surface without changing the total: bring a positive charge close and electrons pile up facing it, leaving positive regions behind.

From outside, that conductor looks exactly like a sphere carrying +q on its surface. Moving the charge about inside the cavity changes nothing you can measure outside, which is the whole principle of electrostatic shielding.
