The Field Concept, Field Lines and Dipoles

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The ideaA charge changes the space around it whether or not anything is there to feel it. The field is what is left when you divide the force by the charge that felt it — and once you have it, the force on any charge you put there is one multiplication. This page is that definition, the rules for drawing field lines, and the one arrangement where the whole thing pays off at once: the dipole.

From force to field

Put a very small positive test charge q0 at some point near a source charge Q and measure the force on it. Move it somewhere else and measure again. The force changes from point to point — but so does the answer if you change q0, and that part has nothing to do with Q.

E = Fq0    units: N/C
The field belongs to the source, not to the charge you probed it with.  That is the whole content of the definition, and it is why the field is worth having: measure once, and you can work out the force on anything you put there afterwards.
E = k Qr² r̂    (a point charge)

In practice nobody resolves r̂ symbolically. Trevor’s own advice on the slide is the working method: draw a line between the charges, then break into components in your chosen coordinate system. A simulation needs the unit vector; a person with a pencil does not.

And back again: the force from the field

F = qE

Field lines

Drawing an arrow at every point is unreadable, so the field is drawn as lines instead — curves that follow the field direction everywhere along their length.

The five rules, and every one of them is checkable.
  1.  A line points along the field vector at every point on it.
  2.  Lines start on positive charges, or come in from infinity.
  3.  Lines end on negative charges, or go out to infinity.
  4.  Lines are denser where the field is stronger.
  5.  Twice the charge, twice as many lines. The absolute number is yours to choose; the ratio is not.

A sixth follows from the first: two field lines can never cross, because the field at a crossing point would have to have two directions at once.

These two pictures are traced from the field itself rather than drawn by hand, so the line spacing is the real spacing and no line crosses another anywhere. The slides use a Wikimedia diagram by Geek3 for the same two cases; it is not reproduced here.

The hair-raising photograph.  The deck carries a picture a friend of Trevor’s took of his children in the mountains with their hair standing straight up on end, minutes before a lightning storm started. It is used there with the photographer’s permission and is not reproduced here, but the physics is worth keeping: the hair carries like charge and the strands are pushing each other apart, which is also the warning sign that the air above is about to break down.

The dipole

Two equal and opposite charges, q and −q, separated by d. The pair is described by one vector:

p = qd
pDipole momentC·m
qThe magnitude of one of the chargesC — not 2q
dSeparation vector, from the negative charge to the positive onem
Two things to get right, and they are the two the deck calls out.
  •  Use q, not 2q. The moment is one charge times the full separation, not the total charge times anything.
  •  d points from − to +, which is the opposite of the direction the field lines run between them.

A dipole in a uniform field

Fnet = qE + (−q)E = 0

Take torques about the centre. Each charge sits a distance d/2 from it, and each force contributes τ = rF sin θ turning the same way:

τ+ = τ− = d2 (qE) sin θ  ⇒  Στ = dqE sin θ
τ = p × E
The sign is orientation-dependent, and the cross product is what keeps track of it.  Written out as dqE sin θ the formula gives you a size; which way the dipole turns you read off the figure, or out of p × E. Either way the dipole turns toward alignment, and at θ = 0 the torque is zero — which is why p parallel to E is where it ends up.

Why this matters beyond the exam.  A water molecule is a permanent dipole, and so is a great many other molecules. Everything from why water dissolves salt to how a microwave oven heats food is this one picture — a dipole twisting to follow a field.
