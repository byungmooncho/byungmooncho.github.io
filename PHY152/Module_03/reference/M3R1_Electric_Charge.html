Electric Charge

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The ideaBefore anything can be calculated, charge has to be counted — and the unit it is counted in is wildly out of scale with the thing it counts. This page is the groundwork: what the two signs mean and why they were named that way, how big a coulomb actually is, and the masses and charges of the three particles everything else is built from.

Two signs, and why they are called that

Rubbing amber with fur makes it pick up small light objects — the Greeks knew it, and the Greek for amber, ἠλεκτρον, is where electron comes from. G. Johnstone Stoney coined the term in 1891.

Two different kinds of charge turned up, and Benjamin Franklin named them positive and negative rather than, say, A and B. That choice is doing real work:

(+5) + (−5) = 0
The names are an arithmetic claim, not a label.  Calling them opposites means you add charges together to get the neutral total, with no special rules. Glass rubbed with silk comes out positive; amber rubbed with fur comes out negative. Those two pairs are the historical references everything else was compared against.

How much is a coulomb?

1 eThe elementary charge1.60 × 10−19 C
1 COne coulomb, in electrons6.25 × 1018 electrons
1 μCOne microcoulomb1 × 10−6 C
Three different things are written μ.  The metric prefix micro, the coefficient of friction, and the linear mass density of a string. They are unrelated, and this module uses the first one. From here on, a μ next to a C is a factor of 10−6.

The three particles

ParticleMass (kg)Charge (C)
Electron9.11 × 10−31−e = −1.60 × 10−19
Proton1.673 × 10−27+e = +1.60 × 10−19
Neutron1.675 × 10−270
Two numbers to carry, and they are the ones that make the physics of this module work out.
  •  A proton is about 1,800 times heavier than an electron, while the proton and neutron are within a fraction of a per cent of each other.
  •  The proton and electron charges are exactly equal and opposite — not approximately. That is why ordinary matter is neutral to an extraordinary precision, and it is what lets you count charge in whole numbers of e.
