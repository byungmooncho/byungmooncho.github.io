Fields by Integration: the Line of Charge and the Ring

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The ideaA real wire is not a point charge, so Coulomb’s law does not apply to it as written. The way through is always the same: cut the object into pieces small enough to be point charges, add up their contributions as vectors, and let the sum become an integral. This page runs that method all the way through for a straight wire, takes the two limits worth knowing, and does the ring the same way.

The method, in four moves

Every charge-distribution problem in this module is these four steps.
  1.  Cut the object into pieces dq small enough to count as point charges.
  2.  Write dq in terms of a charge density and a length, area or volume element.
  3.  Find the component that does not cancel, and argue away the one that does.
  4.  Integrate over the whole object — and get the limits right.

Step 3 is where the work is saved and step 4 is where it is lost.
λLinear charge density, Q/LC/m
σSurface charge density, Q/AC/m²
ρVolume charge density, Q/VC/m³

A straight wire, opposite its middle

A wire of length L carries total charge Q, spread evenly. What is the field a distance x out from the middle?

dEx = 14πε0 λ dyr² cos θ

Two variables, one integral — so one of them has to go. The substitution that clears it is y = x tan θ, which gives dy = x dθ/cos² θ and r = x/cos θ. Everything collapses:

dEx = 14πε0 λx cos θ dθ
That is the whole trick, and it is worth seeing why it works.  Both r² in the denominator and dy in the numerator carry factors of 1/cos² θ, and they cancel against each other. What is left integrates in one line.

The limits are angles now, not lengths: the wire ends at θmax = tan−1[(L/2)/x] and at −θmax below.

Ex = 14πε0 λx [sin θ]+θmax−θmax = 14πε0 2λ sin θmaxx
E = Q4πε0 x √(x² + L²/4)

The two limits

Close in (x ≪ L), θmax → 90° and sin θmax → 1. The wire might as well be endless:

E = λ2πε0 x = 2kλx    (infinite line)

Far out (x ≫ L), the square root is just x and the whole thing becomes kQ/x². A point charge, as it has to be.

“Infinite” is a statement about where you are standing, not about the wire.  A 30 cm rod looks infinite from 1 mm away and looks like a point charge from across the room. Nothing in the problem tells you which case you are in except the ratio x/L.

Off the middle: when nothing cancels

Move the field point away from the plane through the wire’s centre and the symmetry argument in Figure 2 fails — the slice above is no longer the mirror of the slice below. Both components survive, and both have to be integrated, separately.

Ex = ∫ dE cos θ    Ey = ∫ dE sin θ

The integrals are no harder — the same substitution works — there are simply two of them, and the limits are no longer symmetric. The lesson is that the cancellation in Figure 2 was a gift of the geometry, not a general rule, and it is worth being able to say out loud why it applied.

The ring, on its axis

This section is not in the decks.  The deck named Electric Field of a Ring of Charge contains the line-of-charge derivation instead, slide for slide — the file appears to be a mis-saved copy. The ring result is quoted on the Common Electric Fields reference page and used again for the potential of a ring, so it is derived here.

A ring of radius R carries Q spread evenly. Take a point on the axis, a distance z from the centre.

Each slice contributes k dq/r², and only the along-the-axis part survives — a factor cos θ = z/r. Since r is the same for every slice, it comes straight out of the integral and what is left is just ∫dq = Q:

E = kQz(z² + R²)3/2
WhereWhat the formula givesAnd it should be
z = 00zero — by symmetry, at the centre
z ≪ RkQz/R³linear in z — a restoring force
z ≫ RkQ/z²a point charge

The middle row is the interesting one and it is easy to miss. Near the centre the field is proportional to the displacement, so a charge of the opposite sign dropped through the ring oscillates — the same mathematics as a mass on a spring, out of a charged hoop.
