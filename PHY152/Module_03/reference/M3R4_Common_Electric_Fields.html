Common Electric Fields

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The ideaFive charge shapes turn up again and again, and for each one the field has already been worked out once and for all. This page is the lookup table: the shape, the formula, what every symbol means and what the field looks like. It is not a derivation — it is what you reach for when you have identified the geometry and want the answer.
Read the table by what the field does with distance.  That is the single most useful thing on this page, and it is the thing that is easy to get wrong from memory:
  •  a point falls off as 1/r²
  •  an infinite line falls off as 1/r
  •  an infinite sheet does not fall off at all.
Fewer dimensions of charge, faster the fall-off. A ring and a disc sit between these, and each one collapses to a case above it in the right limit.

1.  A point charge

E = k Qr² r̂
EElectric fieldN/C
kCoulomb constant, 8.99 × 109N·m²/C²
QChargeC
rDistance from the chargem

Everything else on this page is built from this one by adding up the contributions of a great many point charges. The other four results are the sums already done for you.

2.  An infinite line of charge

E = k 2λr r̂
EElectric fieldN/C
kCoulomb constantN·m²/C²
λLinear charge density — charge per metre of wireC/m
rPerpendicular distance from the wirem
“Infinite” means long compared with r.  A 2 m wire is effectively infinite 1 cm away from its middle, and not remotely infinite 10 m away, where it looks like a point. The formula is a statement about where you are standing, not about the wire.

3.  A thin sheet of charge

E = σ2ε0
EElectric fieldN/C
σArea charge density — charge per square metreC/m²
ε0Permittivity of free space, 8.85 × 10−12C²/(N·m²)

This is the one students disbelieve, and it is worth seeing why it is true. Move twice as far away and each patch of sheet contributes a quarter as much — but you can now “see” four times as much sheet. The two effects cancel exactly, and they go on cancelling forever, which is what infinite is doing in the name.

Watch the factor of two.  An isolated sheet gives σ/2ε0. The surface of a conductor gives σ/ε0, twice as much, because the field is zero on the inside and all of it comes out one face. Two oppositely charged plates give σ/ε0 between them for the same reason — the two sheets add there and cancel outside.

4.  On the axis of a charged ring

E = k q z(z² + r²)3/2 ẑ
EElectric field, on the axis onlyN/C
qTotal charge on the ringC
kCoulomb constantN·m²/C²
rRadius of the ringm
zDistance along the axis from the centrem
Two limits worth checking, because they check you.
  •  At the centre, z = 0, the numerator vanishes and so does the field — as it must, by symmetry.
  •  Far away, z ≫ r, the bracket becomes z³ and the whole thing collapses to kq/z². A ring seen from far enough away is a point charge.

5.  On the axis of a charged disc

E = σ2ε0 [ 1 − z√(z² + R²) ]
EElectric field, on the axis onlyN/C
σArea charge density on the discC/m²
ε0Permittivity of free spaceC²/(N·m²)
RRadius of the discm
zDistance along the axis from the centrem
This one sits between the sheet and the point, and reduces to each.
  •  Very close, z → 0, the bracket → 1 and the field is σ/2ε0 — exactly result 3.  Stand close enough to any flat charged surface and it looks infinite.
  •  Very far, z ≫ R, the bracket → R²/2z², and with q = σπR² the field becomes kq/z² — a point charge again.

All five at once

The two things to carry away.  First, every one of these is a sum of point charges that somebody has already done — which is why identifying the geometry is most of the work. Second, three of the five are only valid somewhere: the line and the sheet want you close to something large, and the ring and the disc hold on the axis and nowhere else. Quoting one of them off the axis is the commonest way to use this page wrongly.
