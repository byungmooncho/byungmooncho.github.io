The Doppler Effect

SynopsisWhen a source or a listener is moving, the frequency that arrives is not the frequency that left.  We work out the two cases separately and then write them as one formula.
Before you read on — predict

An ambulance is coming towards you with its siren at a steady pitch.  (1) Is the pitch you hear higher, lower, or the same as the pitch the driver hears?  (2) As it gets closer and closer, does the pitch you hear keep climbing?  (3) After it has passed, does the pitch keep falling?  Write down your three answers.  Two of these catch almost everybody.

Every note in this module so far has had the source and the listener sitting still in the medium.  Note 4b let the intensity fall off with distance, but the frequency that arrived was always the frequency that left.  Now let one of them move.

A moving observer

Start with the easier half: the source stays put and you move.  Nothing about the wave in space changes — the source is still sitting where it was, sending out a crest every T seconds.

The source is at rest, so the crests sit in space exactly as they always did.  The only thing that has changed is how quickly the observer runs into them.
  1. A source at rest sends out a crest every T seconds.  How far apart are the crests in space?
  2. Now you run towards the source at vo.  How fast do the crests go past you, and so how many do you meet each second?
  3. Write fo.  What changes if you run away instead?
1)  In one period a crest travels vT, so the crests are λ = vT = v/fs apart — and the source is not moving, so this does not change at all in what follows. 2)  Relative to you the crests now approach at v + vo.  The number you meet per second is that speed divided by the distance between them: fo = (v + vo)/λ 3)  Substituting λ = v/fs: fo = fs (v + vo)/v Running away reverses the sign: fo = fs(v − vo)/v.  — towards raises it, away lowers it. Hold on to that sentence; it is the whole sign convention in the end

A moving source

Now the other half, and it is physically a different thing even though the formula will come out looking symmetric.  This time you stand still and the source moves while it is sounding — so each crest leaves from a different place.

Each circle is a crest, drawn from the point the source was at when it left, and every one has been spreading at v ever since.  Ahead of the source they are squeezed together; behind, stretched apart.  Drawn with vs = v/2 so you can see it — a real ambulance is nearer v/10, and the effect is correspondingly smaller.
  1. In one period the wave travels vT, and the source itself travels vsT.  So how far apart are the crests ahead of the source?
  2. How fast do those crests travel towards you?  Turn the new wavelength into the frequency you hear.
  3. And behind the source?
4)  The source chases its own crest and eats into the gap: λ′ = vT − vsT = (v − vs)/fs The wavelength itself has changed.  — that is what makes this case different from the first one, where the wave in space was untouched and only your crossing rate changed 5)  They still travel at v: the medium sets the speed of a sound wave, not whatever is making it.  So fo = v/λ′ = fs v/(v − vs) 6)  Behind it the gap is stretched instead, λ′ = (v + vs)/fs, giving fo = fsv/(v + vs).  — towards raises it, away lowers it: the same sentence again

Both at once

  1. Put the two results into a single formula, and say what decides each sign.
7)  The observer’s motion acts on the numerator and the source’s on the denominator, so both at once gives fo = fs [ (v ± vo) / (v ∓ vs) ] with v the speed of sound, and + : O moving towards     − : S moving towards If you ever cannot remember which way round the signs go, you do not have to: whichever one is moving towards the other, the frequency goes up.  Pick the sign that makes fo bigger.  — the top sign and the bottom sign are opposite for exactly this reason: a plus on top and a minus underneath both make the fraction larger
The punchline fo = fs [ (v ± vo) / (v ∓ vs) ]

v = speed of sound.  + : O moving towards.   − : S moving towards.
Notice what is not in it: distance.  How far away the ambulance is makes no difference to the pitch at all — only how fast it is closing on you.  That is the single most useful thing on this page, and it is also the thing almost everyone gets wrong the first time.

Looking ahead

That closes Module 1.  You started with a wave on a string in a simulation and a ruler, and you finish holding the equation of motion for a mechanical wave, the condition that picks out a guitar’s notes, the energy an oscillation carries, the way that energy thins with distance, and what happens to all of it when things start moving.

Where this sits. Notes 1 to 3 built the wave, Note 4 trapped it, Notes 4a and 4b followed its energy out into the room, and this note moves the furniture. One thread runs through every one of them: the medium sets the speed. It set v = √(T/μ) in Note 3, it decided which wavelengths fit in Note 4, it carried the intensity outward in Note 4b, and here it is again — the crests travel at v whatever the source is doing, which is exactly why a moving source has to change the wavelength instead.
Back to your prediction

(1) Higher than the driver hears.  The driver is moving with the siren, so for her nothing is approaching anything.  (2) No — it stays constant.  Look at the formula: there is no r in it.  At a steady speed towards you the pitch is steady and high, however close it gets.  (3) No — also constant, and low.  The entire change happens during the pass, not before or after it.  (Strictly, what counts is the part of vs pointing along the line to you, which sweeps from +vs to −vs as it goes by — so the drop is a fast slide rather than a step, and at the instant it is level with you, you hear its true pitch.)
