3.  Power in Waves

Note 4a counted the energy an oscillator carries.  This note follows that energy outwards: how fast it leaves the source, how thinly it is spread by the time it reaches you, and why we end up measuring it on a logarithmic scale.

  1. Write power and intensity as definitions, and give the SI unit of each.
  2. A source radiates Psource equally in every direction.  At a distance r, what area is that power spread over?  Write I(r).
  3. Write I1 and I2 at two distances and form the ratio I1/I2.  What cancels, and what does that tell you?
  4. Intensity turns out to go as the square of the amplitude.  Given E = ½kA² for a sinusoidal wave, why is that the relationship you should have expected?
1)  Power = Energy / time, measured in W.    Intensity = Power / Area, measured in W/m².  — intensity is the one your ear, your skin and a solar panel actually respond to: power alone says nothing until you say how much area it is crossing 2)  Equally in every direction means the power is spread over a whole sphere, and the area of a sphere of radius r is 4πr².  So I(r) = Psource / 4πr² 3)  I1 = Psource/4πr1² and I2 = Psource/4πr2², so dividing one by the other I1 / I2 = r2² / r1² Psource and the 4π both cancel.  — so you can compare two places without ever knowing how loud the source is, which is what makes this usable in practice 4)  E = ½kA² — the energy of an oscillation goes as the square of its amplitude — and intensity is just that energy delivered per second per square metre.  Nothing in the journey changes the A², so I ∝ A² Putting the last two together: if the intensity falls by four, the amplitude falls by two.
The same power crosses both spheres — nothing is absorbed, nothing leaks out of the sides.  The only thing that changed between r1 and r2 is how much area it had to cross, and area goes as r².
The punchline I = Psource / 4πr²

The inverse-square law, and notice what it is not: nothing here is being used up.  The source puts out the same power whether you stand near it or far from it; all that happens is that the power gets spread over a bigger and bigger sphere.  Every inverse-square law in physics — sound, light, gravity, the electric field — is this same piece of geometry, which is why the exponent is exactly 2 and not 1.9.

Sound intensity level (decibel)

A quiet room and a jet engine differ in intensity by about a factor of a trillion.  No linear scale is going to show both ends of that comfortably, so sound is measured logarithmically, against a fixed reference.

  1. Write the definition of the sound intensity level in decibels, and say what the reference I0 is.
  2. What does an increase of 10 dB mean, in terms of intensity?  And what level does a sound at the reference intensity itself have?
5)  dB = (10 dB) log10(I/I0), with the reference I0 = 1 × 10−12 W/m² which is about the faintest sound a healthy ear can detect.  — it is a chosen reference, not a law of nature; the decibel is a ratio, so it only means something once you say a ratio to what 6)  Adding 10 dB adds 1 to log10(I/I0), so it multiplies the intensity by 10.  Every 10 dB is another factor of ten. And at I = I0 the ratio is 1, log101 = 0, so the level is 0 dB.  — 0 dB is not silence. It is "exactly at the threshold", and a quieter sound has a negative level
dB = (10 dB) log10(I / I0)

Looking ahead

Everything so far has had the source and the listener standing still.  Note 5 sets them moving: when a source or an observer is travelling, the frequency that arrives is not the frequency that left.

Where this sits. Note 4a found the energy stored in one oscillator and it went as A². This note sends that energy out into space, and the A² survives the trip: intensity goes as amplitude squared at every distance. The 1/r² is not physics on top of that — it is the geometry of a sphere, and it would be true of anything spreading evenly in three dimensions.
Back to your prediction

(1) The intensity drops to a quarter.  I ∝ 1/r², so doubling r divides I by 4 — while the power of the loudspeaker has not changed at all.  (2) The amplitude halves.  I ∝ A², so a factor of 4 in intensity is a factor of 2 in amplitude.  (3) Down 6 dB — (10 dB) log10(¼) = −6.02 dB.  Every doubling of distance costs you 6 dB, whatever the source and whatever the starting level, which is the one number from this note worth remembering by itself.
