2.  Energy in waves (just SHM)

The block on the spring from Note 2, caught somewhere between the turning points.  It swings between x = −A and x = +A, and at the instant drawn it is at x, moving.

Nothing is lost to friction here, so whatever energy you put in by pulling the block back is still there at every later instant.  It just moves between two accounts: the spring’s and the block’s.

  1. Write the total energy of the block at a general position x, in terms of m, v, k and x.
  2. At x = ±A the block is momentarily at rest.  What is E there?
  3. At x = 0 the spring is relaxed.  What is E there?
  4. Those are the same E.  Set them equal and get vmax.
  5. From Note 2 you know vmax a second way, in terms of ω and A.  Write it, equate the two, and read off ω.  Then write f and T.
1)  total energy E = K + U = ½mv² + ½kx² 2)  v = 0 at the turning points, so all of it is in the spring: E(at x = ±A) = U = ½kA² 3)  x = 0 at the centre, so the spring holds none of it: E(at x = 0) = K = ½mvmax² 4)  And therefore ½mvmax² = ½kA², so vmax = √(k/m) A 5)  But we also know that vmax = ωA = 2πfA = 2πA/T.  Equating the two expressions, the A cancels: ω = √(k/m),    f = (1/2π)√(k/m),    T = 2π√(m/k) — this is the same ω Note 2 got by writing Newton's second law and solving the equation of motion. Here it falls out of one conservation statement, with no differential equation anywhere. Two routes to one number is how you know the number belongs to the system rather than to the method
The same three quantities, plotted against position.  E is flat — that is what conservation looks like on a graph.  U and K are two halves of it, and they trade places twice a swing.
The punchline ω = √(k/m)

The angular frequency comes out of the spring and the mass, and nothing else.  Not the amplitude, not how you started it, not where it is in the swing.  Pull it back twice as far and it still takes exactly as long to come back — which is why a pendulum clock keeps time as its swing dies away, and why ω is worth writing in a box.

v at any position

The last step gave the speed at one point of the swing.  The same conservation statement gives it everywhere, because the energy at the turning point is the energy at every other point too.

  1. Write “the energy at x = ±A equals the energy at a general x”, and solve it for v.
  2. Rewrite that with ω in place of √(k/m).  Check it at x = 0 and at x = ±A.
6)  ½kA² = ½mv² + ½kx² ½mv² = ½kA² − ½kx² Multiply through by 2/m, and the ½ and the m go: v² = (k/m)(A² − x²) v = √(k/m) √(A² − x²) 7)  and √(k/m) is just ω: v = ω√(A² − x²) At x = 0 that is ωA, the top speed.  At x = ±A it is zero.  — one formula for the whole swing, and the square root is why the speed falls off slowly near the middle and then very fast near the ends
v(x) = ω√(A² − x²)

Looking ahead

You now have the energy an oscillator carries and how fast it is moving at every point of its swing.  Note 4b asks what happens when that energy leaves: how fast it goes out, how it thins as it spreads, and how we end up measuring it in decibels.

Where this sits. Note 2 got ω = √(k/m) by writing Newton's second law for the block and solving the equation of motion that came out. This note gets the identical result from a single sentence about energy, with no calculus at all. Both are worth having: the first says why the motion is sinusoidal, the second says what is conserved while it happens.
Back to your prediction

(1) Fastest at the centre, x = 0.  That is the one point where the spring holds none of the energy, so the block holds all of it.  (2) No — and this is the one worth being wrong about.  At x = A/2, v = ω√(A² − A²/4) = (√3/2)ωA ≈ 87% of the top speed.  Energy goes as x², so at half the displacement only a quarter of it has gone into the spring and three quarters is still motion.  (3) Yes, exactly double.  vmax = ωA is linear in A, and ω does not depend on A at all — so twice the pull gives twice the speed in the same period.
