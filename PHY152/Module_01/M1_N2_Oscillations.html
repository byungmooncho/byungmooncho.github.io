Oscillations

Synopsis

Repetitive/cyclical disturbances propagating through space and time are waves.  Some waves are familiar: Ocean waves, waves on a string, and sound waves are disturbances that travel from a place and time of creation to an observer.  Mathematically, cosinusoidal functions encapsulate such disturbances, i.e., some combinations of sines and cosines.  For example, we can write the amplitude of ocean waves as a function of space and time:

(1)A(x, t) = A0 sin(kx − ωt)

where A is the amplitude, A0 is the maximum amplitude, k is the wave number (1/m), and ω is the angular frequency (rad/s).  This is a moving wave traveling along the x-axis.  The wave number, k, and angular frequency, ω, are parameters fixed by the wavelength and period (thus frequency) of the moving wave, respectively.  This is a general expression for a moving wave, and we will delve into this equation further shortly.

Before you read on — predict

Hang a mass on a spring, let it settle, then pull it down a little further and release it.  Gravity is pulling on that mass the whole time.  So when you write down the equation that governs the oscillation, does g appear in it?  Commit to yes or no in your guided notes.  The answer is the whole point of the derivation below, and most people get it wrong the first time.

Instructions and Questions

For now, we examine a simpler system: Spring-mass oscillation.  When you hang a mass on a spring, stretch, and let go, the spring-mass system oscillates back and forth.  If you measured the amplitude of this disturbance as a function of time, i.e., stretching and compression of the spring as a function of time, you see that the following function can describe such disturbance:

(2)A(t) = A0 sin(ωt)

Here, you see that the sine is only a function of time, and the kx term is not in the sine function argument.  This is simply because the spring-mass system is not moving, i.e., the setup is fixed in space.  So, you can see that this is a simpler system.

The spring-mass oscillation is known as simple harmonic motion (SHM).  SHMs are ubiquitous.  Let’s look at the physics that underpins SHM.  The idea is simple: When a spring is stretched or compressed, a “restoring” force tries to pull it back to its natural length.  The further the spring is stretched/compressed, the larger the restoring force.  In other words, the larger the displacement, the larger the restoring force.  Both the displacement and force are vector quantities.  Notice that this restoring force, therefore, always points in the opposite direction to the displacement.  Hooke’s law describes the relationship between the displacement and the restoring force:

(3)Fk = −ksx′

Where Fk is the restoring force (N, the subscript k represents that the force has to do with a spring), ks is the spring constant, and x′ is the displacement (see the figure below).  Don’t confuse this ks, the spring constant, with k, the wavenumber.

Imagine a mass hanging at one end of a spring.  The spring will elongate and settle at a length.  Here, Fk = Fg, where Fg is the weight of the mass.  Let this elongation be x0, so x0 = L − L0, where L0 is the natural length, and L is the length when mass is hung.  At this point, the mass is not oscillating.

Now, if we pull the mass further down (or push it up) and let go, the mass oscillates.  This oscillation is a simple harmonic motion.  We will analyze the forces below and derive Eq. (2) by solving the equation of motion.

The three states the derivation needs.  Down is positive.  x0 is how far the mass stretches the spring just by hanging there; x is how much further you pull it; and the spring itself only ever knows the total, x′ = x0 + x.

First, the mass hanging at rest

  1. Write Newton’s second law for the middle picture, where the mass hangs without oscillating.  What is Fnet, and what does that tell you about Fk and Fg?
  2. Put Hooke’s law and the weight into that statement and solve for the stretch x0.
1)  It is not accelerating, so Fnet = 0 = ΣF = Fk + Fg, and therefore Fk = Fg — the spring is holding up exactly the weight. 2)  ksx0 = mg, so 0 = mg − ksx0. — keep it in this form. It is the line that does the work in step 4, and it is easy to miss that you already have it.

Now pull it down and let go

  1. Write Newton’s second law for the right-hand picture.  The spring is now stretched by the total x′ = x0 + x, so what is the new spring force F′k?  What is Fg?
  2. Substitute both into ma = ΣF and expand the bracket.  Two of the three terms cancel.  Which two, and what lets you cancel them?
3)  Fnet = ma = ΣF = F′k + Fg, with F′k = −k(x0 + x) and Fg = mg.  — down is positive, so the weight is + and the spring force is − 4)  ma = −k(x0 + x) + mg = −kx0 − kx + mg.     The −kx0 and the +mg cancel, because step 2 says exactly that: mg − ksx0 = 0.  What is left is ma = −kx — and that is the answer to the prediction at the top: g is gone. Hanging the mass moved the rest position down by x0 and did nothing else. Measure x from the new rest position and gravity never appears again.
The punchline ma = −kx

The acceleration — and so the force — is proportional to the displacement, and points the opposite way.  That one sentence is the definition of simple harmonic motion.  Any system you can get into this form will oscillate, and it will oscillate as a sine.  That is why Eq. (2) looks the way it does, and it is why Note 2a can run the same argument on a pendulum and get the same answer.

With calculus

Writing it as an equation of motion

ma = −kx says the force is proportional to the displacement.  To get from there to the sine of Eq. (2) rather than merely asserting it, write the acceleration as what it actually is — the second derivative of position.  One subtlety: the position of the mass is x′, not x.

  1. Start from a = d²x′/dt² with x′ = x0 + x, and show that it equals d²x/dt².  Say which property of x0 you used.
  2. Write the result of step 4 as a differential equation, and say what kind of function could satisfy it.
5)  a = d²x′/dt² = d²(x0 + x)/dt² = d²x0/dt² + d²x/dt², and the first term is zero because x0 is a constant — the rest position does not move.  So a = d²x/dt².  — the same cancellation as step 4, in a different costume: the constant offset drops out of both 6)  Substituting into step 4: m d²x/dt² = −kx A function whose second derivative is a negative constant times itself.  Sine and cosine are exactly that — differentiate sin ωt twice and you get −ω² sin ωt.  So Eq. (2) is not a guess, it is the solution, and matching the two expressions will hand you ω in terms of k and m.  Note 2a does that matching.

Check yourself

  1. A mass hangs at rest on a spring, stretching it by 4.0 cm.  You pull it down a further 3.0 cm.  By how much is the spring stretched at that moment, and what is the displacement that appears in ma = −kx?
  2. Two students derive the equation of motion.  One measures displacement from the spring’s natural length, the other from the hanging rest position.  One of them ends up with a g in the answer.  Which one, and is that student wrong?
  3. In one sentence: why does a stiffer spring make the oscillation faster?  Argue it from ma = −kx alone.
7)  The spring is stretched 7.0 cm — that is x′.  The displacement in ma = −kx is 3.0 cm, measured from the hanging rest position.  Confusing the two is the single most common error in this derivation. 8)  The one measuring from the natural length keeps a g.  No, that student is not wrong — the physics is identical and the equation is harder to read, because the rest position of the oscillation is no longer at zero. Choosing the origin at the hanging rest position is what makes the g disappear, and that is a choice, not a law. 9)  A larger k means a larger restoring force for the same displacement, so a larger acceleration back toward the middle, so less time to get there and back.  — and the same sentence run the other way says a heavier mass oscillates more slowly

Looking ahead

You have the shape of simple harmonic motion and the one sentence that defines it.  What you do not yet have is how fast it oscillates — nothing above gives a period or a frequency.  Note 2a gets it, by solving the equation of motion rather than describing it, and then runs the same argument twice more: on a pendulum, where the restoring force comes from gravity rather than a spring, and on a damped oscillator, where the amplitude dies away.

Where this sits. This is Module 3's F = ma applied to one particular force law, and nothing more. Everything characteristic of oscillation comes from the minus sign in Hooke's law — that the force points back toward where you came from.
Back to your prediction

No, g does not appear.  It is in the derivation right up until the second-to-last line and then cancels, against the stretch it caused in the first place.  Hanging the mass moved the rest position down by x0; it did not change how the system oscillates about that position.  A spring-mass oscillator on the Moon, hung from the same spring, has the same period.
