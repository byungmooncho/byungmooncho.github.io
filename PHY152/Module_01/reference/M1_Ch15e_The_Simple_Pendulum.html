The Simple Pendulum, with worked examples

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea A pendulum is not obviously a harmonic oscillator: the restoring force goes as sin θ, not as the displacement itself. For small swings, though, sin θ and θ are the same number to within a per cent, and once that substitution is made the pendulum turns into a spring with an effective stiffness of mg/L. Everything already proved about springs then applies — including the period, which depends on the length and on g, and not on the mass.
You already know this Free-body diagrams and resolving a force into components, arc length s = rθ in radians, and from the earlier pages F = −kx, ω = √(k/m) and T = 1/f.

Analysing a simple pendulum

Two forces act on the bob: the tension in the string and the force of gravity. The tension points along the string, so it can do nothing to move the bob along its arc. Resolving gravity into a component along the string and a component along the path, the piece along the string is cancelled by the tension and the net force is just the component of Fg along the path.

The restoring force
Fnet = −Fg sin θ = −mg sin θ

The minus sign says the force points back towards θ = 0. This is a restoring force — but it is not proportional to the displacement, because sin θ is not θ. As written, this is not a simple harmonic oscillator at all.

The small-angle approximation

Where the approximation comes from
sin θ = θ − θ33! + θ55! − θ77! + ···
for small θ:   sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ   (radians only)

Every term after the first carries θ cubed or higher. Make θ small and those terms fall away far faster than the first one does, so the series is just θ to a good approximation. Radians only — the series is written for radians and is simply false in degrees.

How good is it?

15° is 0.2618 rad. How far off is the approximation there?

sin 0.2618 rad = 0.2588, against θ = 0.2618 — about 1% high. At 30° the error is near 5%, which is usually where it stops being acceptable.

From the arc to a spring constant

Rewriting the restoring force as F = −kx
s = rθ = Lθ  →  θ = sL ≈ sin θ
Fnet ≈ −mg sL = −mgLs
compare   F = −kx   →   k ≈ mgL

Two substitutions did all the work: the arc length turned the angle into a displacement, and the small-angle approximation turned sin θ into that same displacement divided by L. What is left has exactly the shape of Hooke’s law, with everything that is not s playing the part of k.

Comparing the two potential energies

Elastic against gravitational
Uspr = ½kx2
Upen = ½ mgL (L sin θ)2
Ug = mgh = mg(L − L cos θ)

The middle line is the spring expression with the pendulum’s effective k and its horizontal displacement; the last is the honest gravitational potential, found from how far the bob has risen. They are not the same function — but plot them and they sit on top of each other near θ = 0 and only separate well outside the small-angle range. Trevor’s chart makes the point at 20° = 0.349 rad, where they still agree.

The period of a simple pendulum

PendulumSpring
Restoring force Fnet ≈ −(mg/L)s Fnet = −ks
Effective stiffness k = mg/L k
Angular frequency ω = √(mg/mL) = √(g/L) ω = √(k/m)
Period T = 2π√(L/g) T = 2π√(m/k)
The mass cancels It goes into ω = √(mg/mL) and comes straight back out. The period of a pendulum does not depend on the mass of the bob — it depends on the length and on the local g. That is the opposite of the spring, whose period depends on the mass and not at all on g. Any pendulum mass quoted in a problem is there to be left unused.

Worked example 1 — finding the length

A simple pendulum has a mass of 2.00 kg and a period of 2.50 s. What is its length?
given:  T = 2.50 s,  m = 2.00 kg,  g = 9.80 m/s2   find L
T = 2π√(L/g)  →  T2 = 4π2L/g  →  L = T24π2g
L = (2.50 s)24π2 × 9.80 m/s2 = 1.55148 m
L = 1.55 m

The 2.00 kg is never used. Rearranging before substituting is worth the extra line: squaring both sides first is what gets L out from under the root.

Worked example 2 — the same pendulum on the moon

A pendulum of mass 0.75 kg has a period of 1.25 s on the earth. What is its period on the moon?
given:  T = 1.25 s,  ge = 9.80 m/s2,  gm = 1.622 m/s2
on earth:  L = (1.25 s)24π2 × 9.80 m/s2 = 0.38787 m ≈ 0.39 m
on the moon:  T = 2π√(0.38787 m / 1.622 m/s2) = 3.0725 s
T = 3.07 s

The length is the bridge. It is the one thing that travels unchanged, so the route is always earth → length → moon. Carry the unrounded 0.38787 m into the second step and round once, at the end. Weaker gravity, slower swing — about two and a half times the period, which is √(9.80/1.622).

Worked example 3 — a spring-mass system in two places

A 0.500 kg mass on a spring oscillates at 12.2 Hz. Find (a) the period, (b) the spring constant, (c) the period on the moon.
(a)  T = 1/f = 1 / (12.2 s−1) = 0.08197 s = 0.0820 s
(b)  √(k/m) = 2πf  →  k/m = 4π2f2  →  k = 4π2f2m
k = 4π2(12.2 Hz)2(0.500 kg) = 2937.98 kg/s2 = 2940 N/m
(c)  T = 2π√(m/k) — no g appears, so T = 0.0820 s on the moon as well.

kg/s2 and N/m are the same unit: N = kg·m/s2, so N/m = kg/s2. Part (c) is the whole point of the problem. A spring-mass system keeps its period anywhere; a pendulum does not, because its restoring force is gravity.

In short

  • The net force on a pendulum bob is the component of gravity along the arc; the tension and the other component cancel.
  • The small-angle approximation, in radians, turns sin θ into θ and makes the pendulum a simple harmonic oscillator with k = mg/L.
  • T = 2π√(L/g) — length and local gravity, never the mass.
  • Pendulums need a gravitational field; a properly designed spring-mass system has the same period anywhere.
  • Several equations give a period. Do not mix them up: check whether the one in your hand has a g in it.
