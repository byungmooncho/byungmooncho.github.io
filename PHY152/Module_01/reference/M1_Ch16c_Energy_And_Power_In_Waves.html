Energy and Power in Waves

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea A wave moves energy, and this page works out how much. Take one small piece of the string, find its kinetic and potential energy as an oscillator, and add up every piece along one wavelength. Both halves come out the same, the total depends on the square of the amplitude and the square of the frequency, and dividing it by the period gives the power. The one trap is that a power graph repeats twice as fast as the wave does.
You already know this y = A sin(kx − ωt) and μ = m/L from the previous pages, ½mv2 and ½ksy2 from the oscillations pages, and enough calculus to integrate a sin2.
Two different k’s Do not confuse the wave number k = 2π/λ with the spring constant ks. They appear in the same lines on this page and they are unrelated: one is radians per metre, the other newtons per metre. The subscript is doing real work here, and dropping it is the commonest way to lose this derivation.

Setting up: one wave, one small piece of it

The position and the velocity of that piece
y(x,t) = A sin(kx − ωt)   k = 2π/λ,  ω = 2πf
vy = dydt = A cos(kx − ωt)(−ω) = −ωA cos(kx − ωt)

The derivative is with respect to t at a fixed x, which is what makes it the speed of the string and not the speed of the wave. The −ω falls out of the chain rule, and the maximum transverse speed is ωA — the same Aω the oscillator pages produced, because each piece of string is an oscillator.

Kinetic energy, piece by piece

From ½mv2 to a differential
K = ½Δm vy2    μ = mL = ΔmΔx  →  Δm = μΔx
ΔK = ½(μΔx) vy2
dK = ½(μ dx) {−ωA cos(kx − ωt)}2
dK = ½(μ dx) ω2A2 cos2(kx − ωt)

μ is what turns a length into a mass. That one substitution, Δm = μΔx, is what lets an energy be integrated along the string — without it there is nothing to integrate over. Squaring kills the minus sign, which is why the energy does not care which way the piece happens to be moving.

Adding up one wavelength — a little bit of calculus
Kλ = ∫0λ dK = ∫0λ ½(μ dx) ω2A2 cos2(kx − ωt)
Kλ = ½μω2A2 ∫0λ cos2(kx − ωt) dx
Kλ = ¼μω2A2λ

Everything except the cos2 is constant along the string at a fixed instant, so it comes outside. The integral of cos2 over exactly one cycle is half the interval — that is where the ½ becomes a ¼, and it is the only piece of calculus in the whole derivation.

Potential energy, the same way

Each piece of string is a little spring
U = ½ksy2    with   ks = mω2  (from the oscillations chapter)
dU = ½ks {A sin(kx − ωt)}2
dU = ½ks A2 sin2(kx − ωt)
dU = ½(μ dx) ω2A2 sin2(kx − ωt)
Uλ = ∫0λ dU = ¼μω2A2λ

The last substitution is the one to watch: ks = mω2 with m = μ dx for this piece, so ks = (μ dx)ω2. After that the potential integral is the kinetic one with a sin2 in place of a cos2 — and over one full cycle those integrate to the same thing. The two energies come out equal.

The total, and the power

Energy in one wavelength, and the rate it goes past
Eλ = Uλ + Kλ = ¼μω2A2λ + ¼μω2A2λ
Eλ = ½μω2A2λ
Pλ(ave) = EλT = ½μω2A2 λT = ½μω2A2vw

λ/T is the wave speed, which is the only reason vw appears in a power equation. One wavelength of energy goes past any point in one period, so energy per wavelength divided by period is energy per second.

Kinetic in one λ¼μω2A2λμ, ω2, A2
Potential in one λ¼μω2A2λ

Average power½μω2A2vwthe same, times the wave speed
Both squares matter Double the amplitude and the power goes up four times. Double the frequency and it also goes up four times, because ω is squared too. This is the same amplitude-squared rule the oscillator pages reached for E = ½kA2, and it is why a loud sound costs so much more energy than a slightly quieter one.

Reading a power graph

The power carries a square of the transverse velocity, and squaring a sinusoid halves its period. So in a window of one wave period you see the power pattern twice. Trevor states it bluntly, and it is worth repeating:

Be careful how you count

The power graph above shows three peaks across the window. Is the period 2.0 s, 1.0 s, or 0.67 s?

The wave’s period is 2.0 s. The power pattern repeats every 1.0 s, which is half of it, and 0.67 s is what you get by dividing the window by the number of peaks — the mistake the figure is drawn to prevent. Count periods, not peaks, and read the period off the position graph whenever you have one.

Two more warnings go with it. The power equation uses the speed of the wave, not the speed of a segment — vw, never vy. And the power equation is an average: on the graph above the peak power is 100.0 W but the average is 50.0 W, and it is the 50.0 W that ½μω2A2vw predicts. Reading the peak off the graph overstates the answer by a factor of two, every time.

In short

  • The energy within one wavelength is equally divided between kinetic and potential.
  • Eλ = ½μω2A2λ, and the power through one wavelength is Pλ = ½μω2A2vw.
  • Power goes as the square of both the amplitude and the frequency.
  • A power graph repeats twice as often as the wave, and the formula gives the average, not the peak.
  • Be careful with variables that have more than one meaning — especially k.
