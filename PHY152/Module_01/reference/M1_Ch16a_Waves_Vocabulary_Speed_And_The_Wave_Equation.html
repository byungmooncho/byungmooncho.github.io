Waves: Vocabulary, Speed and the Wave Equation

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea A wave carries energy from one place to another without carrying the material with it. The medium goes up and down, or back and forth, and returns to where it started; only the pattern moves. Almost everything on this page follows from keeping those two motions apart — the speed of the wave and the speed of a bit of the medium are different numbers, and they are not even measured in the same picture.
You already know this Period and frequency from the oscillations pages, where f = 1/T and ω = 2πf. A wave is what you get when a whole line of oscillators is coupled together, each one started a little later than the one before.

The words

Pulsea single disturbance travelling through a medium — one flick of a rope.
Wavea repeated disturbance; a train of pulses one after another.
Mediumthe stuff the disturbance travels through. The medium does not travel with it.
Transversethe medium vibrates at right angles to the direction the wave moves.
Longitudinalthe medium vibrates parallel to the direction the wave moves.
Amplitude, Amaximum displacement of the medium from equilibrium.
Wavelength, λthe distance over which the pattern repeats — crest to crest.
Period, Tthe time for one full cycle to pass a fixed point.
Frequency, fcycles per second; f = 1/T, measured in hertz.

Two pictures of the same wave

Check the axis before you read the width Both graphs are sinusoids and they look identical. The only way to tell what a horizontal distance on one of them means is to read the axis label. A width on the position axis is a wavelength, in metres; the same-looking width on the time axis is a period, in seconds. Nearly every early mistake with waves is a width read off the wrong graph.

Period and frequency

Stand in one spot and count the crests going past in a time t. Trevor's wave sends one crest by every 0.5 seconds, so:

Counting crests
f = 1 cycle0.5 seconds = 2 cycles1 second = 2 hertz
f = 1/T     T = time for one cycle

Period and frequency are the same fact counted two ways: seconds per cycle, or cycles per second. One hertz is one per second, and nothing more.

Wave speed against particle speed

These two charts carry the whole distinction. The first is all positions at one time — a photograph. The second is one position at all times — a film of a single point. The wave moves forward; the particle moves up and down and gets nowhere.

The speed of a wave

A wave travels at a constant speed v unless it changes medium. To find that speed, pick a spot and watch one whole wave go by: the distance that passes is one wavelength, and the time it takes is one period.

v = fλ
v = dt = λT = fλ
his wave:  v = (2 Hz)(1.0 m) = 2 m/s

The middle step is where T turns into f: dividing by T is the same as multiplying by 1/T. The speed belongs to the medium, not to the source — so if you change the frequency, the wavelength changes to keep fλ the same.

Writing the wave down

From the two graphs to one equation
y(x,t) = A sin( 2πλx − 2πft )
y(x,t) = A sin(kx − ωt)
k = 2πλ  the wave number    ω = 2πf  the angular frequency

A sine needs a pure number inside it, and x is in metres. k is what converts: its units are radians per metre, so kx is (radians/m)×(m) = radians. ω does the same job for the time term, in radians per second. Both are conversion factors, not new physics — which is why the second line is just shorthand for the first.

Why the minus sign The kx and the ωt have opposite signs because the pattern is moving forward. As t grows, you have to go further along in x to find the same value of kx − ωt — which is exactly what it means for a crest to travel. Flip the sign to a plus and the wave runs backwards.

Check yourself on his wave

From the two charts above

1. What is the wavelength?   2. What is the wave number?   3. What is the period?

1. 1.0 m — crest to crest on the position chart.   2. 2π radians per metre, since k = 2π/λ = 2π/(1.0 m).   3. 0.5 s — crest to crest on the time chart. Note that 2.5 is an offered answer for both the wavelength and the period, and is the full width of each chart rather than one cycle of anything.

Longitudinal waves, and sound

Sound is a longitudinal wave, and it travels through any medium — air, water, steel — at a speed set by that medium and by its temperature. For the calculations in this course, take the speed of sound in air as:

The speed of sound in air
vs ≈ 340 m/s   (about 770 miles per hour)

It is worth carrying the second number as a sanity check: a value that comes out at jetliner speed is probably right, and one that comes out at walking pace or at the speed of light is not. Everything about wavelength, frequency and speed on this page applies to sound unchanged — only the picture of the medium is different.

Trevor points to Dan Russell’s animated demonstrations of transverse and longitudinal motion, which are worth watching once: wave motion animations (Dan Russell, Pennsylvania State University; the kettering.edu address on his slide has since moved).

In short

  • A wave moves energy through a medium; the medium itself returns to where it started.
  • Transverse means the medium moves across the direction of travel; longitudinal means it moves along it. Sound is longitudinal.
  • λ is a width on the position graph; T is a width on the time graph. They are not interchangeable.
  • v = λ/T = fλ, and the speed belongs to the medium.
  • y = A sin(kx − ωt) with k = 2π/λ and ω = 2πf — both of them unit converters that turn metres and seconds into radians.
