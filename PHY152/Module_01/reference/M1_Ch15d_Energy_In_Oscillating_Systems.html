Energy in Oscillating Systems

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea An oscillator holds a fixed amount of energy and keeps moving it between two accounts. At the ends of the swing it is all potential; at the middle it is all kinetic; everywhere else it is some of each. The total never changes, and it turns out to depend only on the stiffness and the amplitude — not on the mass, and not on where in the cycle you look.
You already know this Elastic potential energy U = ½kx2 and kinetic energy K = ½mv2 from the first semester, and from the previous pages x = A cos(ωt), v = −Aω sin(ωt) and ω = √(k/m).

The two energies, and where x is measured from

The two accounts
U = ½kx2   with   x = A cos(ωt)
K = ½mv2   with   v = −Aω sin(ωt)

Each is the ordinary expression you already have; what makes this an oscillator problem is that the x and the v going into them are the two functions from the previous page, a quarter cycle out of step with each other. When one is at its maximum the other is zero, which is why the energy never sits in one account for long.

Conservation: the same energy, in a different account

Both ends of the swing are turning points: the block is momentarily at rest, so K = 0 and the whole energy is potential. At equilibrium it is moving fastest, so the potential term vanishes and the whole energy is kinetic. Those two statements are the useful ones in practice, because each lets you write the total energy with a single term.

Gravity is already paid for On a hanging spring, the gravitational and elastic potentials have been folded together by the choice of origin made on Ch15a: measuring from the equilibrium position rather than from the unstretched spring made gravity cancel out of the equation of motion. The same choice is what lets this page write a single ½kx2 and not carry an mgh alongside it.

The mix, position by position

Total energy at any instant
E = K + U = ½mv2 + ½kx2

The energy changes between potential and kinetic; the total stays put.

Why the total comes out so simple

Substituting the two functions and collecting
E = ½mA2ω2 sin2(ωt) + ½kA2 cos2(ωt)
E = ½kA2 sin2(ωt) + ½kA2 cos2(ωt)
E = ½kA2 { sin2(ωt) + cos2(ωt) }
E = ½kA2

The second line is the step worth watching: mω2 is k, because ω = √(k/m). Once both terms carry the same ½kA2, the Pythagorean identity sin2θ + cos2θ = 1 collapses them to one. The time dependence disappears entirely — which is exactly what “conserved” means.

The amplitude is squared, and that matters

WhereWhat is left of EValue
At the extremes, x = ±A all potential, K = 0 Umax = ½kA2
At equilibrium, x = 0 all kinetic, U = 0 Kmax = ½m(Aω)2 = ½kA2
Anywhere in betweensome of each K + U = ½kA2

Maximum kinetic energy, maximum potential energy and total energy are all the same number for a given oscillator. They have to be: each is the whole energy, counted at a moment when it all sits in one account.

Important note about energy

The total energy depends on the square of the amplitude. So what happens if you pull the block twice as far before releasing it?

The energy goes up by a factor of four, not two. Doubling the amplitude quadruples the energy in the system. The period, though, does not change at all — T = 2π√(m/k) has no A in it.

In short

  • Energy in an oscillating system changes back and forth between potential and kinetic, and the total is constant.
  • Maximum kinetic, maximum potential and total energy all have the same value for a given oscillator.
  • E = ½kA2 — it depends on stiffness and amplitude, and on nothing else.
  • Because the amplitude is squared, doubling it quadruples the energy.
