Sound Intensity and the Inverse Square Law

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea A source puts out a fixed number of watts. As the sound travels outward, those watts are spread over a larger and larger sphere, so the amount arriving on any given patch falls away — not because the source is weakening, but because the sound is getting thinner. The sphere grows as the square of the distance, so the intensity falls as one over the square of the distance.
You already know this Energy, work and power from the first semester, and from Ch16c that a wave carries power through the medium it travels in.

Three quantities, each the one before divided by something

Energythe ability to do work; measured in joules. W = Force × distance, so 1 J = 1 N·m.
Powerenergy used in a given time; measured in watts. P = Energy / time, so 1 W = 1 J/s.
Intensityenergy through an area in a given time; measured in watts per square metre. I = Power / area.

The ladder is worth reading twice. Each step divides by one more thing — first by time, then by area — and each step makes the quantity more local. Energy belongs to the whole event, power to the source, and intensity to the particular place you are standing.

A speaker’s “power” is not this power Shop for speakers and you will find them rated in watts. That number is the electrical power the speaker can handle without damage, not the acoustic power it radiates. The two differ by a lot: loudspeakers are only a few per cent efficient, so a 100 W speaker may put a watt or two of actual sound into the room. The P in the formulas below is the acoustic one.

Spreading out over a sphere

Intensity at a distance r
I = PowerArea = P4πr2

4πr2 is the surface area of a sphere, and it is the only thing in the expression that changes with distance. The source is isotropic — it radiates equally in all directions — so the power is shared out evenly over the whole sphere.

Comparing two distances without knowing the power
I 4πr2 = P
I1 4πr12 = P = I2 4πr22
I1r12 = I2r22   →   I1I2 = r22r12

The power of the source is fixed, so it is the same P on both sides and it cancels — along with the 4π. What is left relates two intensities to two distances with nothing else in it, which is why you can answer “how much quieter is it twice as far away?” without knowing anything about the source. Note the flip: the ratio of intensities is the inverse ratio of the squared distances.

Why the square — bread and butter

  • The number of slices covered increases — and it increases as the square of the distance, because the patch grows in both directions at once.
  • The amount of butter remains the same. Nothing is lost.
  • The amount of butter per slice is reduced — by 1/4 at twice the distance, 1/9 at three times, 1/16 at four times.

That third line is the inverse square law, and the second line is why it is not a law about energy disappearing. The sound is just as loud in total; it is spread thinner.

See it happen Falstad’s ripple tank shows the same thing in two dimensions, where the circumference grows as r rather than the area as r2, so the falling-off is gentler: ripple tank simulation. Comparing the two is a good way to see that the exponent comes from the geometry and nothing else.

Worked example — intensity from power

A source radiates 3.25 × 10−7 W. What is the intensity 1.5 m away?
given:  P = 3.25 × 10−7 W,  r = 1.5 m
I = P4πr2 = 3.25 × 10−7 W4π(1.5 m)2 = 3.25 × 10−7 W28.274 m2
I = 1.149 × 10−8 W/m2 = 1.15 × 10−8 W/m2

Square the radius before multiplying by 4π — the commonest slip here is (4πr)2. Keep the unrounded 1.149 × 10−8: Ch17c takes this exact number and turns it into decibels.

Check the scale

10−8 W/m2 sounds like nothing at all. Is this a sound anyone could hear?

Easily. The threshold of human hearing is 10−12 W/m2, so this is ten thousand times above it — a quiet but perfectly audible sound, around 40 dB. The ear works over an extraordinary range of intensities, which is the problem Ch17c exists to solve.

In short

  • Waves move energy from one place to another; power is energy per unit time and intensity is power per unit area.
  • The intensity depends on how much area the wave has spread out into.
  • For a point source, I = P/4πr2 — the inverse square law.
  • I1r12 = I2r22 lets you compare two places without knowing the source at all.
