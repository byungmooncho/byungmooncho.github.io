Finding Information in a Graph

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea A graph of measured data will not usually hand you the quantity you want. What it hands you is a slope. The method is always the same three steps: write the equation the physics predicts, rearrange it until it has the shape of a straight line, and then match it term by term against the trendline the spreadsheet fitted. The quantity you are after is hiding in the slope — but it is almost never equal to the slope.
You already know this y = mx + b, and from the earlier pages T = 2π√(m/k).

The experiment

Trevor demonstrates this with the PhET Masses and Springs simulation: hang a mass from a spring, set it oscillating, and time the period. Change the mass and do it again. Eleven different masses give eleven periods, and the question is how to get the spring constant out of them.

Run it yourself The simulation is free and runs in a browser: PhET — Masses and Springs. Trevor also keeps a link beside this deck to a second mass-on-a-spring demonstration at oPhysics, with the warning that it shows more than this course goes into. Both are worth ten minutes before the graph below makes sense.

Building a model equation

From the period equation to the equation of a line
T = 2π√ ((m + m′) / k)
T2 = 4π2km + 4π2km′
y = (slope)x + b

Squaring is what does the work. T against m is a curve and a curve is hard to read; T2 against m is a straight line, and a straight line has exactly two numbers in it. This is linearising the data, and it is the standard move whenever a model has a square root in it.

The m′ is the spring’s own effective mass. The spring is not weightless, and part of it moves with the block, so it contributes to the inertia of the system. It is a constant for a given spring, which is why it lands in the intercept rather than the slope.

Reading the graph

Matching the trendline to the model, term by term
model:   slope = 4π2/k   →   k = 4π2slope
fit:   slope = 1.182 s2/kg
k = 4π21.182 s2/kg = 33.4 kg/s2 = 33.4 N/m

Check the unit conversion on the last line, because it is the step students skip. A newton is kg·m/s2, so N/m is kg/s2 — the same unit written two ways, not a conversion factor. If the algebra gives you kg/s2 you already have a spring constant.

The point of the whole lesson

The slope is 1.182. Is the spring constant 1.182 N/m?

No. The answer is not the slope — but it is found using the slope. The slope is 4π2/k, so k is 4π2 divided by the slope: 33.4 N/m, nearly thirty times larger. Reaching for the slope itself is the single most common way to get this kind of problem wrong.

What the intercept is telling us

The fitted intercept is −0.0112 s2. The model says the intercept should be 4π2m′/k, which is positive — a mass cannot be negative. So either the model is wrong or the intercept is not really different from zero.

It is the second. With R2 = 0.997 there is still scatter in the points, and −0.0112 is about the size of that scatter: the line is being fitted over masses from 0.100 to 0.600 kg and then extrapolated back to zero, where there are no measurements at all. The honest reading is that the spring’s effective mass is too small to measure in this data, not that it is negative. A sign that comes out impossible is worth stopping on rather than reporting.

The method, in general

StepWhat you do
1Write the equation the physics predicts, with the unknown in it.
2Rearrange it into the shape y = (slope)x + b, choosing what to plot so that the relationship is straight.
3Plot it, fit a trendline, and read off the slope and intercept.
4Match the two equations term by term, and solve for the unknown.
5Check the units of the answer, and check the intercept means something.

In short

  • Information is often hidden in the trendline of a graph rather than printed on it.
  • Compare the model equation to the parameters of the trendline equation, one term at a time.
  • The answer is not the slope, but it is found using the slope.
  • Squaring the period is what makes this data linear — linearise first, fit second.
