Sound Intensity Level: the Decibel

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea The quietest sound a person can hear and the loudest one they can survive differ by a factor of about ten thousand billion. No ordinary scale copes with that, so sound is measured on a logarithmic one, in decibels. The price is that decibels do not add the way intensities do — doubling the intensity adds three to the level, not a hundred per cent — and getting comfortable with that is most of the work on this page.
You already know this Intensity in W/m2 from Ch17b, and logarithms from algebra: log(AB) = log A + log B, and 10log x = x.

The log function compresses powers of ten
y = log x

What the log does is turn multiplying into adding. Every time x is multiplied by ten, log x goes up by exactly one. Sixteen orders of magnitude become sixteen equal steps, and a range nothing could display becomes a range that fits on a ruler.

Two values in that row are worth memorising, because they turn up constantly in this topic: log 2 = 0.301 and log 10 = 1.

The decibel

Sound intensity level
β = 10 dB · log ( IIo )   with Io = 1 × 10−12 W/m2

β is a ratio of two intensities, so it is dimensionless — the W/m2 cancel inside the log. It is nonetheless labelled in decibels, to say which reference and which factor of ten were used. Io is the threshold of hearing, which is why 0 dB means “only just audible” rather than “no sound”.

What the numbers sound like

Ten decibels: ten times the intensity, twice the loudness These are two different statements and both are true. Intensity is physics: +10 dB is ten times the watts per square metre, every time. Loudness is perception: the same +10 dB sounds about twice as loud to a person. The human ear is not a linear device, and the gap between those two sentences is exactly why the decibel exists.

Working in both directions

Intensity → level: the source from Ch17b, at 1.5 m
given:  I = 1.149 × 10−8 W/m2,  Io = 1.0 × 10−12 W/m2
β = 10 dB · log ( 1.149 × 10−8 W/m21.0 × 10−12 W/m2 ) = 10 dB · log(1.149 × 104)
β = 10 dB · 4.06 = 40.6 dB

The units cancel inside the log, which is the check that you divided by Io and not by something else. A ratio of about 104 gives a log of about 4, so the answer had to be near 40 dB before any button was pressed — a quiet room.

Level → intensity: a siren at 130 dB
130 dB = 10 dB · log(I/Io)  →  130 dB10 dB = log(I/Io)  →  13 = log(I/Io)
1013 = 10log(I/Io) = I/Io  →  I = 1013Io
I = 1013 · 10−12 W/m2 = 101 W/m2 = 10 W/m2

Undo the log by raising ten to both sides — that is the only move in the whole calculation, and it is what 10log x = x is for. Divide by the 10 dB first; raising ten to the power of 130 is the classic error and gives an answer with no physical meaning at all.

A conversation at 60 dB
6 = log(I/Io)  →  106 = I/Io  →  I = 106 · 10−12 W/m2 = 10−6 W/m2

A millionth of a watt per square metre, for ordinary speech. The ear is an astonishing instrument.

Two trumpets

Two instruments play together, nearly in phase. How much louder?
one instrument:  β1 = 10 dB · log(I1/Io)   two instruments:  I2 = 2I1
β2 = 10 dB · log (2I1/Io) = 10 dB · { log 2 + log(I1/Io) }
β2 = 10 dB · log 2 + β1 = 10 dB(0.301) + β1 = 3.01 dB + β1

log(AB) = log A + log B splits the 2 off from the ratio, and what is left is the original level untouched. Doubling the intensity adds about three decibels — which is barely noticeable. Two trumpets are not twice as loud as one; ten of them are.

Frequency, and why that axis is logarithmic too

Sensitivity curves are drawn on semi-log axes — logarithmic in frequency, linear in level — for the same reason the level itself is logarithmic: the ear responds to ratios. A step from 100 Hz to 200 Hz is the same musical interval, an octave, as a step from 1000 Hz to 2000 Hz, and a log axis gives them the same width.

Hearing damage is a dose, not a level Trevor cites research on personal music players finding doses as high as 120 dB — comparable to a loud rock concert or to sandblasting, and enough to risk damage after about 7.5 minutes of exposure. The number that matters is level multiplied by time: 85 dB for a working day does the same harm as something much louder for a few minutes. A phone with a sound-level app will tell you which side of that line you are on. (Gregory Mott, “The iPod and the Fury”, Washington Post, 17 January 2006.)

In short

  • β = 10 dB · log(I/Io), with Io = 10−12 W/m2.
  • β is dimensionless but labelled in decibels; 0 dB is the threshold of hearing, not silence.
  • +10 dB is ten times the intensity and about twice the loudness. They are not the same claim.
  • Doubling the intensity adds only 3 dB.
  • To go from dB to intensity: divide by 10 dB first, then raise ten to that power.
