Harmonic Oscillators and Hooke’s Law

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea A spring pulls back harder the further you stretch it, and that single fact — a restoring force proportional to displacement — is enough to force sinusoidal motion. Put Hooke’s law into Newton’s second law and out comes an angular frequency that depends on the spring and the mass and on nothing else. Most of what makes oscillator problems messy is not the physics but the bookkeeping, and nearly all of that bookkeeping is a choice of where to put the origin.
You already know this Newton’s second law and free-body diagrams, and position–velocity–acceleration from kinematics. Nothing here needs energy or calculus — though the last section shows what the calculus adds if you want it.

Reading an oscillation off a graph

Before any of the algebra, a sanity check Trevor opens with. A mass on a spring oscillates back and forth, and its position is plotted against time:

At t = 3.0 s the mass is moving downward and still speeding up for a moment longer, but it has already passed equilibrium — so the force, and with it the acceleration, has turned around and now points back up. Negative velocity, positive acceleration. The general rule is worth carrying: the acceleration always points toward equilibrium, whatever the velocity is doing.

What “simple harmonic” actually means

The phrase is three separate conditions stacked up, and Trevor separates them deliberately:

Oscillationrepeated motion around a fixed point.
Harmonicthe oscillations occur with a fixed pattern in time.
Simplethe restoring force is linearly proportional to the displacement of the object.
Why “simple” is the load-bearing word Drop the word simple and the restoring force can be any function of displacement at all, and the motion is no longer a sine. Nearly everything on this page follows from the linear in that third line, and nothing follows from the first two.

What the motion looks like

Plot the position of an oscillator against time and you get a sinusoid — a sine or cosine curve. Trevor draws it the useful way round, by hanging the actual spring above each point of the graph, so the picture of the system and the picture of the motion are the same picture:

Where to put the origin

This is the part the second deck is entirely about, and it is worth the attention: almost every avoidable difficulty in an oscillator problem is a bad choice here. There are six plausible places to put the zero of the coordinate system for a mass hanging on a spring.

Any of them works. The physics does not care. But the algebra cares a great deal, and the equilibrium position is the one that makes it come out short.

Why that choice makes gravity disappear

Here is the argument in full, and it is the best thing on either deck. Trevor measures y downward from the free end of the unstretched spring, and writes the forces as magnitudes with their signs put in by hand.

General — Newton’s second law for the block
ΣF = ma  →  Fs + Fg = ma  →  −Fs + Fg = ma

The minus appears because the spring pulls up while +y points down.

At equilibrium — net force is zero
−Fs(eq) + Fg = m·0  →  −ky1 + Fg = 0  →  −ky1 = −Fg

So the equilibrium stretch is exactly the one that makes the spring carry the weight: ky1 = Fg.

At any other spot — a distance Δy lower
Fs2 = ky2 = k(y1 + Δy) = ky1 + kΔy = Fg + kΔy
−Fs2 + Fg = ma  →  −(Fg + kΔy) + Fg = ma
−kΔy = ma

The two Fg terms cancel. Gravity is gone from the equation of motion entirely — not neglected, not approximated, cancelled. In Trevor’s words: it is the change in position that matters, not the position.

What this buys you A hanging spring and a horizontal spring now obey the same equation. Every result on the rest of this page was derived for one and applies to the other, and that is only true because the origin was put at equilibrium.

And it lines the force up with the acceleration

The horizontal spring, and why it is the awkward one

Textbooks usually draw a block on a horizontal spring, because it looks like the simplest case — one unbalanced force and no gravity to worry about. Trevor flags the problem with it:

  • Real springs are either compression springs or extension springs, not both.
  • A compressed spring eventually runs into itself, and the linear law stops holding well before that.

The hanging spring avoids both: it stays in extension through the whole oscillation. The reason the horizontal picture survives in textbooks is that it looks like there is only one force acting — and the section above is exactly what buys you that same simplicity in the vertical case honestly.

Hooke’s law

Hooke’s law
F = −kx

k is the spring constant. The minus sign is there because the force is always in the direction opposite to the displacement — which is why this kind of force is called a restoring force.

Measure the force needed for a series of stretches and plot one against the other, and the points fall on a line through the origin. Trevor’s own plot:

The units of k
[k] = [N][m] = [kg·m/s2][m] = [kg][s2]

A spring constant is a mass per time squared. That looks odd until the next section, where k/m turns out to be one over a time squared — which is exactly what a frequency squared is.

Putting the two laws together

This is the step the whole page has been building to, and it is three lines long.

Newton’s second law meets Hooke’s law
F = ma  and  F = −kx  →  ma = −kx
a = − km x
km ≡ ω2   →   ω = √(k/m)   angular frequency

The definition k/m ≡ ω2 is not pulled out of the air: the units force it. [k/m] = [kg/s2] / [kg] = [1/s2], so ω is in [1/s], which is what an angular frequency is.

From ω to the period

Period of a mass–spring system
√(k/m) = ω = 2πT   →   T = 2π√(m/k)

Check the units: [√(m/k)] = [√(s2)] = s. A period, as it should be.

Trevor’s warning, kept verbatim Be careful — the m and k have switched places. ω has k on top; T has m on top. It is the single most common slip in this chapter, and the physical check is quick: a heavier mass should oscillate more slowly, so m belongs on top of the period.

The same result with calculus

Nothing above needed calculus. If you want the equation of motion in its standard form, though, it is two substitutions away — and this is the form that shows up again for pendulums, for waves on a string, and for every other oscillator in the course.

The equation of motion
may = ΣFy = Fs − Fg  →  may = −kΔy
m d2ydt2 = −ky  →  d2ydt2 = −kmy  →  d2ydt2 = −ω2y

Read it in words: the acceleration is proportional to the displacement and points the other way. Any function whose second derivative is a negative multiple of itself is a sine or a cosine — which is why the motion had to be sinusoidal, and why the graph at the top of this page looks the way it does.

In short

  • The motion of a harmonic oscillator looks like a sine or cosine function.
  • The angular frequency depends on the mass and the spring constant, and on nothing else — not the amplitude, not how it was started.
  • That equation rearranges to give the period of a spring–mass system.
  • Put the origin at the equilibrium position and gravity cancels out of the equation of motion. The change in position matters; the position does not.
