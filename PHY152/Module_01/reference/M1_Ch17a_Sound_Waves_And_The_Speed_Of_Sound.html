Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea Sound is a longitudinal wave in a material. The air does not travel from the speaker to your ear; it squeezes and spreads in place, and the squeeze travels. How fast it travels is a property of the material — of how stiff it is and how heavy — which is why sound runs through steel sixteen times faster than through air, and why a wave on a string and a wave in a room are governed by the same kind of formula.
You already know this v = fλ and the difference between transverse and longitudinal waves from Ch16a, and v = √(FT/μ) from Ch16b — a stiffness over an inertia, under a square root.

Compressions and rarefactions

Compressiona high pressure region, where the particles are close together.
Rarefactiona low pressure region, where the particles are far apart.

Longitudinal waves make the medium vibrate parallel to the direction the wave moves — each particle slides a little way forward and back along the line of travel, not across it. One wavelength is the distance from one compression to the next.

Two ways to draw the same wave The particle picture and the pressure graph are the same wave described twice, and the second one is the useful one. A microphone cannot see particles; it measures pressure, and the graph it produces is an ordinary sinusoid oscillating about atmospheric pressure. That is why a longitudinal wave can be handled with exactly the same mathematics as a transverse one.

Trevor points to Dan Russell’s animations, which show a longitudinal and a transverse wave side by side with one particle highlighted in each: wave motion animations.

How fast, and why it depends on the material

Three formulas, one shape
in a gas:   v = √(γRTK / M)   γ adiabatic index, R gas constant, TK in kelvin, M molecular mass
in a liquid:   v = √(B / ρ)   B bulk modulus, ρ density
in a solid:   v = √(Y / ρ)   Y Young’s modulus, ρ density

All three are stiffness over inertia, under a square root — the same shape as √(FT/μ) for a string. Only the names change: the bulk modulus and Young’s modulus measure how hard the material resists being squeezed, and ρ measures how much of it there is to move. Stiff and light is fast; floppy and heavy is slow.

Helium breaks the pattern — or does it?

Gases are supposed to be slowest, yet sound travels through helium at 965 m/s, three times faster than through air. Why?

Look at the formula: v ∝ 1/√M. Helium’s molecular mass is about seven times smaller than air’s, and √7 ≈ 2.6 — which is most of the factor of three. The rule “gases are slowest” is really “gases are the least stiff”; helium wins back on the inertia side. It is also why a lungful of helium raises your voice: the resonant frequencies of your vocal tract scale with the speed of sound in it.

Finding the speed, and finding the wavelength

An A3 note, 220 Hz, in air
v = fλ   speed = frequency × wavelength
λ = vf = 340 m/s220 Hz = 1.55 m

A hertz is a per-second, so m/s divided by 1/s leaves metres. A metre and a half is about the length of a cello — which is roughly the right size for an instrument that plays that note, and a useful sanity check.

Echoes

A yell returns after 1.50 s. How far away is the wall? (vs = 340 m/s)
x = xo + voΔt + ½a(Δt)2  →  the speed of sound is constant, so a = 0 and xo = 0
x = voΔt = (340 m/s)(1.50 s) = 510 m
d = x2 = 510 m2 = 255 m

Be careful with echoes: the sound must travel out and back. The 510 m is the whole journey; the wall is half that away. Forgetting the factor of two here is the single commonest error in this topic, and it doubles your answer rather than nudging it.

In short

  • Sound is longitudinal: compressions are high pressure, rarefactions are low.
  • The speed of sound depends on the medium — slowest in gases, faster in liquids, fastest in rigid solids — and on its temperature.
  • All three speed formulas are stiffness over inertia under a square root; only the names differ.
  • Several methods give the speed; use the one that matches what you are given: v = d/t, v = fλ, or a materials formula.
  • Echoes travel out and back.
