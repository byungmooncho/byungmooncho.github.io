Standing Waves and Harmonics

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea Fix a string at both ends and it will not vibrate at just any frequency. Only the shapes that fit — a whole number of loops between the two ends — survive, and every one of them has a frequency that is a whole-number multiple of the lowest. That restriction is what turns a stretched string into a musical instrument instead of a noise.
You already know this v = fλ and v = √(FT/μ) from the earlier pages, and from Ch16d that a wave reflecting from a fixed end comes back inverted — which is what makes a standing wave in the first place.

Nodes and antinodes

Nodea point that never moves. The two travelling waves always cancel there.
Antinodea point that moves the most. The two waves always reinforce there.
Loopthe bulge between two neighbouring nodes — half a wavelength.

The number of antinodes is the harmonic number. The figure above has four, so it is the fourth harmonic. Count the loops, and you have the harmonic; there is always exactly one more node than antinode on a string fixed at both ends, because the two fixed ends are nodes themselves.

Why it stands still A standing wave is not a new kind of wave. It is the wave you sent down the string interfering with its own reflection coming back — two identical waves travelling in opposite directions. The fixed end forces the displacement there to be zero at all times, and that one condition is what picks out which wavelengths are allowed.

Naming them: harmonics and overtones

Two naming systems for the same ladder, offset by one. Harmonic counts from the fundamental inclusive; overtone counts how far above the fundamental you have gone. The fundamental is the first harmonic and is not an overtone at all. The offset is a permanent nuisance and the only cure is to say which word you mean.

Working out the frequencies

From the picture to fn
first harmonic:  L = ½λ1  →  λ1 = 2L  →  f1 = vλ1 = v2L
second harmonic:  L = λ2  →  f2 = vλ2 = vL = 2v2L = 2f1
in general:  L = n(½λ)  →  λ = 2L/n  →  fn = n v2L = nf1

The wavelength comes off the picture — count loops, multiply by two, divide into the length — and the frequency then comes off v = fλ, which you already have. The speed v is the same for every mode, because it belongs to the string, not to the note; that is why the frequencies come out as exact whole-number multiples rather than as something messier.

Where this joins up with Ch16b Put v = √(FT/μ) into f1 = v/2L and you get f1 = (1/2L)√(FT/μ) — which is Mersenne’s formula, arrived at from the opposite direction. Shorter string, higher note; tighter string, higher note; heavier string, lower note; and all three read straight off the one expression.

More than one at a time

A real string does not pick one mode. Pluck it and several harmonics start at once, in proportions that depend on where and how you plucked, and the string’s shape is their sum. Which harmonics are present, and how strongly, is what makes a guitar sound different from a piano playing the same note — the fundamental is the same, the mix above it is not.

Trevor demonstrates this with two simulations worth a few minutes each: Falstad’s loaded string, where you can excite modes separately and together, and a waveform synthesiser that builds a shape from its harmonics.

In short

  • A standing wave is a wave interfering with its own reflection; the fixed ends are forced to be nodes.
  • Each loop is half a wavelength, and the number of loops (antinodes) is the harmonic number.
  • The nth harmonic is the (n−1)th overtone. The fundamental is the first harmonic.
  • λn = 2L/n and fn = nv/2L = nf1 — every harmonic is a whole multiple of the fundamental.
  • Several harmonics can be present at once, and the mix is what gives an instrument its character.
