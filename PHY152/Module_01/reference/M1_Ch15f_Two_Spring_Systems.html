Two-Spring Systems: Parallel and Series

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea Put two springs on one mass and you do not have to start over: the pair behaves as a single spring with one effective stiffness. Which stiffness depends entirely on how they are connected. Side by side, both pull at once and the system is stiffer; end to end, each takes half the stretch and the system is floppier. For two identical springs the two answers are 2k and ½k.
You already know this Hooke’s law F = −kx, Newton’s second law, and free-body diagrams. Nothing new is needed — this page is those three applied twice.

First, a question

Think first

A block sits between two springs anchored to opposite walls, both already stretched. Do the spring forces on the block point (1) in the same direction, (2) in opposite directions, or (3) it depends on where the block is?

(2) — opposite directions, always. Each spring is stretched and each wants to return to its own natural length, so each pulls the block towards its own wall. That is true wherever the block sits, which rules out (3). The tempting wrong answer is (1), from picturing the net effect rather than the two individual forces.

Why opposite forces still add up

This is the part worth slowing down for. The two forces genuinely point in opposite directions at every instant — but what matters for the oscillation is how they change as the block moves. Move the block one way and one spring’s pull weakens while the other’s strengthens, and both of those changes act in the same direction. That is why two springs in this arrangement are stiffer than one, even though they spend the whole time fighting each other.

Doing the algebra properly

Two springs, one block
ΣF = ma  →  −Fℓ + Fr = ma
−kdℓ + kdr = ma
Y = dℓ + dr  →  dr = Y − dℓ
−kdℓ + k(Y − dℓ) = ma  →  −2kdℓ + kY = ma
Y = dℓ + x + ½Y  →  dℓ = ½Y − x
−2k(½Y − x) + kY = ma
−kY + 2kx + kY = ma  →  2kx = ma

Watch Y: it enters in the third line and leaves in the last, which is the whole trick. The unstretched gap is a property of the apparatus, not of the motion, so it has to cancel — in the same way gravity cancelled on Ch15a once the origin was put at equilibrium. What survives is 2kx, with no Y and no pre-stretch in it at all.

Where the missing minus sign went The last line reads 2kx = ma, not −2kx = ma, and that is not an error. In his figure the forces are positive to the right while x is measured to the left. A positive x therefore means the block has moved away from the direction the net force points, which is what a restoring force is. Put both on one axis and the familiar minus sign comes back.

The result: two springs in parallel

One spring with twice the stiffness
2kx = ma   compare   keffx = ma
k∥ = k1 + k2 = 2k   (identical springs)
ω = √(2k/m),   T = 2π√(m/2k)

The system is indistinguishable from a single spring of stiffness 2k. The period is shorter than one spring alone would give, by a factor of √2 — stiffer means faster.

“Parallel” does not mean the springs look parallel on the page. It means both springs act on the same mass and both feel the same displacement. A block between two walls, a mass sitting on two springs side by side, a mass hanging from two springs from the same ceiling — all parallel, all 2k.

Two springs in series

Each spring carries the whole force
ΣF2 = ΣF1  →  Fb + Fg = F1 + Fg  →  Fb = F1
k(½Δy) = keffΔy
½kΔy = keffΔy  →  kseries = ½k

The comparison is between two systems carrying the same block: a pair in series, and a single spring. The same weight hangs below both, so each spring in the pair carries the same force as the single one and stretches by the same ½Δy — but there are two of them in the chain, so the block drops twice as far. Twice the stretch for the same force is half the stiffness.

It is the other way round from resistors If you have met resistors in a circuit, the rule here is reversed: springs add in parallel and combine reciprocally in series, where resistors do the opposite. Springs behave like capacitors, not like resistors. Worth noticing once now rather than being caught by it in the second half of the course.

In short

  • Two stretched springs on one block always pull in opposite directions — but their changes reinforce, which is what makes the pair stiffer.
  • Two identical springs in parallel have an effective spring constant that is the sum of the two: 2k.
  • Two identical springs in series have an effective spring constant that is half of one of them: ½k.
  • In both cases the pair is then treated as a single spring, and everything from the earlier pages applies unchanged.
