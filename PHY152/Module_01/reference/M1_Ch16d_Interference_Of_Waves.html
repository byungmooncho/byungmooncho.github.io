Interference of Waves

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea Two waves can occupy the same place at the same time, and when they do you simply add their displacements. That one rule — superposition — is the whole of this page. Where the two add up you get a bigger displacement; where they oppose you get a smaller one; and since a wave's displacement changes from point to point, both happen at once in different places. That pattern is what interference is.
You already know this y = A sin(kx − ωt + φ) from the earlier pages, including what the phase constant φ does.

The rule

Superposition
y(x,t) = y1(x,t) + y2(x,t)

More than one wave or pulse can move through a medium at a time, and they do not collide or scatter off one another. At every point, the medium’s displacement is the sum of what each wave would have given on its own. Nothing is averaged and nothing is lost.

Two pulses on the same side

Constructive interference means the net displacement is further from equilibrium than either wave alone. Three things are worth noticing, and Trevor flags all three:

  • Both displacements could be negative. Two downward pulses add to a deeper downward pulse, and that is just as constructive.
  • The direction of displacement matters less than the amount. Constructive is about the size of the result, not its sign.
  • The pulses survive. They add while they overlap and then carry on unchanged, which is not obvious and is worth watching for on a real rope.

Two pulses on opposite sides

It only looks like subtraction
A + (−B) = A − B

The rule is still addition. One of the two displacements simply happens to be negative, and adding a negative number is subtracting. Destructive interference means the net displacement is closer to equilibrium.

Where does the energy go? At the instant the string looks flat, every piece of it is moving — fast. The energy is all kinetic at that moment, exactly as it is for an oscillator passing through equilibrium. A moment later both pulses re-emerge, unchanged, which could not happen if anything had actually been cancelled.

Two wave trains

With continuous waves rather than single pulses, the result is permanent rather than momentary. Two identical waves in phase give a wave of twice the amplitude everywhere; two half a cycle apart give nothing, anywhere, ever. Those are the two extremes, and real situations sit between them — in practice both things happen at once at different parts of the wave, which is precisely what makes a pattern.

Counting it: the phase difference

Two identical waves, differing only in phase
yn = An sin(knxn − ωnt + φn)
same source, same medium → same A, k and ω:
y1 = A sin(kx1 − ωt + φ1o) = A sin(φ1)   with φ1 = kx1 − ωt + φ1o
y2 = A sin(kx2 − ωt + φ2o) = A sin(φ2)

Folding everything inside the sine into a single symbol φ is the move that makes the rest easy. Two waves of the same amplitude, wavelength and frequency can then differ in exactly one way, and the whole question becomes: by how much?

Subtracting the two phases
Δφ = φ2 − φ1 = (kx2 − ωt + φ2o) − (kx1 − ωt + φ1o)
= (kx2 − kx1) + ωt − ωt + (φ2o − φ1o)
= 2πλ (x2 − x1) + (φ2o − φ1o)
Δφ = 2πλ Δx + Δφo

The time drops out. ωt is the same in both waves, so it cancels in the subtraction — which is why an interference pattern sits still instead of running away. What is left has two parts: a piece from the path difference Δx, and a piece from however the two sources were started, Δφo. The 2π/λ is the wave number doing its usual job of turning metres into radians.

The two extremes

Maximum constructive and maximum destructive
Δφmax = 2πλ Δx + Δφo = m · 2π rad   constructive
Δφmin = 2πλ Δx + Δφo = (m + ½) · 2π rad   destructive
m = 0, 1, 2, …

A whole number of full cycles puts the two waves back in step — it does not matter whether it is one cycle or a hundred, which is why m runs over all the integers. Half a cycle more than that puts them exactly opposed. Constructive adds; destructive cancels. For two sources started together, Δφo = 0 and the condition reduces to the familiar one: a path difference of a whole number of wavelengths builds, half a wavelength extra cancels.

Reflection, and the phase change at a boundary

BoundaryWhat happensPhase change
“Fixed” end or interfacethe pulse flips over180° (π rad)
“Free” end or interfacethe pulse returns unchangednone

This matters because the reflected wave then interferes with the wave still arriving — the wave interferes with itself. At a fixed end the inversion forces the displacement at the wall to be zero at all times, which is a node; that is the seed of the standing waves on Ch16e, and of the thin-film and anti-reflection coating work in Module 2. Trevor demonstrates both cases with the oPhysics simulation, which is worth running: wave reflection at a boundary.

Interference is visible, not just theoretical Trevor shows a photograph of two thin microscope slides pressed together. The light reflecting off the two surfaces travels slightly different distances and arrives out of step, so the slides are crossed by bright and dark bands — constructive interference in the bright areas, destructive in the dim ones. The gap between the slides varies by well under the thickness of a hair, and the pattern makes that variation visible. Module 2 does the arithmetic.

In short

  • Interference happens when two pulses or two waves are at the same place at the same time; their displacements add.
  • Constructive moves the medium further from equilibrium; destructive moves it closer.
  • The phase difference is Δφ = (2π/λ)Δx + Δφo — the time cancels, which is why the pattern stands still.
  • Whole multiples of 2π build; half-integer multiples cancel.
  • A wave reflecting from a fixed boundary flips; from a free one it does not.
