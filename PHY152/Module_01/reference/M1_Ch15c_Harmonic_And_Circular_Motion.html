Harmonic Motion and Uniform Circular Motion

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea Simple harmonic motion is not merely like circular motion — it is circular motion, viewed from the side. Take an object going round a circle at a steady rate and look only at its shadow on a wall: that shadow performs exactly the oscillation a spring does. Every formula on the previous two pages can be read straight off the circle, which is where the ω in all of them comes from.
You already know this Uniform circular motion from the first semester — the speed round the circle, the centripetal acceleration pointing inward, and the relationship between period and frequency.

What the circle already gives you

Circular motion, written with ω
2πA = vpT  →  vp = 2πA/T = 2πfA = Aω
ac = v2/r = (2πfA)2/A = Aω2

θ = ωt, and one full turn is 2π = ωT. Note what has happened: the circle’s speed is Aω and its acceleration is Aω2 — the same two maxima the oscillator had.

The shadow

Trevor shows a video of an orange ball going round a circle with a light behind it. The ball moves in a circle; its shadow moves in simple harmonic motion. Not approximately — exactly.

Why this is more than a coincidence The shadow’s position is the projection of the circular position onto one axis — and the projection of a vector rotating at a steady rate is, by definition, a cosine of the angle. Since the angle grows as ωt, the projection is A cos(ωt). The spring was never doing anything else.

Taking the components

QuantityProjection onto xMaximum
Positionx = A cos θ = A cos(ωt)A
Velocityvx = −vp sin θ = −Aω sin(ωt)Aω
Accelerationax = −ac cos θ = −Aω2 cos(ωt)Aω2

Those are the three equations from the previous page, arrived at without writing down a single force. The minus signs are not extra physics — they are what projection does when the vector has swung past the axis.

The same thing with calculus

Differentiating the projection
Position:   x = A cos(ωt)
Velocity:   dxdt = −Aω sin(ωt)
Acceleration:   dvdt = −Aω2 cos(ωt) = −ω2x

The last line is the equation of motion again. Whichever way you come at it — forces, projection, or differentiation — you land on a = −ω2x.

In short

  • Simple harmonic motion is the projection of uniform circular motion onto a line.
  • The circle’s radius is the amplitude; its angular speed is the oscillator’s ω.
  • Once you have the position equation, the velocity and acceleration equations follow — by projection or by differentiation, and they agree.
