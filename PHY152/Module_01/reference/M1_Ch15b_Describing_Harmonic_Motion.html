Describing Harmonic Motion

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea Once you know an oscillation is sinusoidal, three numbers pin it down completely: how far it swings, how long one swing takes, and where in the cycle it happened to be when you started the clock. Those are amplitude, period and phase — and the third one is the only one that gives anybody trouble, because it is the only one that depends on you rather than on the system.
You already know this That a spring and a mass oscillate, and that ω = √(k/m) comes out of Hooke’s law and Newton’s second law. This page is about writing the motion down rather than deriving it.

Three words for three numbers

Amplitude, A maximum displacement from equilibrium — measured up or down.
Period, T the time for a single oscillation.
Frequency, f the number of oscillations per second; f = 1/T.
Measured up or down The amplitude is the distance from equilibrium to one extreme, not from the top of the motion to the bottom. The full swing is 2A. It is an easy factor of two to lose, and it propagates into every energy calculation afterwards.

Writing the motion as a function

The curve looks like a cosine of time whose maximum is the amplitude. Two things have to be right before it is usable: the argument of a cosine must be in radians, which are unitless, and the cosine repeats every 2π radians — so the argument has to run through 2π in exactly one period.

The same equation, four ways of writing it
y(t) = A cos( ? t)  →  y(t) = A cos ( 2πT t)
y(t) = A cos(2πf t)  →  y(t) = A cos(ωt)

All four are the same statement. The last is the one worth keeping, because ω is what the physics hands you — √(k/m) for a spring — while T and f are what a stopwatch hands you.

Phase — where the clock was started

Everything above assumed the oscillator was at its maximum when the stopwatch started. It usually is not. Start the clock at a different moment and the same motion is described by a different-looking function:

One form covers all of them
y(t) = A cos(ωt + φ)

φ is the phase angle. It carries no physics about the spring or the mass — it records only when you chose to press start.

Written asIs the same asPhase angle
+A cos(ωt) A cos(ωt + 0)φ = 0
−A sin(ωt) A cos(ωt + π/2)φ = +π/2
−A cos(ωt) A cos(ωt + π)φ = π
+A sin(ωt) A cos(ωt − π/2)φ = −π/2
Reading φ off the initial conditions You almost never choose φ; you read it off what the oscillator was doing at t = 0. Released from rest at maximum displacement → φ = 0. Passing through equilibrium moving in the +y direction → φ = −π/2. Those two cover most problems you will meet.

Position, speed and acceleration together

Trevor builds this up one curve at a time — position, then position and speed, then all three. They are shown together here because the relationship between them is the point:

The three equations
y(t) = +A cos(ωt)
v(t) = −Aω sin(ωt)
a(t) = −Aω2 cos(ωt)

Each is the derivative of the one above it, and each differentiation brings down another factor of ω. So the maximum speed is Aω and the maximum acceleration is Aω2.

Read off the picture rather than the algebra and three facts fall out at once. Where the position is at an extreme the speed is zero. Where the position crosses zero the speed is greatest. And the acceleration is always the opposite sign to the position — which is just −ω2y from the previous page, seen on a graph.

In short

  • Amplitude is the maximum displacement from equilibrium.
  • Period is the time for one oscillation; frequency is the number of oscillations per second.
  • The phase angle records when the clock was started, nothing more.
  • Position, velocity and acceleration are all the same curve, shifted and scaled by ω.
