The Doppler Effect, with worked examples

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea A siren sounds higher as it comes towards you and lower as it goes away, and the horn itself never changes. What changes is the spacing of the sound that has already left it: the source catches up a little with each front it emits, so the fronts ahead are crowded and the ones behind are stretched. Everything else on this page is bookkeeping for that one sentence.
You already know this v = fλ and T = 1/f from Ch16a, and that the speed of sound belongs to the air and not to whatever made the sound.

Approaching: the fronts crowd together

What an observer in front measures
Δx = vsndΔt − vsrcΔt  →  over one period,  λobs = (vsnd − vsrc)T
λobs = (340 − 40.0 m/s)(0.001 s) = 0.300 m  against 0.340 m at rest
fobs = vsndλobs = vsnd(vsnd − vsrc)T = vsnd(vsnd − vsrc)fsrc
fobs = 340 m/s(340 − 40.0) m/s(1000 Hz) = 340300(1000 Hz) = 1133.33 Hz = 1130 Hz

Nothing has happened to the horn. The sound still leaves it a thousand times a second and still travels at 340 m/s — the speed of sound is a property of the air and never changes. What changed is λ, and f = v/λ then does the rest. Approaching, the frequency goes up.

Receding: the fronts stretch out

What an observer behind measures
λobs = (vsnd − (−vsrc))T = (vsnd + vsrc)T = 0.380 m
fobs = 340 m/s(340 + 40.0) m/s(1000 Hz) = 894.736 Hz = 895 Hz

The same expression, with the source’s velocity carrying the other sign because it now moves away from this observer rather than towards them. Moving away, the frequency goes down. Note the asymmetry: approaching raised the pitch by 133 Hz, receding lowers it by only 105 Hz. The shift is not symmetric, because the source speed sits in the denominator.

The general equation, and how to get the signs right

Observers can move too, and both can move at once. Written with a ± over a ∓ the result is genuinely confusing, and Trevor says so on his own slide. There is a better way: put each velocity on the side it belongs to, and decide its sign by asking one question about direction.

One equation for every case
fobs(vsnd − vobs) = fsrc(vsnd − vsrc)
sign rule:  − ≡ moving with the sound,  + ≡ moving against the sound
vsnd is always positive

Both sides have the same shape, which is the point — you never have to remember which of the four combinations you are in. Write the equation down, put a sign on each velocity by asking “is this thing moving with the sound or against it?”, and solve. A stationary observer or source simply gets a velocity of zero.

Check the direction before you check the arithmetic Use your experience to make sure the frequency shifts the right way. You have heard a siren pass a thousand times: approaching is higher, receding is lower. If your answer disagrees with that, a sign is wrong, and no amount of recomputing the fraction will fix it. Decide which way the answer should go before entering numbers.

Worked example 1 — a train approaching

A train approaches at 20.0 m/s and you measure its horn at 800.0 Hz. What is the horn’s actual frequency?
given:  fobs = 800.0 Hz,  vsnd = 340.0 m/s,  vsrc = +20.0 m/s,  vobs = 0 (you are stopped)
fsrc = fobs(vsnd − vsrc)(vsnd − vobs) = 800 Hz (340 − 20.0) m/s(340 − 0) m/s = 800 Hz 320 m/s340 m/s
fsrc = 752.94 Hz = 753 Hz

Approaching, so what you hear is higher than the truth — and 753 is indeed below 800. The velocity units cancel, which is the other check worth making: if they do not, a speed got into the wrong place.

Worked example 2 — after the train passes

The same train, now receding at 20.0 m/s, with its horn at a known 753 Hz. What do you record?
given:  fsrc = 753 Hz,  vsrc = −20.0 m/s (now moving against the sound that reaches you)
fobs = 753 Hz 340 m/s(340 − (−20.0)) m/s = 753 Hz 340 m/s360 m/s = 711.17 Hz = 711 Hz

Receding, so the frequency goes down, and 711 is below 753. The equation starts out with minus signs, so a source moving away supplies a second minus and the two make a plus in the denominator. Writing the −(−20.0) out rather than simplifying it in your head is how you keep that straight.

Worked example 3 — riding on the train

You are riding the train, travelling at 20.0 m/s, and its horn sounds at 753 Hz. What do you hear?
given:  vsrc = 20.0 m/s,  vobs = 20.0 m/s — both moving the same way, at the same speed
fobs = 753 Hz (340 − 20.0) m/s(340 − 20.0) m/s = 753 Hz 320 m/s320 m/s = 753 Hz · 1 = 753 Hz

No shift at all, and the fraction says so before any arithmetic: the two brackets are identical, so they cancel. The Doppler effect depends on relative motion, and there is none here. This is the example to keep in mind whenever a problem gives you two velocities — check whether they cancel before computing anything.

In short

  • The Doppler effect is heard when the source and the observer move relative to each other; the Doppler shift is the change in frequency.
  • It happens because the spacing of the already-emitted wavefronts changes. The source’s frequency and the speed of sound do not.
  • Moving towards each other raises the frequency; apart, lowers it.
  • One equation covers every case: fobs/(vsnd − vobs) = fsrc/(vsnd − vsrc), with − for moving with the sound and + for against it.
  • Think about the expected result before entering numbers, and check that the velocity units cancel.
