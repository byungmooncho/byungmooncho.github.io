Beats

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea Play two notes that are nearly but not quite the same and you do not hear two notes. You hear one note, at the average of the two, and it swells and fades at a steady rate. That throbbing is beats, the rate is the difference of the two frequencies, and it is how instruments have been tuned by ear for centuries — you adjust until the throbbing stops.
You already know this Superposition from Ch16d: two waves in the same place add, and when they drift in and out of step the sum grows and shrinks. Beats are that drift happening in time rather than across space.

Hear it first

Two minutes with a tone generator Open szynalski.com/tone-generator in two browser windows, set the frequencies close together — 440 Hz and 441 Hz to start — and press play in both. Then widen the gap and listen to what changes. Turn your volume down first. Nothing on this page means much until you have heard the throb and heard it speed up.

Why it happens

The two waves start in step, so they add — that is the loud moment. Because one has slightly more cycles per second than the other, it gradually gets ahead, and after a while it is half a cycle ahead: now they cancel, and that is the quiet moment. Keep going and it is a whole cycle ahead, back in step, loud again. The cycle of loud-and-quiet repeats once for every extra cycle the faster wave manages, which is once per second for every hertz of difference.

The two frequencies you hear
fb = f1 − f2   the BEAT frequency — how often it gets loud
fave = (f1 + f2)2   the TONE frequency — the pitch you actually hear

Take the difference as a positive number; a beat rate of −1 Hz is the same as one of +1 Hz, since you cannot hear which of the two is higher from the beating alone. And note what the ear is not doing: it does not report two pitches. It reports one, at the average, getting louder and softer.

Trevor’s three cases

Two tonesfbfaveWhat you hear
440 Hz and 441 Hz1 Hz440.5 Hzone note, swelling once a second
440 Hz and 442 Hz2 Hz441 Hzthe same note, throbbing twice as fast
440 Hz and 450 Hz10 Hz445 Hza rough, unpleasant sound — or two separate notes

After about 6 Hz of difference, most people can tell the two tones apart rather than hearing one beating note. Below that the ear fuses them; above it, the beating gets too fast to follow and starts to be heard as roughness instead, and then as two pitches. The change is gradual and it is a fact about hearing, not about the waves — the mathematics is the same at 1 Hz and at 100 Hz.

Tuning by ear

You strike a 440 Hz tuning fork and play the A string on your guitar at the same time. You count four swells in two seconds. What is the string sounding at, and what do you do next?

The beat frequency is 2 Hz, so the string is at 438 Hz or 442 Hz — the beating cannot tell you which. Tighten the string a little and listen again: if the beating slows, you were flat and you are going the right way; if it speeds up, you were sharp. Keep going until it stops. This is exactly how a piano is tuned, and it is more precise than most people's ability to judge pitch directly.

In short

  • Beats are the slow swelling and fading heard when two nearly equal frequencies interfere.
  • The beat frequency is the difference, fb = f1 − f2 — the frequency of the oscillation in volume.
  • The tone frequency is the average, fave = (f1 + f2)/2 — the pitch you hear.
  • At large differences the fusion breaks down and two distinct tones are heard instead.
