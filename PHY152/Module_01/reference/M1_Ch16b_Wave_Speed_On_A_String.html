Wave Speed on a String

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 152.

The idea How fast a wave runs along a string is fixed by the string, not by whoever plucks it. Two properties decide it: how hard the string is pulled, and how heavy a metre of it is. You can find the combination without doing any physics at all — just by insisting the units come out as metres per second. That is the whole of the first deck, and the result it reaches, v = √(FT/μ), is the whole of the second.
You already know this v = fλ from the previous page, and that a newton is kg·m/s2.

Two things a guitar makes obvious

  • A tighter string gives a higher frequency, for the same length.
  • A thicker string gives a lower frequency.

So the speed of a pulse on a string depends on the tension FT and on the mass per unit length. That second quantity gets its own name and symbol, because it is about to appear everywhere:

Linear density
μ ≡ masslength = mL   kilograms per metre
his example:  μ = 1.140 × 10−3 kg/m × (1000 g / 1 kg) = 1.140 g/m

It is not the mass of the string and not its thickness — it is mass per metre, which is what lets the same number describe a guitar string and a bridge cable. The conversion line is there because g/m is the friendlier unit to picture and kg/m is the one the formula needs.

Finding the formula from the units alone

Guess that the speed is some constant times a power of the tension and a power of the linear density, and then let the units decide the powers.

Dimensional analysis
first guess:  v = (constant) Fμ
kg·ms2 · kgm = kg2s2   — not m/s. Try again.
second guess:  v = (constant) Faμb
ms = ( kg·ms2 )a ( kgm )b = kga+b ma−b s−2a
no kg on the left:  a + b = 0  →  b = −a
one over s on the left:  −2a = −1  →  a = ½,  b = −½
v = F1/2μ−1/2 = √(FT/μ)

Three unknowns, three equations, and the exponents fall straight out: match the kilograms, match the seconds, and the metres then match on their own. Testing shows no constant factor is needed — the plain square root is the whole answer, which is unusual and worth noticing.

What dimensional analysis can and cannot do It found the shape of the relationship without a single force diagram, which is remarkable. What it cannot find is the dimensionless constant out front — here that turns out to be 1, but nothing in the units said so; that had to be measured. Any constant that does appear in a result like this is usually a unit conversion rather than physics.

Mersenne’s rules, and what they are really saying

Long before anyone wrote v = √(FT/μ), Mersenne had the behaviour of a vibrating string from experiment. The frequency is:

Mersenne’s ruleIn symbols
Inversely proportional to its lengthf ∝ 1/L
Proportional to the square root of the applied tensionf ∝ √FT
Inversely proportional to its diameterf ∝ 1/d
Inversely proportional to the square root of its densityf ∝ 1/√μ
The four rules are one equation
f = ½ 1L √(FT/μ)

The ½ is the proportionality constant, found by experiment. Rules two and four are just the square root split into its numerator and denominator; rule three is rule four in disguise, since a thicker string of the same material has more mass per metre. Four separate observations, one formula.

Putting the two together

Why the fundamental frequency is v/2L
fn = n v2L  →  f1 = v2L  →  v = f1 2L
f1 = v2L = ½1L √(FT/μ)

Mersenne’s formula and the dimensional-analysis result are the same statement, with v = fλ and λ = 2L standing between them. Ch16e builds the λ = 2L step properly; here it is enough that the two agree.

What happens if you change the tension

Double the tension, F → 2F √2 v1 ≈ 1.41 v1 √2 forig
Quadruple the tension, F → 4F 2 v1 2 forig

It takes four times the tension to get twice the frequency — an octave. That is why tuning is delicate near the top of a string’s range: the same turn of the peg buys less and less pitch as the tension rises, because the square root is flattening out. The figure shows that flattening directly.

A warning worth keeping

The table lists “tension” in kilograms. Why can a tension not be 9.03 kg?

Because tension is a force, and a kilogram is a mass. String makers quote the mass whose weight would pull that hard, which is a real convention but not a force. Convert before using it: FT = mg. Trevor multiplies by 10 m/s2, not 9.8, so his 9.03 kg becomes 90.3 N — and that choice is carried through the worked example below so the numbers match his.

Worked example — checking the length of a guitar string

The A string is tightened to “9.03 kg”, has μ = 4.466 × 10−3 kg/m, and sounds at 110 Hz. How fast does the wave travel, and how long is the string?
(a)  FT = (9.03 kg)(10 m/s2) = 90.3 N
v = √(FT/μ) = √(90.3 kg·m/s2 ÷ 4.466 × 10−3 kg/m)
v = √(20219.4 m2/s2) = 142.19 m/s = 142 m/s
(b)  λ = vf = 142.19 m/s110 1/s = 1.293 m
fundamental:  L = ½λ = ½(1.293 m) = 0.65 m

Watch the kilograms cancel in the square root: kg·m/s2 divided by kg/m is m2/s2, whose root is m/s. If the kilograms do not cancel, the tension was left in kg.

Is 0.65 m reasonable? Yes — and that is the point of the question. The D’Addario string data Trevor quotes was measured on a 0.648 m scale length, which is the standard for a classical guitar. Getting 0.65 m from a frequency, a tension and a linear density, with no ruler anywhere in the calculation, is the strongest possible check that v = √(FT/μ) is right. (Source for the string data: donaldsauter.com.)

In short

  • The speed of a wave on a string is set by the string: v = √(FT/μ).
  • μ = m/L is the linear density, in kg/m.
  • Unit analysis alone finds the relationship between the variables in a system — it cannot find the dimensionless constant, which here happens to be 1.
  • Because the speed goes as the square root of the tension, four times the tension gives twice the frequency.
  • “Tension” quoted in kilograms is a mass; multiply by g before using it.
