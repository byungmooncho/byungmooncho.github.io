Standing Waves

SynopsisWe look at the conditions for forming a standing wave and derive equations describing the standing wave.
Before you read on — predict

A guitar string, clamped at both ends, plays one definite note.  Three things you could change: fret it, so the vibrating part is shorter, tighten the tuning peg, pluck it harder.  Which of those change the note?  Write down your three answers.  By the end of this note all three fall out of one formula.

1.  Standing Waves

We start with a moving wave (forward going)

A+(x, t) = A0 sin(kx − ωt)

If we pluck a guitar string (fixed at both ends) we launch a moving wave.  If we continue to strum, we launch multiple waves, all forward moving.

★  What happens when the forward going wave reaches the fixed end?

They bounce back — 180° out of phase with respect to the incoming wave — and start to move in the opposite direction, so the returning waves are

A−(x, t) = A0 sin(kx + ωt)

The overall effect of the interference between the forward and the returning waves is that they create the standing wave.

  1. Write the forward-going wave, and then the returning wave after it has reflected off the fixed end.
  2. The string carries both at once.  Write the total displacement as a sum.
  3. Which trig identity turns that sum into a product?  Use it, and see what cancels.
  4. Look at what is left.  Which factor depends only on x, and which only on t?  What does that say about where the zeros are?
1)  A+(x,t) = A0 sin(kx − ωt) going one way, and after the reflection A−(x,t) = A0 sin(kx + ωt) coming back.  — the sign of the ωt term is what carries the direction, from Note 3 2)  They are on the same string at the same time, so the displacements add: Astand(x,t) = A+(x,t) + A−(x,t) = A0[ sin(kx − ωt) + sin(kx + ωt) ] 3)  Using the trig identities sin(a + b) = sin a cos b + cos a sin b sin(a − b) = sin a cos b − cos a sin b Astand(x,t) = A0[ (sin kx cos ωt − cos kx sin ωt) + (sin kx cos ωt + cos kx sin ωt) ] The two cos kx sin ωt terms are equal and opposite, so they go, and the two surviving terms are identical: Astand(x,t) = A0[ 2 sin kx cos ωt ] = 2A0 (sin kx)(cos ωt) 4)  x appears only inside sin kx, and t only inside cos ωt.  The shape along the string is therefore always the same shape; cos ωt only scales it up and down and flips it over.  — compare Note 3, where x and t appeared only in the combination x − vt and the shape moved. Here they have come apart, and nothing travels Wherever sin kx = 0, the string is at rest for all time.  Those points are the nodes.
Astand(x, t) = 2A0 sin kx cos ωt
The standing wave at full amplitude (solid) and at four later moments (dashed).  sin kx is the envelope — it fixes the shape and pins the nodes — and cos ωt does nothing but breathe the whole thing in and out.

Standing waves on a string with 2 fixed ends

The speed is fixed by the string itself, not by us:

v = √(T/μ)   and   v = λf

so once the string is strung and tuned, choosing a wavelength chooses the frequency.  The clamped ends decide which wavelengths are allowed.

  1. The string is clamped at x = 0 and at x = L.  What must the standing wave do there, for every t?  What does that force on sin kx?
  2. Sketch the first four shapes that satisfy it, and read the wavelength of each off the picture in terms of L.
  3. Use v = λf to turn each wavelength into a frequency.  Then write λn and fn for the nth.
  4. v is a property of the string.  Of its length, its tension, and how hard you pluck it, which ones change fn?
5)  A clamped end cannot move, so Astand = 0 at both ends for every t.  At x = 0 that is free — sin 0 = 0 already.  At x = L it is a condition: sin kL = 0, so kL = nπ, and with k = 2π/λ that is L = nλ/2   —   a whole number of half-wavelengths fits between the clamps 6)  One loop, two, three, four — the figure below.  Reading each off: λ1 = 2L/1,   λ2 = 2L/2,   λ3 = 2L/3,   λ4 = 2L/4 7)  v = λf gives f = v/λ, so f1 = 1v/2L,   f2 = 2v/2L,   f3 = 3v/2L,   f4 = 4v/2L and for the nth harmonic λn = 2L/n,   fn = n v/2L 8)  L and T do; plucking harder does not.  L is underneath fn, and T is inside v = √(T/μ), which is on top.  — the amplitude never appears in either formula, which is exactly why a guitar is playable: you can make a note louder without making it sharp
The first four allowed shapes on a string of length L, clamped at both ends.  Each dashed curve is the same mode half a period later.  The dots are the nodes; the nth harmonic has n loops and n + 1 of them.
The punchline λn = 2L/n,    fn = n v/2L

A string clamped at both ends cannot vibrate at just any frequency.  It has a discrete set, evenly spaced, and every one is a whole-number multiple of the lowest.  Nothing in this argument is about guitars: it is what happens to any wave shut into a box, and the same counting comes back for a pipe of air, a microwave cavity and an electron in an atom.

Looking ahead

You now have the shapes a trapped wave is allowed to take.  Note 4a asks what it is carrying: the energy in an oscillation, where it sits at each moment, and the speed of the oscillator at any point of its swing.

Where this sits. Note 3 built one travelling wave, f(x − vt), and got its speed out of the string. This note sends two of those at each other. The sum of two travelling waves is not a travelling wave at all — x and t come apart, the shape stops moving, and the clamps then throw away all but a discrete list of it.
Back to your prediction

Fret it: higher.  L is underneath fn = nv/2L, so halving the vibrating length doubles every frequency — an octave, which is why the 12th fret sits at the middle of the string.  Tighten it: higher.  T is on top of v = √(T/μ) and v is on top of fn.  Pluck harder: no change.  Louder, same note — the amplitude appears nowhere in λn or fn.  That is the same shape of answer as Note 3 gave for the speed of a pulse, and for the same reason: the medium sets the terms.
