Sound Is A Pressure Wave

SynopsisNotes 1 to 8 built the wave in general and then trapped one between two ends.  This note asks what a sound wave is, and the answer is that it is a pressure wave.  The air does not travel across the room with the sound — it crowds together and spreads apart in place, and what travels is the pattern.  Everything else here follows from that: how fast the pattern moves, and what the medium has to do with it.
Before you read on — predict

Clap once in an empty room.  (1) Does any air make the trip from your hands to the far wall?  (2) At the instant the air at some spot is squeezed hardest, is that same air also moving fastest?  (3) Sound runs through steel about fifteen times faster than through air.  Is that because steel is heavier, or because it is stiffer?  Write down your three answers.

Performance expectations

  2. State that displacement and pressure run a quarter wavelength out of step, and say which node goes with which antinode.
  3. Explain the speed of sound as a stiffness over an inertia, and connect √(B/ρ) to √(FT/μ).
  4. Use v = 331.5 + 0.610T to find a speed from a temperature, and a temperature from a speed.
  5. Compute a wavelength from a frequency, and check the answer against the size of a real object.

1.  Compressions and rarefactions

A sound wave is a longitudinal wave in a material: the medium vibrates along the line the wave travels, not across it.  Each molecule slides a little way forward and back about its own spot.  Where the molecules crowd, the pressure sits above the everyday value P0; where they spread out, it sits below.  Those are the compressions and the rarefactions, and one wavelength is the distance from one compression to the next.

  1. Define a compression and a rarefaction in terms of pressure rather than crowding.
  2. The molecule picture and the pressure graph are the same wave drawn twice.  Which of the two does a microphone actually measure, and why does that matter for the mathematics you are about to use?
1)  A compression is a pressure maximum; a rarefaction is a pressure minimum.  — both are measured against P0, the pressure the room is at when nothing is sounding; a sound wave is a small wobble on top of a large steady pressure 2)  A microphone measures pressure.  It cannot see molecules, and what it hands you is an ordinary sinusoid oscillating about P0.  — which is the useful part: once the wave is written as a pressure, a longitudinal wave takes exactly the same mathematics as the transverse ones in Notes 7 and 8, and nothing from those notes has to be relearned
Figure 1.  One instant.  Every molecule has been moved off its resting place by the same rule, and the pressure curve below is drawn from that same rule — so the crowding and the curve agree by construction rather than by drawing.  No molecule travels; each is displaced a little and will come back.

2.  Displacement and pressure are not in step

This is the idea worth the most in this note.  Two things vary along the wave: how far the molecules swing, and what the pressure does.  They do not peak in the same places.  They are a quarter of a wavelength apart.

Figure 2.  Displacement drawn as a cosine and pressure as a sine of the same k.  Where the molecules swing hardest they are not being squeezed, and where they do not move at all the squeeze is greatest.
  1. Finish the sentence from the figure, both ways round: a displacement node is a pressure ________, and a displacement antinode is a pressure ________.
  2. Why should that be?  Think about what the molecules on either side of a displacement node are doing at the moment the node itself is standing still.
3)  A displacement node is a pressure antinode.  A displacement antinode is a pressure node. 4)  At a displacement node the air itself cannot move, but the air on either side is moving toward it and then away from it.  Squeeze from both sides, then stretch from both sides — the biggest pressure swing anywhere.  At an antinode the molecules move together, as a block, so nothing is compressed and the pressure barely changes.  — students who have not separated these two words get every air-column question backwards, because a closed end is a displacement node and therefore a pressure antinode

Where the quarter wavelength comes from

The figure asserts the offset.  One line of calculus produces it.  Squeeze a slab of air and the pressure change depends on how much the displacement is changing across the slab — not on the displacement itself:

p(x,t) = −B ∂s/∂x

with B the bulk modulus and s the displacement.  Take the ordinary travelling wave of Note 7 and put it in:

s(x,t) = smax cos(kx − ωt) ∂s/∂x = −k smax sin(kx − ωt) p(x,t) = B k smax sin(kx − ωt) — a sine, where the displacement was a cosine

A sine against a cosine is a quarter cycle apart, which in space is a quarter wavelength.  The offset is not a convention and not a drawing choice: it is what differentiating a cosine does.  It also hands you the amplitude of the pressure wave for free, pmax = B k smax — so a high-frequency sound of the same displacement amplitude is a louder one, because k is bigger.

3.  What the speed depends on

Sound needs something to travel in.  Pump the air out of a jar with a bell ringing inside and the bell goes quiet while you can still see it striking: there is nothing left to compress.  What is left to ask is what about the medium sets the speed — and you have met the answer already, in a different costume.

5)  v = √(FT/µ) — the tension measures how hard the string pulls back when you displace it, and µ measures how much string there is to move.  — a stiffness over an inertia, under a square root 6)  Gases around 300 m/s, liquids around 1,300, solids around 4,000.  Sound goes faster in stiffer, more tightly bound media: the push reaches the next molecule sooner when the neighbours are held closely. Hydrogen is the outlier, and it is the one that proves the shape of the formula.  It is a gas, so it is not stiff — but it is extremely light, and the inertia sits underneath the square root.  Hydrogen is about 14.5 times lighter than air per molecule, and √14.5 = 3.8, so it should carry sound about 3.8 times faster: 331.5 × 3.8 ≈ 1,260 m/s against the measured 1,269.  — the rule is not "gases are slow"; it is stiffness over inertia, and hydrogen wins it back on the bottom
The punchline v = √(B/ρ)

Every wave speed in this module has the same shape: a stiffness over an inertia, under a square root.  On a string it was the tension over the mass per length.  In a fluid it is the bulk modulus B, which measures how hard the material resists being squeezed, over the density ρ, which measures how much of it there is to move.  In a solid rod the bulk modulus is replaced by Young's modulus and nothing else changes.  Stiff and light is fast; floppy and heavy is slow.

This is not a coincidence.  Note 7 derived the wave equation ∂²y/∂x² = (1/v²) ∂²y/∂t² for a string and read the speed off the constant in front.  Run the same argument on a slab of fluid, with p = −B ∂s/∂x in place of the tension, and the identical equation comes out with v² = B/ρ.  One derivation, two media — which is why the formulas look the same.

4.  And on temperature

For air there is one more dependence worth carrying, and it is a straight line.

Figure 3.  Intercept 331.5 m/s at 0 °C, slope 0.610 (m/s) per °C, so v = 331.5 + 0.610T with T in °C.
  1. Room temperature is about 70 °F, which is about 20 °C.  What is the speed of sound there?
  2. You run an experiment and measure 338 m/s.  How warm was the room?
  3. You see lightning and hear the thunder 10 seconds later.  How far away did it strike?
7)  v = 331.5 + 0.610(20) = 343.7 m/s.  — this is where the 343 m/s you have been using all semester comes from; it was never a constant, it was room temperature 8)  T = (338 − 331.5)/0.610 = 10.7 °C, about 51 °F.  Cold. 9)  d = vt = 343 × 10 = 3,430 m, about 3.4 km or just over two miles.  — the rule of thumb, three seconds to the kilometre or five to the mile, is this calculation; light arrives effectively instantly, so the delay is all sound

5.  One wavelength, to finish

With a speed in hand, v = fλ from Note 2 turns any pitch into a length.

An A3 on a cello, 220 Hz, in room-temperature air: λ = v/f = 343 / 220 = 1.56 m A hertz is a per-second, so m/s divided by 1/s leaves metres.  A metre and a half is about the length of a cello — roughly the right size for an instrument that plays that note, which is the sort of check worth making every time.

Looking ahead

You now know what is travelling and how fast.  Note 10 counts the energy a wave carries, Note 11 follows that energy outwards into intensity and decibels — both of which are about sound, and now have a medium to be about — and Note 12 sets the source moving.

Where this sits. Note 8 trapped a wave between two ends. This note says what the wave is made of when the medium is air, and gives it a speed. Nothing here is new mathematics: the pressure wave is the same sinusoid as every wave since Note 2, and v = √(B/ρ) is the same stiffness-over-inertia as the string.
Back to your prediction

(1) No air makes the trip.  Each molecule slides a little way and comes back; what crosses the room is the pattern and the energy it carries.  Sound in a room is not a draught.  (2) No — the opposite.  Squeeze and swing are a quarter wavelength apart: where the air is squeezed hardest it is momentarily not moving at all.  (3) Because it is stiffer.  Steel is far denser than air, which on its own would make sound slower; it wins because its stiffness beats its density by much more than the density costs it.
