Beats and Standing Waves in Tubes

SynopsisTwo more things that fall out of adding waves together.  Put two waves of nearly the same frequency into the same place and the loudness itself rises and falls.  Shut a wave into a tube of air instead of onto a string and the same counting comes back — but one closed end changes the answer.
Before you read on — predict

Two flutes play together, one at 440 Hz and one at 444 Hz.  (1) What do you hear — one note, two notes, or something else?  (2) Now take two pipes of the same length, one open at both ends and one stopped at one end, and blow across each.  Which plays the lower note?  Write down both answers.  The second one catches most people.

Performance expectations
  1. Add two waves of nearly equal frequency and show that the result is a single tone whose loudness is modulated.
  2. Compute the beat frequency from two frequencies, and use it the way a tuner does.
  3. Say where the nodes and antinodes must sit in a pipe open at one end and closed at the other, and why.
  4. Find the wavelengths and frequencies of the harmonics of a stopped pipe, and explain why only odd harmonics appear.
  5. Determine the speed of sound from air-column measurements by plotting frequency against inverse wavelength.

Beats

Note 8 put two waves of the same frequency into one string travelling in opposite directions, and got a standing wave.  Change one thing.  Send two waves the same way, at nearly the same frequency, into the same patch of air.  The identity that does the work is the one Note 8 already used.

  1. Write the two waves arriving at one point as y1 = A sin(2πf1t) and y2 = A sin(2πf2t), and add them with the sum-to-product identity.
  2. Which factor in your answer is the tone you hear, and which is the slow swelling and fading?
  3. The envelope goes through zero twice in each of its cycles.  So what is the frequency of the loudness?
1)  sin P + sin Q = 2 cos[(P−Q)/2] sin[(P+Q)/2] — the same identity as Note 8, used on two frequencies instead of two directions.  So y = 2A cos(2π f1−f2⁄2 t) · sin(2π f1+f2⁄2 t) 2)  The sin factor carries the average of the two frequencies — that is the pitch you hear, and for 440 and 444 it is 442 Hz.  The cos factor is slow, and it multiplies the amplitude: it is an envelope, exactly as the decaying exponential was in Note 5. 3)  The envelope's own frequency is (f1−f2)/2, but loudness does not care about sign: the sound is just as loud at −2A as at +2A.  Each envelope cycle therefore gives two loud moments, and fbeat = | f1 − f2 | — the factor of two is the whole trap here; the envelope frequency and the beat frequency are not the same number
Two waves of nearly equal frequency, and their sum.  The sum is a single tone at the average frequency, inside a slow envelope.  Each time the envelope pinches to zero the sound dies away; that happens Δf times a second.

Listen.  Two pure tones, generated in your browser.  Start them a long way apart, then bring them together and listen for the moment the beating slows down to nothing.  That is what a piano tuner is listening for.

Standing Waves in Tubes

Note 8 ended by saying that the counting it had just done was not really about guitars: it is what happens to any wave shut into a box, and the same counting comes back for a pipe of air.  Here is that pipe.  Only one thing has to change, and it is the boundary condition — what the wave is forced to do at each end.

A closed end is a wall: the air there cannot move, so the displacement must be zero.  An open end is free: the air there moves as much as it can.  In his own words, completing the sentence:

The rule Nodes are always at the closed ends,
antinodes are always at the open ends

Everything else about a pipe follows from fitting a sine wave between those two requirements.

  1. A pipe of length L, closed at one end and open at the other.  Sketch the longest wave that can fit, with a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open one.  What fraction of a wavelength fits in the pipe?
  2. Add half a wave at a time and see which ones still obey both ends.  Which harmonics survive, and which are impossible?
  3. Take L = 1.5 m and the speed of sound at STP, v = 331 m/s.  Find λ and f for the harmonics that survive.
6)  Node at one end, antinode at the other, and nothing in between: that is a quarter of a wave.  So 1⁄4λ = L, and λ1 = 4L. 7)  Each extra half wave keeps a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open one, so the lengths that fit are 1⁄4, 3⁄4, 5⁄4… of a wavelength.  Only the ODD harmonics exist — there is no second harmonic in a stopped pipe, because an even one would need an antinode where the wall is. λn = 4L⁄n  and  fn = nv⁄4L,  n = 1, 3, 5… 8)  With L = 1.5 m and v = 331 m/s:

The first three harmonics of a pipe closed at the left and open at the right.  The solid and dashed curves are the two extremes of the same standing wave.  A node sits at the wall and an antinode at the opening in every one, which is what forces the quarter, three-quarters and five-quarters.

And if the pipe is open at both ends, both ends must be antinodes.  The longest wave that fits is then a half wave rather than a quarter, so λn = 2L/n with every n, odd and even alike.  An open pipe of a given length therefore plays an octave higher than a stopped one of the same length, and it has twice as many harmonics to call on — which is most of why a stopped organ pipe sounds hollow beside an open one.  That answers the second half of the prediction above.

Worked example — the speed of sound from an air column

A tuning fork of known frequency is held over a tube standing in water.  Raise or lower the water and the air column above it changes length; at one particular length the sound jumps in loudness, because the column has been tuned to resonate at the fork's frequency.  That length is a quarter of a wavelength, by the rule above.  Six forks, six lengths:

The fit gives 329 m/s against the 331 m/s in the reference table: low by about 0.7%.  Why low?  The column behaves as though it were slightly longer than it is measured to be — the antinode sits a little outside the open end rather than exactly at it (the end correction, roughly 0.6 of the tube's radius).  Every measured λ is therefore slightly short, so every f sits slightly left of where it belongs, and the slope comes out low.  Room temperature matters too, and in the opposite direction.
Frequency against inverse wavelength for the six measurements.  A straight line through the origin, and its slope is the speed of sound.  Plot the thing that makes your relationship a straight line, and the quantity you are after becomes a slope rather than six separate answers to be averaged.
The punchline fbeat = | f1−f2 |    λn = 4L⁄n  (n odd)

Both of these are the same move as Note 8 — add two waves and look at what survives.  Beats come from adding along the frequency axis; the pipe comes from adding along the direction axis and then letting the ends decide what is allowed.  A boundary condition is not a detail: it is what picks the notes an instrument can play.

Where this sits. That closes Module 1.  It began with a wave on a string you could watch, turned it into an oscillator and an equation of motion, drove that oscillator, let the disturbance travel, shut it into a string and then into a tube, and worked out what it carries and what happens when the source moves.  Everything after this has the same shape: write what is happening, impose what the situation forces, and read off what survives.
Back to your prediction

(1) One note, throbbing.  Not two notes — 440 and 444 are far too close for your ear to separate — and not a steady tone either.  You hear 442 Hz swelling and fading four times a second.  (2) The stopped pipe plays lower, by an octave.  Most people guess the open one, on the reasoning that closing an end must "shorten" it; in fact the closed end buys you a node, and a node-to-antinode fit needs four pipe lengths of wavelength where open-to-open needs only two.
