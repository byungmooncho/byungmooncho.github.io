Synopsis

What are waves, and why do we study waves in physics?  Waves can be actual things, such as ocean waves, sound waves, or waves in a slinky when shaken side to side.  Waves can also be a description of motion that goes back and forth, such as a pendulum swing or a planet orbiting a sun.  The “wave motions” are repetitive, oscillatory, or cyclical; whether they are a thing or description, these cyclical behaviors are ubiquitous (i.e., all around us) and are all mathematically described using sine or cosine functions (or some combination).  So, if there are repetitions (whatever it may be, thing, motion, or even stuff), you can bet that any combination of sine or cosine functions will describe this repetition.  You will be surprised by the ubiquitousness of wave-like (repetitive) things or behaviors in nature and how cosinusoidal (combining sine and cosine) math functions with only three parameters (amplitude, wavelength, and frequency; more on these later) describe them all.

Why study waves?  Because let me repeat: Actually, everything is a wave.  You will agree with me when we study Modern Physics.  For now, I will say that initially, waves as a thing were thought to be distinct and behave quite differently from things like rocks or cannonballs, in that unlike rocks or cannonballs, which can be thought of as particles (localized things), ocean waves, sound waves, or waves in a string are spread out and do things that particles can never do, such as go through each other, add or subtract from each other.  For this Unit, waves are a necessary addition to the particle-centric worldview we are familiar with, such as a flying cannonball whose position and velocity can be pinpointed.  Since a wave is spread out (imagine the peaks and troughs of an ocean wave extending into the horizon), we can’t pinpoint where the wave is (it’s “everywhere”) like we can with a particle, thus requiring a different description using cosinusoidal functions.  Note that waves can be moving things, just like a flying cannonball is a moving thing, but their repetitive features are spread out in both space and time!  So, when you look at these moving waves, you can freeze time (like taking a snapshot with a camera) and see its spatial features at a given time or stay at a fixed position in space and let the waves come at you as time passes to see the temporal features at a given position.

As we begin this Module, I want you to explore the characteristics of waves using PhET simulation, “Waves On A String.”  How do you generate waves in a string?  How do you describe a wave, i.e., the parameters needed to define a wave fully?  How do you make waves with different features?

19) No — changing the frequency does not change the speed.  Double the frequency and the wavelength halves, so λf is unchanged.  That is the point of the table: the speed is set by the string — how tight it is and how heavy it is — and not by how you shake it.  Frequency is yours to choose; the string then decides the wavelength.
20) No.  v = λf has no amplitude in it, so there is nothing for the amplitude to change.  Slide the amplitude up and down with the wave running and the peaks get taller, but they arrive just as often and sit just as far apart.
21) Yes — more tension, faster.  Tighten the string and the wave runs along it more quickly, which means the wavelength grows at the same frequency.  This is the first half of the answer to “what sets the speed”; the other half is how heavy the string is, which this simulation does not let you change.
22) Damping takes energy out of the string as it goes.  What you see is the amplitude dying away along the string — big at the driven end, smaller further along.  It does not change the frequency, and it does not change the wavelength.
23) Yes, it is a wave.  It is a disturbance that travels along the string, carrying energy with it, while the string itself only moves up and down and stays where it is — which is the whole definition.  What it is not is periodic: it happens once, so it has no frequency and no wavelength, and λf has nothing to put into it.  It still has a speed, and it is the same speed as before — set by the string, exactly as item 19 said.

1) (b) higher.  The shape moves right; the bead does not.  One frame later, whatever was just to A’s left is now at A’s position — and just to A’s left is the peak.  So A rises.  (d) and (e) are the trap: the pulse goes right, the rope does not go anywhere.
2) (c) narrower.  A quicker flick means the hand is up and back down in less time, and the pulse is as long as the hand was moving — less time at the same speed is less rope.
3) (b) taller.  How far his hand travels is how far the rope is displaced, and that is the amplitude.  It is item 9 again: how hard you drive sets the height and nothing else.
4) (b) faster.  Item 21.  The speed belongs to the rope, and tightening the rope is a change to the rope.
5) (b) higher, for exactly the reason in 1 — A is on the descending face just past a crest, so the crest is the thing about to arrive.
6) (b) shifted right.  The pattern travels; the hand stays where it is.
7) (c) closer together.  Shaking faster is a higher frequency, and the speed is still the rope’s — so λ = v/f gets smaller.  This is the same sentence as item 19, asked about a hand instead of a slider.
8) (b) increase its wavelength.  Same relation, read the other way: lower f at fixed v means longer λ.  (a) is wrong because the speed is the string’s; (c) is wrong because that is amplitude; (d) is wrong because a lower frequency is a longer period.

Looking ahead

You now have the three parameters the synopsis promised — amplitude, wavelength and frequency — and the two relations that tie them together, T = 1/f and v = λf.  You also have the fact that does most of the work for the rest of the module: the speed belongs to the medium, not to whoever is shaking it.  Note 1a fills in the vocabulary around those parameters; Note 2 goes back to the one oscillating bead and asks what force makes it move that way, which is where the sines and cosines of the synopsis actually come from.

Where this sits. Everything here was measured, not derived — a ruler, a timer and a slider.  That is deliberate: the definitions have to be yours before the algebra is worth doing.  From Note 2 on, the same quantities arrive the other way round, out of Newton’s second law.
