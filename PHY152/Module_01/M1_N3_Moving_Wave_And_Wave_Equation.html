Moving Wave and Wave Equation

SynopsisA spring-mass system can undergo oscillatory motion, and Hooke’s Law combined with Newton’s Second Law enables us to write an equation of motion whose solution is described by the simple harmonic motion, SHM (see below).
Before you read on — predict

A pulse runs along a stretched string at some speed.  Three things you could change: pull the string tighter, swap it for a heavier string of the same length, shake it faster.  Which of those change the speed of the pulse?  Write down your three answers.  The last section derives the speed from scratch and settles all three at once.

Notes 2 and 2a got that equation of motion and solved it:

d²x/dt² = −(k/m)x   with solution    x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ)

where ω is the angular frequency in rad/s and φ is the phase angle.

The SHM describes the amplitude of the back-and-forth oscillation as a function of time only.  Since the oscillations occur at a fixed position, say, x = 0 on an x-axis (so the amplitude variation is in the y-axis), the amplitude does not vary along the x-axis.  The oscillations or the “wave motion” does not “move”.

1.  Moving wave

Now, let’s consider ocean waves or waves on a string.  Here, the “disturbances” do move in space or along the string.  To see this, imagine taking a snapshot of the string at one time and taking another snapshot at another time.  When you compare the two, you will find that the wave is simply shifted in space and the amount of shift in space will depend on the speed at which the wave moved.  Mathematically, we can denote this shift as follows:

The same disturbance, photographed twice.  Nothing about its shape has changed — it has only moved along x, and how far it moved is the speed times the time.
  1. Call the shape at t = 0 simply A(x).  If the whole shape travels a distance vt to the right, what is the height at position x at time t, written in terms of the original function?
  2. What general form must any rightward-moving wave therefore have?
  3. Check it against a familiar one: show that A(x, t) = A0 cos(kx − ωt) has that form, and read off its speed.
  4. Rewrite that wave using the wavelength and the frequency instead of k and ω.
1)  The height now at x is whatever the height used to be at x − vt, because that is the bit of the shape that has arrived: A(x) → A(x − vt) This is akin to function translation — the same trick that moves y = f(x) to the right by replacing x with x − (shift). 2)  So formally, a moving wave has the form A = f(x − vt) — any function at all, as long as x and t appear only in that combination. That is a strong statement: it says the shape is irrelevant and the pairing of x with t is everything. 3)  A(x,t) = A0 cos(kx − ωt) = A0 cos[k(x − (ω/k)t)] ≡ f(x − vt) if v = ω/k.  A forward-going wave: as t increases, x must increase to keep the bracket the same. 4)  With ω = 2πf and k = 2π/λ: A(x,t) = A0 cos[(2π/λ)(x − vt)] — and v = ω/k = (2πf)/(2π/λ) = λf, which is Note 1 item 18 arriving a second time by a completely different route

2.  Wave equation

Section 1 says what a moving wave looks like.  It does not say why a string should carry one, or how fast.  For that, do to a piece of string exactly what Note 2 did to a block: write down Newton’s second law for it.  We are deriving an equation of motion for a mechanical wave.

A short element of the string, of mass m = μΔx, with μ the mass per unit length.  The tension pulls along the string at each end — and because the ends have different slopes, the two pulls do not cancel.
  1. Write the mass of the element and its vertical acceleration, using D for the displacement of the string.  Then write ma for the element.
  2. Write the net vertical force as the sum of the vertical parts of the two tensions.  What happens to the horizontal parts, and why does that matter?
  3. Express each vertical part using the slope of the string at that end.  Mind the sign at the left-hand end.
  4. Put the two sides together, divide by μΔx, and let Δx → 0.  What is the bracket in the limit?
  5. Check the units of Th/μ.  What must that combination be?
5)  m = μΔx, and the string moves only up and down, so vy = ∂D/∂t and ay = ∂²D/∂t².  So may = μΔx ∂²D/∂t² = Fnet 6)  Fnet = Ty(x+Δx) + Ty(x).  The horizontals cancel — the element is not going anywhere sideways, so the two horizontal pulls are equal and opposite and share one value, Th.  — which is what makes this tractable: one tension, two slopes 7)  The slope of the string is ∂D/∂x, and vertical = slope × horizontal: Ty(x+Δx) = (slope at x+Δx) × Th = (∂D/∂x)Th Ty(x) = −(slope at x) × Th = −(∂D/∂x)Th — minus, because that end pulls back down the string so Fnet = Th[ ∂D/∂x|x+Δx − ∂D/∂x|x ] 8)  Setting the two expressions equal and dividing both sides by μΔx: ∂²D/∂t² = (Th/μ) × [ ∂D/∂x|x+Δx − ∂D/∂x|x ] / Δx and that bracket is a derivative — df/dx = limΔx→0 [f(x+Δx) − f(x)]/Δx — of the slope.  The derivative of the slope is the second derivative: ∂²D/∂t² = (Th/μ) ∂²D/∂x² 9)  F is in N = kg m/s² and μ is in kg/m, so F/μ = (kg m/s²)/(kg/m) = m²/s² — which is a velocity squared.  — the units do not merely allow it, they force it: there is nothing else that combination could be
The punchline ∂²ψ/∂t² = v² ∂²ψ/∂x²

The wave equation, with v² = Th/μ for a string.  Two things are worth seeing at once. The speed came out of the string — its tension and its mass per length — and nothing about the shaking appears in it.  And ψ is deliberately not called D: the same equation turns up for sound, for light, for a rope and for a column of air, and every time it does, whatever is waving travels at the v that equation contains.

Looking ahead

You now have both halves: what a travelling wave must look like, f(x − vt), and why a medium carries one at a speed of its own choosing.  Note 4 sends two of them at each other.  A wave travelling right meets its own reflection travelling left, and the sum of the two does something neither one does alone: it stops moving, and only certain wavelengths survive.

Where this sits. Section 2 is Note 2's derivation done to a piece of string instead of a block — the same Newton's second law, the same "write the net force and see what it is proportional to". The difference is that a string has neighbours, so the restoring force comes from the curvature rather than from a spring, and the result is an equation in two variables instead of one.
Back to your prediction

Tighter: faster.  Th is on top of v² = Th/μ.  Heavier string: slower.  μ is underneath.  Shaking faster: no change at all.  Neither the frequency nor the amplitude appears anywhere in the derivation — shake it faster and the wavelength shortens to keep λf equal to the speed the string already had.  That is the same answer Note 1 got from a simulation and a ruler, and it is now a theorem rather than an observation.
