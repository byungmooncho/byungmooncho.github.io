Springs, Simple Pendulums, Damped and Driven Oscillations — Examples of Oscillatory Motion

SynopsisWe explore a few examples of oscillatory motions: Springs, and simple pendulums.
Before you read on — predict

A pendulum clock keeps time by swinging a bob on a string.  Three things you could change: use a heavier bob, use a longer string, take it up a mountain.  Which of those change how long a swing takes?  Write down your three answers before you read on.  One of them catches almost everybody.

Performance expectations
  1. Solve the spring equation of motion and obtain ω = √(k/m).
  2. Show that a pendulum at small angles is simple harmonic, with ω = √(g/L).
  3. State the small-angle approximation and say at what angle it stops being safe.
  4. Explain why a pendulum’s period does not depend on the mass of the bob where a spring’s does.
  5. Describe what damping does to the amplitude of an oscillation, and what it very nearly does not do to the period.

Spring Oscillation

Note 4 got as far as ma = −kx: the force is proportional to the displacement.  That is a statement about the system, and it is not yet a statement about time — nothing in it says how long a swing takes.  Getting the period out means treating the acceleration as what it is, the second derivative of position, and then solving the result rather than describing it.

  1. Write the acceleration as a derivative of position, in two steps, and so turn a = −(k/m)x into an equation of motion.  What kind of equation is it?
  2. Guess a solution of the form x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ0), where A, ω and φ0 are constants.  Differentiate it once to get v, and again to get a.
  3. Put your expression for a back into the equation of motion and cancel what you can.  What does ω have to be?
  4. Turn ω into a frequency.  What happens to f if the spring is stiffer?  If the mass is larger?
1)  v = dx/dt, so a = dv/dt = (d/dt)(dx/dt) = d²x/dt².  So d²x/dt² = −(k/m)x which is the equation of motion — a second-order differential equation: it relates a function to its own second derivative. 2)  Guess a solution!  With x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ0): dx/dt = v = −Aω sin(ωt + φ0) d²x/dt² = dv/dt = a = −Aω² cos(ωt + φ0) — which is −ω² times the x you started with. That is the whole reason the guess works. 3)  Since a = −(k/m)x: −Aω² cos(ωt+φ0) = −(k/m) A cos(ωt+φ0) The amplitude and the cosine are the same on both sides and cancel, leaving ω² = k/m: ω = √(k/m) 4)  ω = 2πf, so f = (1/2π)√(k/m).  Stiffer spring, f high.  Larger mass, f low.  — and notice what is missing: no amplitude. How far you pull it does not change how often it oscillates.

Simple Pendulum

The same question for a different restoring force.  A bob on a string of length L, pulled aside through an angle θ: there is no spring here, so what pulls it back?  Gravity does — but only the part of it that lies along the arc.  The string takes care of the rest.

The bob moves along the arc, so only the tangential component of the weight does anything — tangential motion only.  The radial component is balanced by the tension.  The arc length is s = θL, so θ = s/L.
  1. Write Newton’s second law for the tangential direction only.  Which forces appear, and which do not?
  2. Resolve the weight and put it in.  Then cancel the mass.  What is at?
  3. For a small swing, replace sin θ with θ, and then replace θ using the arc length.  What equation of motion do you get?
  4. Compare it with the spring’s equation of motion.  Write down ω without doing any more work.
5)  (Fnet)t = ΣFt = (Fg)t = mat.  The tension is entirely radial, so it does not appear at all; nor does the radial part of the weight.  — that is what "tangential motion only" buys you: a one-dimensional problem along a curved line 6)  (Fg)t = −Fg sin θ = −mg sin θ, so mat = −mg sin θ and at = −g sin θ.  — the mass cancels, and that is the answer to one third of the prediction above 7)  For small θ, sin θ ≈ θ, so at = −gθ.  And θ = s/L, while at is the second derivative of the arc length, so d²s/dt² = −g(s/L) = −(g/L)s 8)  That is the spring equation with s for x and g/L for k/m.  So the solution is s(t) = A cos(ωt + φ0) with ω = √(g/L) — no second derivation needed. Once two systems have the same equation of motion they have the same behaviour, whatever they are made of. That is most of what "simple harmonic motion" is for.
The punchline ω = √(k/m)    ω = √(g/L)

Two completely different systems, one equation of motion, one solution.  Neither frequency contains the amplitude, and the pendulum’s does not contain the mass.  A heavy bob and a light bob on the same string keep the same time, which is exactly why a pendulum makes a usable clock.

Damped Oscillation

Real oscillations stop.  Add one more force — a drag that opposes the motion and grows with speed — and the same method still works, with one more term to carry.

A block sliding on a surface, with the spring pulling it back and drag opposing the velocity.  Drag depends on how fast it is going, not on where it is: Fdrag = −bv.
  1. Write the net force with both the spring and the drag in it, and set it equal to ma.
  2. Turn it into an equation of motion by writing both a and v as derivatives of x, and gather everything on one side.
  3. The solution is x(t) = Ae−(b/2m)t cos(ωt + φ0).  Which part of that is the oscillation and which part is the dying away?
  4. What does a large b/2m do?  A small one?  And what stays the same as the amplitude falls?
9)  Fnet = Fsp + Fdrag = −kx − bv = ma. 10)  d²x/dt² = −(k/m)x − (b/m)v = −(k/m)x − (b/m)(dx/dt), so d²x/dt² + (b/m)(dx/dt) + (k/m)x = 0 11)  The cosine is the oscillation, unchanged in shape.  The e−(b/2m)t is the dying away: it is an envelope that the oscillation is squeezed inside, and it never crosses zero, it just gets smaller. 12)  Large b/2m, decay fast.  Small b/2m, decay slow.  What stays the same is the period, T = 2π/ω — the swings get smaller but they keep coming at the same rate.  And the half-life t½, the time for the amplitude to halve, is constant: it takes as long to go from A to A/2 as from A/2 to A/4.  — which is what "exponential" means, and the reason the envelope is drawn as a curve rather than a straight line down to zero
The damped oscillation and its envelope.  The period is measured trough to trough, and it does not change as the amplitude falls.

Driven Oscillation and Resonance

So far the oscillator has been let go and left alone.  Now push it — over and over, at a frequency you choose.  Call that the driving frequency, ωd.  The object still has its own natural frequency ω0 = √(k/m), and it keeps it whatever you do.  The whole of resonance is what happens as you turn one against the other.

Drive it far from ω0 and you are fighting it: a push arrives while the mass is already coming back at you, and almost nothing builds up.  Drive it at ω0 and every push arrives exactly when the mass is already going that way.  The pushes add.  That is resonance.  The method does not change — one more force into the net force, one more term to carry.

  1. Add a driving force F0 cos ωdt to the net force of item 9, and write the new equation of motion.
  2. Once the transient has died away the mass settles into oscillating at ωd — the frequency you are driving it at, not its own.  Write that steady state down, and say what is left to find.
  3. Put it in and solve for the amplitude A(ωd).
  4. Now read the answer.  Where is A largest, what holds it down there, and what does b do to the height and the width of the peak?
13)  Fnet = −kx − bv + F0 cos ωdt = ma, so d²x/dt² + (b/m)(dx/dt) + (k/m)x = (F0/m) cos ωdt The left-hand side is exactly the damped equation from item 10.  All that is new is a source on the right. — which is why nothing about the method changes 14)  x(t) = A cos(ωdt − δ).  Note the frequency: it is ωd, not ω0.  Two unknowns are left — the amplitude A and the phase lag δ, by how much the mass runs behind the push. 15)  Substituting and collecting the cos and sin parts separately gives A(ωd) = (F0/m) ⁄ √[ (ω0² − ωd²)² + (bωd/m)² ] 16)  The first bracket is the one that can vanish, and it vanishes at ωd = ω0.  There the whole denominator is the damping term and nothing else, so Amax ≈ F0 ⁄ bω0 The damping alone is what stops it going to infinity.  Small b: a tall, narrow peak.  Large b: a low, broad one.  And as ωd → 0 the amplitude goes to F0/k for every curve — push it infinitely slowly and you just stretch the spring. — which is why all four curves below start together on the left
Steady-state amplitude against driving frequency, for four dampings.  The less the damping, the taller the peak and the narrower it is: a lightly damped object responds enormously, but only to a driving frequency very close to ω0.  A heavily damped one never responds much, but it responds across a wide band.
  1. A child on a swing is a pendulum with a natural frequency of about one swing every three seconds, and you want to get her as high as possible.  Describe how you should push — and then say what happens if you push twice as often instead.
  2. Two wine glasses, one thin crystal and one thick cheap glass.  A singer can shatter one of them with a sustained note and not the other.  Using the curves above, say which one, and why she has to hit the note almost exactly.
17)  Push once every three seconds, at the moment she is moving away from you: you are driving at ω0 and every push adds to what is already there.  Push twice as often and half of your pushes arrive while she is coming back toward you — those take energy out, and she goes nowhere.  Driving at the wrong frequency is not merely less good; it actively fights the motion. 18)  The thin crystal.  It is the lightly damped one, so its peak is very tall and a note at its natural frequency builds the vibration up far enough to break it.  But that peak is also very narrow, which is why she has to be almost exactly on pitch — a little off and the response collapses.  The thick glass is heavily damped: it turns the energy into heat as fast as she puts it in, and never builds up.
The punchline Amax ≈ F0 ⁄ bω0

An oscillator keeps its own frequency no matter what you do to it, and it answers loudest when you match it.  The only thing standing between resonance and an infinite amplitude is the damping — which is why the lightly damped systems are the dangerous ones, and why a bridge, a wine glass and a tuned circuit all have the same shape of answer.

Looking ahead

Four systems, one method: write the net force, turn it into an equation of motion, solve it.  You now have a frequency for a spring and for a pendulum, a description of what happens when friction is let in, and what happens when you drive it.  Note 6 turns all of this on an actual problem — a block on a spring with real numbers, where you have to work backwards from a graph to find ω and then to a force.  After that, Note 7 leaves the single oscillating object behind and lets the disturbance travel.

Where this sits. Every one of these four is Newton's second law with a different force law put into it. Nothing about oscillation came from anywhere else — it is all in the minus sign, which is what sends the system back toward where it started.
Back to your prediction

A heavier bob: no change.  The mass cancels in step 6, so it is not in ω = √(g/L) at all.  A longer string: slower.  L is in the denominator.  Up a mountain: slower.  g is a little smaller up there and g is on top, so ω falls, the swings take longer, and a pendulum clock carried up a mountain loses time.  The heavy-bob one is what catches people — the same instinct that says heavy things fall faster.
