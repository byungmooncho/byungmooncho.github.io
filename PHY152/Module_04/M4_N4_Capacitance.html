Capacitor And Capacitance

What a capacitor is, how capacitances combine, the energy it stores, and what a dielectric changes

Synopsis

A capacitor is a device used to store electrical charge, and thus electrical energy — to be used later, like a battery, but distinct from a battery. It is two parallel conducting plates separated by a distance. Connect it across a battery and charge flows onto the plates until the battery can no longer push or pull any more; how much charge it holds for a given voltage is its capacitance, and that turns out to depend on nothing but the area of the plates and how far apart they are.

1. What is a capacitor?

  • A device used to store electrical charge and thus electrical energy (to be used later like a battery, but distinct from a battery).
  • Parallel conducting plates separated by a distance.
  • At the − terminal, electrons are pushed onto the plate, making it − charge.
  • At the + terminal, electrons are pulled from the plate, making it + charge.
  • When connected to a battery, charge flows from the battery terminals to the plates until the battery can no longer push or pull charges — equilibrium.
Figure 1. Two parallel conducting plates a distance d apart, of plate area A, carrying equal and opposite charge, connected across a battery.

The electric field between the plates is constant, given by

E = σ/ε0

where σ is the surface charge, and the field points from + to −. The voltage across the plates follows from it:

V = Ed

Figure 2. The side view. One plate at +5 V, the other at −5 V, so 10 V across the pair. The dashed box is the Gaussian surface drawn around the inner face of the positive plate — it is what gives the field below.

Where E = σ/ε0 comes from

Put a Gaussian surface around the charged face of one plate. All the flux leaves through the one face that lies in the gap, so

∮S E·dA = Q/ε0 = σA/ε0

E ∮S dA = EA = Q/ε0   ∴   E = Q/Aε0 = σ/ε0

And so, capacitance

An ability to charge up the plate at a given voltage is capacitance.

C = Q/V ,  V = Ed = (Q/Aε0)d

Substitute the one into the other and the charge cancels:

C = Q ÷ [(Q/Aε0)d] = ε0A/d

The key resultC = ε0A/d

i.e. capacitance only depends on area and separation distance — not on how much charge you put on it, and not on the voltage you use to put it there.

2. Capacitance

C = Q/V ,  Q = CV ,  V = Q/C

In series

Figure 3. Three capacitors in series. There is nowhere for charge to go but onto the next plate, so every one of them carries the same charge, and the battery voltage divides between them.

Q1 = Q2 = Q3 = Q

and the voltages add:

Vtotal = V1 + V2 + V3 = Q1/C1 + Q2/C2 + Q3/C3

= Q(1/C1 + 1/C2 + 1/C3)   ⇒   Vtotal/Q = 1/Ctotal

In parallel

Figure 4. Three capacitors in parallel. Every one of them spans the same two points, so every one sees the same voltage, and the charge divides between them.

V1 = V2 = V3 = V

and the charges add:

Qtotal = Q1 + Q2 + Q3 = C1V1 + C2V2 + C3V3

= V(C1 + C2 + C3)   ⇒   Qtotal/V = Ctotal

The two key resultsseries:  1/Ctotal = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + 1/C3parallel:  Ctotal = C1 + C2 + C3

They are the opposite way round from resistors. Capacitors in parallel add; capacitors in series add as inverses.

A first combination

Take C1 = 1 µF and C2 = 5 µF in series, and put that pair in parallel with C3 = 8 µF. Reduce the series pair first, then add the parallel one.

Cs = [(5)−1 + (1)−1]−1 = 0.83 µF

Ctot = Cs + C3 = 0.83 + 8 = 8.83 µF

Notice the series pair came out smaller than either of the two capacitors in it, and the parallel step then simply added on.

The worked network

Now C1 = 12 µF, C2 = 2 µF, C3 = 4 µF across a 12.0 V battery, wired as below. Find the V’s and Q’s for all the C’s.

Figure 5. C1 in series with the parallel pair C2 and C3, across 12.0 V.

Reduce the parallel pair first, then the series step:

C2 + C3 = 2 + 4 = 6 µF

1/Ctot = 1/12 + 1/6  ⇒  Ctot = 4 µF

Q = CV = (4 × 10−6 F)(12 V) = 48 µC

That 48 µC is the charge on C1 as well, because C1 is in series with everything else.

V1 = Q/C1 = 48 µC / 12 µF = 4 V

The rest of the battery voltage falls across the parallel pair, and both of them span the same two points:

V2 = V3 = 12 − 4 = 8 V

Q2 = C2V2 = (2 µF)(8 V) = 16 µC

Q3 = C3V3 = (4 µF)(8 V) = 32 µC

And 16 + 32 = 48 µC, which is the charge that came through C1 — so the books balance.

3. Energy in a capacitor

Start from the energy density of an electric field — the energy per cubic metre, J/m3:

uE = ½ ε0E2

The total energy is that density times the volume the field fills, and between parallel plates that volume is just the plate area times the separation:

UE = uE × volume = uE(Ad)

Figure 7. The field fills the space between the plates: plate area A, thickness d, so the volume is Ad.

Three ways to write the same energy

Work the volume expression through, using E = V/d and C = ε0A/d:

UE = ½ ε0E2Ad = ½ ε0(V/d)2Ad = ½ V2[ε0A/d] = ½ V2C

Then substitute V = Q/C:

UE = ½ V2C = ½ (Q2/C2)C = ½ Q2/C

and once more, using 1/C = V/Q:

UE = ½ Q2/C = ½ Q2(V/Q) = ½ QV

The three forms½ CV2  =  ½ Q2/C  =  ½ QV

That last one looks like U = qV — and the factor of a half is the whole story: the first charge onto an empty capacitor costs nothing, the last costs the full V, and on average each costs half of it.

Back to the network

Same circuit as Figure 5 — C1 = 12 µF, C2 = 2 µF, C3 = 4 µF at 12.0 V. Find the energies in the C’s.

The voltages are already known from Figure 5, so use ½ CV2 on each:

U1 = ½(12 µF)(4 V)2 = 96 µJ

U2 = ½(2 µF)(8 V)2 = 64 µJ

U3 = ½(4 µF)(8 V)2 = 128 µJ

In all, 288 µJ — and the check is that the whole network, taken as one 4 µF capacitor at 12 V, stores ½(4 µF)(12 V)2 = 288 µJ as well.

The heart defibrillator

A heart defibrillator stores UE = 4 × 102 J and discharges at 10 000 V.

Of the three forms, the one with U and V in it is the one to rearrange:

C = 2U/V2 = 2(400 J) / (10 000 V)2 = 8 µF

Q = CV = (8 × 10−6 F)(10 000 V) = 0.08 C

Eight hundredths of a coulomb, delivered in a few milliseconds.

4. Capacitor with a dielectric

Inside a dielectric, the applied field causes “polarization” of the material — dipoles — which in turn reacts against the applied field, reducing it.

Figure 8. The same charged plates with nothing between them, and with a dielectric slab between them. The slab’s own induced surface charge sits opposite the plate charge it faces, and what is left of the field is weaker.

So the electric field inside is reduced, and therefore the voltage drops. For an isolated capacitor — one that has been charged and then disconnected, so its charge cannot change —

V = V0/κ

The charge Q0 is stuck where it is, and the voltage has fallen, so the capacitance must have risen:

C = Q0/V = Q0 ÷ (V0/κ) = κQ0/V0 = κC0

κ is the dielectric constant, and it is greater than 1 for any real material. κ = 1 for vacuum.

The worked example

A 20 pF capacitor at V = 40 V. Insert a dielectric with κ = 2.1.

  1. What is the new capacitance?
  2. What is Q0?
  3. What is the new voltage?
  4. What is the new energy?

C = κC0 = (2.1)(20 pF) = 42 pF

The charge was put on before the slab went in, at the original capacitance and the original voltage:

Q0 = C0V0 = (20 pF)(40 V) = 0.8 nC

V = V0/κ = 40 / 2.1 = 19 V

The charge has not moved. The field inside is weaker, so there is less potential difference across the same gap.

U = ½ Q0V = ½(0.8 nC)(19 V) = 7.6 nJ

against U0 = ½(0.8 nC)(40 V) = 16 nJ before the slab went in. Less than half the energy it started with — the difference is the work the capacitor did pulling the slab in between its plates.
