RC Circuits

Charging and discharging a capacitor through a resistor, and the time constant that sets how long it takes

Synopsis

An RC circuit is a circuit containing resistance and capacitance. Close it and the capacitor does not charge instantly: the resistor limits how fast charge can arrive, so the charge builds up over time and the current dies away. Everything in this note comes out of one application of the loop rule — but because the current is I = dq/dt, the loop rule becomes a differential equation, and solving it is the work.

1. What is an RC Circuit?

A circuit containing resistance and capacitance.

Figure 1. The capacitor charges through the resistor when the switch is at A, and discharges through the same resistor when it is at B. Two situations, one circuit.

With the switch at A the circuit is closed through the battery and the capacitor charges. With it at B the battery is out of the loop and the capacitor discharges through the resistor, driving the current the other way round.

Figure 2. The two loops on their own. In (a) the battery drives charge onto the capacitor; in (b) there is no battery and the charge already on the capacitor drives the current.

i) Charging a capacitor

Round the loop, with the battery in it:

ε − VR − VC = 0    (ΣVi = 0)

and the three things we need to substitute into it:

V = q/C ,  I = dq/dt ,  V = IR

ε − IR − q/C = 0

ε − R dq/dt − q/C = 0

Collect the two terms that do not carry the derivative:

−R dq/dt + (εC − q)/C = 0

dq/dt = (εC − q)/RC

This is a differential equation, not an algebraic one: the rate at which charge arrives depends on how much charge is already there. That is the whole difference between this note and the last one.

It separates: everything in q on one side, everything in t on the other.

∫0Q dq/(εC − q) = ∫0t dt/RC

The antiderivative of 1/(εC − q) carries a minus sign, because the thing inside the bracket decreases as q grows:

−ln(εC − q) ∣0Q = t/RC

−ln(εC − Q) + ln(εC) = t/RC

ln[(εC − Q)/εC] = −t/RC

and exponentiating both sides:

(εC − Q)/εC = e−t/RC

εC − Q = εC e−t/RC

Q(t) = εC (1 − e−t/RC)

At t = ∞ the exponential is gone and the capacitor holds εC — which is just Q = CV with the full battery voltage across it. So εC = Q0, the final charge, and

Q(t) = Q0(1 − e−t/RC)

The time constantRC = τQ(t) = Q0(1 − e−t/τ)

RC has units of time, and it is the only time in the problem. Every RC circuit charges on the same curve — all that changes is how fast the clock runs.

Figure 3. The charge climbing towards Q0, through 0.63Q0 at one time constant. It never quite arrives — an exponential approaches its asymptote and does not reach it.

So what happens after exactly one time constant?

Put t = τ:

Q(τ) = Q0(1 − e−1)

Q(τ)/Q0 = 1 − e−1 = 0.63

63% of the way there, after one time constant — and that is true of every RC circuit ever built, whatever R and C are.

The current, and the two voltages

The current is the rate at which charge arrives, so differentiate what we just found.

I = dq/dt = dQ/dt = (Q0/τ) e−t/RC

I = I0 e−t/RC

and since Q0/τ = εC/RC = ε/R, the starting current is I0 = ε/R — which is what you would get from Ohm’s law alone, because at t = 0 the capacitor is empty and has no voltage across it to push back with.

Figure 4. The current is largest the instant the switch closes and dies away as the capacitor fills. Charge and current run in opposite directions on the clock.

Capacitor:

V = q/C = ε(1 − e−t/RC)

Resistor:

V = IR = ε e−t/RC

Add them and the exponentials cancel: ε(1 − e−t/RC) + εe−t/RC = ε, at every instant. That is the loop rule still holding, moment by moment, all the way through the charging.

ii) Discharging — no battery, ε = 0

Throw the switch to B. The battery is out of the loop, so the same loop rule has nothing on the supply side:

−VR − VC = 0

Same substitutions as before, with ε = 0:

−R dq/dt − q/C = 0

which separates immediately:

dq/q = −dt/RC

Q = Q0 e−t/RC

The same time constant governs it. After one τ the capacitor has lost 63% of its charge and keeps 37% — the mirror of the charging result.

Figure 5. Discharging. The charge and the capacitor voltage both decay; the current is negative, because it runs the opposite way round the loop from the charging current, and climbs back to zero as the capacitor empties.

Looking ahead

One loop rule, one differential equation, one time constant — and the whole behaviour of the circuit falls out of it. Every circuit from here on is solved the same way: write the loop rule, substitute until one variable is left, and solve what you get.
