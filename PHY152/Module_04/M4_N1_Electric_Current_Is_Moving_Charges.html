Electric Current Is Moving Charges

Electric Circuit Preamble

Synopsis

You have learned that electrical charges generate electric fields that, in turn, influence (as attraction and repulsion force) other charges around them. Depending on the distance from the source charge, the affected charge feels the force (via an electric field, E) and, thus, has potential energy (by being in the potential, V). So far, we only talked about static charges, meaning stationary (not moving).

Charges want to move

Imagine charges sitting in a field (thus, potential). They want to move, if allowed! As we explore electric circuits (which make all electronic devices work, such as cell phones, computers, TVs, and so on), we need to look at moving charges! When charges are stationary, the energy is also stationary. For things to happen, we need a change in energy. The potential, measured in voltage (V), which is also known as the potential difference (note the word “difference”), tells us how much work the charges in the potential can do. This is, of course, related to the potential energy (qV, charge times potential). Just as an object higher up from the ground has larger potential energy than that on the ground (so it can do more damage when it falls), a large potential difference or the voltage indicates the charges that have high potential energy can do more work than that have a lower potential difference or lower voltage (so lower potential energy).

When charges are raised up higher in potential, meaning the charges are sitting in high potential (rocks are raised up high) and allowed to move (the rocks are released from the high position), the charges move toward the lower potential (the rocks fall). When the charges move from high voltage to lower voltage (the rocks fall from high to low), an electric current is created (falling rocks), which is measured in Amps (ampere, A = C/s), or the number of coulombs passing through in one second (if there was an unfortunate creature below the rocks, the number of rocks hitting the creature per second).

If the charges move down in potential (rocks fall) with minimum obstacles (freefalling rocks), charges can move at maximum speed, or the current is high (rocks falling at maximum speed). When the charges move at maximum speed, the maximum number of charges will pass through per second (maximum number of rocks per second hitting the creature). When the charges encounter restrictions (falling rocks hitting trees on the way down), the current decreases (the speed of rocks moving through decreases), and so the amount of charge passing through in one second is reduced (the number of rocks hitting the creature per second is reduced).

When obstacles restrict the charges’ movements (we will discuss the nature of the obstacles soon), they move slower. This is exactly like the rocks experiencing friction (like air resistance or trees), which you know to be something that always impedes motion. When they move slower, the current is smaller, reducing the number of charges passing through. The obstacles create resistance, which is measured in Ohms (Ω, a Greek letter omega, and named after Ohm, who discovered Ohm’s law, which we will delve into soon), and its effect is to slow down the current (the flow or movement of charges), in other words, reduce the current.

The picture of raising a rock (increasing potential or potential energy) and the falling rock (potential energy converting to other forms of energy, like movement energy, i.e., kinetic energy, or heat due to friction) is an excellent analogy for charge in a field or potential that wants to move; it moves from a higher potential and drop toward lower potential like a rock rolling down a hill.

A more popular picture: water in a pipe

A more popular picture is water being pushed through pipes using a pump. Here, the pump creates pressure that can push the water higher, let’s say, into a town water tower, which then flows down, moving through the pipes at lower heights. The pump is the battery, and the pressure (thus, height) in the tower is the voltage. So, the pressure is caused by the pump, moving water up and the water wanting to flow down; the potential difference is caused by the battery, moving charges up and the charges wanting to flow down. This is the voltage.

Instructions and Questions

1) Watch “What is electricity? — Electricity Explained (1)”

youtube.com/watch?v=ru032Mfsfig  (Type “What is electricity? Electricity Explained” in the YouTube search bar).

  1. What subatomic particles are responsible for electricity?

    Electrons.

  2. What forms an electric current?

    Flow of electrons.

  3. How do you form a positively or negatively charged atom?

    Loss or gain of electrons, typically from the outer shell. These are ions.

  4. What are conductors? Give an example.

    Metals — they have free electrons. Cu, Fe, so on.

  5. What are insulators? Give an example.

    Plastics, rubber, nonmetal — all bound electrons.

  6. What is an electrical circuit?

    Battery, wire, and load. The + terminal connected to the − terminal, with loads in between, via wires.

  7. What are the basic electrical elements and conditions for a continuous electrical circuit?

    Battery, wires, loads — connected in a continuous loop.

  8. What is a short circuit?

    The + terminal and − terminal connected directly.

  9. Why is a short circuit dangerous?

    Can cause fire, because too much current flows.

  10. How does a fuse prevent a short circuit?

    It heats up and melts, breaking the circuit.

    Free electrons.

3) Watch “What is voltage? — Electricity Explained (3)”

youtube.com/watch?v=wmaobOy8r6I  (Type “What is voltage? Electricity Explained” in the YouTube search bar).

  1. How is the negative terminal in a battery akin to water in a water tower? Use the terms surplus/shortage and high or low pressure in your description.

    Surplus of electrons ⇒ repulsion very high ⇒ like pressure.

  2. How is the positive terminal in a battery akin to a syringe’s plunger being pulled? Use the terms surplus/shortage and high or low pressure in your description.

    Lack of electrons ⇒ electrons are “pulled” or sucked in ⇒ low pressure.

  3. Using the notion that nature always seeks the charge balance, why is there charge flow or current in a circuit?

    When electrons are piled up, the potential is highly negative and energetically unstable. Electrons want to redistribute and spread out, lowering the potential.

  4. Explain why you think we don’t say just potential when we describe voltage but potential difference.

    Potential depends on the reference zero potential. The potential difference is absolute.

  5. How does high voltage (potential difference) have a bigger capacity to do work? Use the water pressure and current analogy to explain your answer.

    Voltage = potential difference = ΔV   Work = qΔV

    High pressure ⇒ high current, and so on.

  6. Why can’t you light a regular household bulb with a 1.5 V battery? Explain your answer using the analogy of water pressure, flow, and heavy water mill.

    The pressure is not high enough to push electrons through the bulb.

  8. What is a series connection? How does connecting three 1.5 V batteries in series affect the total voltage of the three batteries?
    Figure 2. His sketch: three cells end to end, the + end of one to the − end of the next.

    Yes — in series the voltages add.

    1.5 V + 1.5 V + 1.5 V = 4.5 V

  9. What is a parallel connection? How does connecting three 1.5 V batteries in parallel affect the total voltage of the three batteries?
    Figure 3. His sketch: three cells side by side between the same two rails.

    No — in parallel the voltage does not change. The three cells together still give 1.5 V. What parallel buys you is how long they last, not how hard they push.
