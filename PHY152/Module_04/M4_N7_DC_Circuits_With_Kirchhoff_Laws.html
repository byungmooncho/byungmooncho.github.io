DC Circuits With Kirchhoff Laws

Four worked circuits, and the matrix method that solves the ones too tangled to reduce

Synopsis

Series and parallel will get you a long way, but not everywhere: as soon as a circuit has two batteries in different branches, there is nothing left to reduce. What always works is the pair of rules from Note 3 — charge in equals charge out at every junction, and the voltages round every closed loop sum to zero. Write one equation per junction and one per independent loop, and you have as many equations as unknown currents. After that it is algebra — and when there are five of them, it is linear algebra.

Example 1 — one loop, two batteries

Find all the V’s and I’s.

Figure 1. Five resistors and two batteries in a single loop. The two sources face each other: going round, you meet 24 V one way and 12 V the other.

Two things to carry in:

ΣVi = 0 ,  V = IR

Round the loop:

−VR3 − VR2 − VR1 − 12 − VR5 − VR4 + 24 = 0

and with V = IR on every resistor:

−I3R3 − I2R2 − I1R1 − 12 − I5R5 − I4R4 + 24 = 0

There is only one loop, so there is only one current:

I = I1 = I2 = I3 = I4 = I5

Collect the resistances:

12 + I(−R3 − R2 − R1 − R5 − R4) = 0

12 = I(R1 + R2 + R3 + R4 + R5) = I(60 × 103)

∴ I = 2 × 10−4 A

The 12 on the left is what is left of the two batteries after they have fought each other: 24 − 12.

Same current through all five, so it is V = IR five times:

VR1 = IR1 = (2 × 10−4)(10 kΩ) = 2 V

VR2 = 4 V ,  VR3 = VR4 = VR5 = 2 V

And they add to 12 V, which is exactly the net voltage the two batteries supply. That is the loop rule holding, and it is the check worth doing every time.

Power

P = IV  ⇒  [W] = [J/s] = [C/s][J/C]

The units are the argument: a coulomb per second times joules per coulomb is joules per second. Power out from this circuit:

ΣPi = (ΣVi)I

= (2 × 10−4 A)(2 + 4 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 12 V) = 4.8 × 10−3 W

The five resistor drops plus the 12 V battery, which is being charged by the bigger one and so takes power out of the circuit just as a resistor does. 4.8 mW in all, every bit of it supplied by the 24 V source.

Example 2 — two loops and a junction

Figure 2. Two loops sharing the middle branch. I1 and V2 are given; V1, I2 and I3 are not.

Three unknowns, so three equations — one for each loop, one for the junction:

Loop 1:  +21 − 5I3 + V1 − (12 · 2) = 0

Loop 2:  +21 − 5I3 − 6I2 = 0

Junction A:  2 + I2 = I3

The junction equation has no unknown but I2 and I3 in it, so substitute it straight into loop 2:

21 − 5(2 + I2) − 6I2 = 0

21 − 10 − 5I2 − 6I2 = 0  ⇒  11 = 11I2

I2 = 1 A ,  I3 = 2 + 1 = 3 A

Then loop 1 gives up the battery, since 21 − 24 = −3:

−5I3 + V1 − 3 = 0  ⇒  V1 = 18 V

When substitution runs out: the resistor matrix

Two unknowns you can substitute. Five you cannot — not without losing your place. But every one of those equations is linear in the currents, and a set of linear equations is a matrix.

Figure 3. Five unknown currents, two junctions and three independent loops — five equations for five unknowns.

Write the five equations as rows, one column per current and one for the constant. Kirchhoff’s rules give exactly as many rows as you need:

I1I2I3I4I5V
loop 34300012
loop 40320012 − 5
loop 50322012
junction A1−11000
junction B00−11−10

That table is the “resistor matrix”. Call it A, call the column of unknown currents x and the column of constants b, and the whole circuit is one line:

The methodAx = b  ⇒  A−1Ax = A−1b  ⇒  x = A−1b

Because A−1A is the identity. Build the matrix, invert it, multiply. The circuit stops being a puzzle and becomes a procedure.

Rows 2 and 3 differ only by the 2I4 term, so subtracting gives I4 straight away:

2I4 = 12 − 7  ⇒  I4 = 2.5 A

and the rest follow:

I1 = 1.5 A ,  I2 = 2 A ,  I3 = 0.5 A ,  I4 = 2.5 A ,  I5 = 2 A

Check them against both junctions before you believe them: 1.5 − 2 + 0.5 = 0 at A, and −0.5 + 2.5 − 2 = 0 at B.

Put them back into the rows as a check: 8(1.5) + 6(2) = 24 ✓, 6(2) + 4(0.5) = 14 ✓, and 1.5 − 2 + 0.5 = 0 ✓ at the junction.

Choosing the loops

The matrix is the easy part. The step that goes wrong is choosing the loops — which closed paths to take, and how to be sure you have enough of them and no repeats. It is much easier to see traced on the circuit than written out.

Figure 5. Three independent loops traced straight onto the circuit, each in its own colour. Every one starts and ends at the battery and takes a different route back.

Two junctions give two equations:

A:  I1 = I2 + I3    B:  I2 = I4 + I5

and each traced loop gives one more:

3:  I1(100) + I2(220) + I4(20) = 3 V

4:  I1(100) + I3(470) = 3 V

5:  I1(100) + I2(220) + I5(10) = 3 V

Five equations, five unknowns. Every loop here passes through the battery and through R1, then takes a different way home — that is what makes them independent, and it is the pattern to look for.

Looking ahead

That closes the circuits. Everything in this module came from two rules about conservation and one about resistance, and the only thing that ever changed was how many equations you had to write down at once. Next the charges stop just flowing and start pushing on each other sideways — magnetism.
