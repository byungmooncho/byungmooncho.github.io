Harmonics and Wave Synthesis

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 105.

The ideaTwo waves in the same medium at the same time simply add, and the result depends on which frequencies are present, how loud each one is, and how they line up in phase. This page fixes the vocabulary — pure tone, partial, synthesis, analysis — separates a time graph from a wave graph, and then builds sawtooth, square and triangle waves from nothing but sinusoids.

The words, first

Everything in this module is about adding waves together, and the four terms below are the ones Trevor uses from here on. They are worth getting straight before the pictures start, because three of them sound alike.

Pure toneA simple sinusoidal wave with one clearly defined frequency.
PartialOne of the components of a more complex waveform.
SynthesisCombining simple tones into more complex tones.
AnalysisBreaking a complex wave back into its partials.
Why it mattersSynthesis and analysis are the same relationship read in opposite directions. A synthesiser does the first; a spectrum analyser does the second.

Same frequency, different phase

Start with the simplest case of all: two pure tones at the same frequency. Nothing about the pitch can change — there is only one frequency in the room. What changes is how big the result is, and that is settled entirely by how the two waves line up.

Carry this forwardWaves nearly in phase tend to add and get bigger. Waves near 180° apart tend to cancel. That one sentence is behind interference, beats and every standing wave in this course.

Two pictures of the same sound

A wave can be plotted against time or against distance, and the two plots look identical until you read the horizontal axis. They are not the same graph and they do not measure the same thing.

This is also where the string and the air part company. A vibrating string sets the air around it moving at the same frequency, and the air then carries the wave away at the speed of sound, roughly 340 m/s. Frequency is shared across the boundary; speed and wavelength are not.

Frequency crosses a boundary unchanged. Speed and wavelength do not.
  •  The source sets f.
  •  The medium sets v.
  •  λ = v / f follows from the other two.

Building the “basic” waveforms

Sawtooth, square and triangle waves are not primitives. Each one is a particular recipe of sinusoids, and the recipe is short: pick which harmonics, how loud, and at what phase. The tables under the three figures are Trevor's own.

Read the three figures togetherAll three use the same first harmonic. What makes them different instruments of a kind is which harmonics are present, how loud each one is, and what phase it is at. More harmonics gets you closer to the ideal shape — compare the green curve against the dashed one in each figure.
