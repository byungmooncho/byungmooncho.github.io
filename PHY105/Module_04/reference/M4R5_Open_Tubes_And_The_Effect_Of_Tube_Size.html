Open Tubes and the Effect of Tube Size

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 105.

The ideaOpening the second end of a tube changes the boundary condition and with it everything that fits: an antinode is now required at both ends, so the fundamental is half a wavelength and the full harmonic series is available. This page sets the open tube against the closed one, then takes up the two ways a tube's width matters — pipe scale, which decides which harmonics an instrument favours, and the end correction, which decides whether your speed-of-sound measurement is right.

Both ends open

Open the second end and the boundary condition changes at a stroke: now the air is free to move at both ends, so the tube needs an antinode at each. The shortest thing that does that is half a wavelength, and every whole number of half-waves works after it.

L = n λ/2   with   n = 1, 2, 3, …
The comparison is the pointTwo tubes of the same length, one closed at one end and one open at both: the open tube's fundamental is an octave higher, and it has all the harmonics rather than only the odd ones. One boundary condition changed, and both the pitch and the timbre moved.

The deck follows this with a live demonstration — a tube swung overhead until it sings. Which harmonic it lands on depends on how fast it is swung, and the jump from one to the next is audible as a step rather than a slide.

Pipe scale: a tube is not just a length

Everything above treats the tube as a one-dimensional line. Real pipes have a width, and the ratio of width to length changes which harmonics a pipe favours — which is a large part of why a clarinet and a trumpet do not sound alike even when they are the same length.

Drum—1–2

Read down the right-hand columnThe narrower the pipe, the higher the harmonics it favours. The clarinet's 1, 3, (5) is the odd series you met in M4R4 — it is a tube effectively closed at the reed end, and the pipe scale tells you it will stay on the low odd harmonics. The trumpet, ten times narrower in scale, lives on harmonics 3 to 6.

Edge effects, and the length you cannot measure

The wave does not stop dead at the mouth of the tube. A little of the air just outside gets dragged along, so the tube behaves as though it were slightly longer than the ruler says. The correction is small, but it is the difference between a speed-of-sound measurement that works and one that is systematically low.

Two values, and they disagree slightlyTrevor quotes L′ = L + 0.60 r when introducing edge effects, and uses e = 0.58 r in the worked example below. Both are in the usual range — the textbook figure for a flanged pipe is about 0.61 r — and the choice is not worth agonising over. What matters is applying one of them, once per open end.
Worked example

A pipe open at both ends is 64.0 cm long and 8.0 cm in diameter. Its first harmonic is measured at 238 Hz. What is the speed of sound?

r = ½d = 0.04 m, so e = 0.58 × 0.04 m = 0.0232 m

Open at both ends, so L + 2e = ½λ:

λ = 2(L + 2e) = 2(0.64 m + 2 × 0.0232 m) = 1.3728 m

v = f λ = 238 Hz × 1.3728 m = 326.7 m/s

Without the correction you would have used λ = 2L = 1.28 m and got 304.6 m/s — about 7% low. On a fat tube like this one the correction is not optional.
