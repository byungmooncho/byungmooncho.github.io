Resonance in a Tube Closed at One End

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 105.

The ideaA tube closed at one end has to hold a displacement node at the closed end and an antinode at the open one, and that single requirement decides everything: only odd quarter-wavelengths fit, so the tube sounds the odd harmonics and nothing else. This page works through the boundary conditions, the difference between the pressure and displacement pictures, and the two standard calculations — finding the speed of sound from one resonance, and predicting where the next resonance will be.

Displacement or pressure?

Air in a tube can be described two ways, and they are a quarter wave out of step with each other. Pick one and stay with it, because the word node means opposite things in the two descriptions.

The boundary conditions, in one line each.
  •  Closed end: displacement node, pressure antinode.
  •  Open end: displacement antinode, pressure node.
For the loudest resonance you want a displacement antinode at the open end.

Which wavelengths fit

A node at one end and an antinode at the other is a quarter of a wavelength. Everything else follows from adding whole half-waves to that quarter — which is why a tube closed at one end supports the odd harmonics and nothing else.

L = n λ/4   with   n = 1, 3, 5, …

Fixing the frequency and changing the tube instead

The laboratory version turns the problem around. Hold a tuning fork or a speaker over a tube of adjustable length, keep the frequency fixed, and slide the length until the tube rings. It will ring at more than one length, and the lengths are in a ratio you can predict.

The shortcut worth memorisingWith the frequency fixed, the resonant lengths are in the ratio 1 : 3 : 5. Find the first one and you have all of them.

Worked examples

Worked example

A tube closed at one end has its first resonance at 440 Hz, and the tube is 18.4 cm long. What is the speed of sound today?

First resonance means L = λ/4, so λ = 4L.

λ = 4 × 0.184 m = 0.736 m

v = f λ = 440 Hz × 0.736 m = 324 m/s

There are size effects that should be corrected for — see M4R5. Uncorrected, this will read a little low.

Worked example

A 10 cm tube resonates at 500 Hz. What should the tube length be for the next two resonances?

The frequency is fixed, so the resonant lengths go as 1 : 3 : 5.

Next resonance: 3 × 10 cm = 30 cm

The one after: 5 × 10 cm = 50 cm

Note what is not happening: the pitch is the same at all three lengths. You are not playing three notes, you are finding three places where one note fits.

Check yourself

Trevor runs a live poll at this point in the lesson, showing a tube with a wave drawn in it and asking a single question: how much of a wavelength is here? Three things make that question answerable every time.

Answering “how much of a wavelength is here?”
  1.  Find the ends: which is closed, which is open.
  2.  Count the loops. Each full loop is ½ λ, each half-loop at an end is ¼ λ.
  3.  Add them up. In a tube closed at one end the answer is always an odd number of quarters.
