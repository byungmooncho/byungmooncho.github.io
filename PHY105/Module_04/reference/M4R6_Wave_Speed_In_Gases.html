Wave Speed in Gases

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 105.

The ideaThe speed of sound is a property of the gas, not a constant: it goes as the square root of compressibility over density, so a light gas carries sound fast and a heavy one carries it slowly. This page works that out for air, helium and xenon, and then uses it to settle what actually happens to a voice after a breath of helium — which is not what most people say it is.

What sets the speed of sound in a gas

Every resonance result so far has taken the speed of sound as a given number. It is not a constant of nature — it is a property of the gas, and it comes out of a competition between two things: how stiff the gas is, and how heavy it is.

v = √( K / ρ )
Kthe compressibility — how strongly the gas pushes back when you squeeze it. Set by how many molecules are present and how they are bound together.
ρthe density, in kg/m3. The lower-case Greek letter rho.
Read the formula before you use itK is upstairs and ρ is downstairs, so stiffer means faster and heavier means slower. Under ordinary conditions the densities of different gases vary far more than their compressibilities do, so in practice it is the density that does the work.

Helium and xenon have the same compressibility — both are monatomic — so the whole of the difference between 968.6 m/s and 169.9 m/s is density. That is as clean a demonstration of the formula as you will get.

Why helium makes your voice sound funny

This is the part that is almost always explained wrongly, including in most places you will read about it. Your vocal cords do not speed up.

The chain of reasoning, in order.
  1.  The vocal cords vibrate at their normal frequency. Nothing changes here.
  2.  Sound travels faster in helium, so each partial has a longer wavelength.
  3.  The low partials no longer fit the cavity in your throat and mouth, so they are not reinforced.
  4.  Only the higher partials resonate, so the timbre shifts upward and the voice sounds thin and high.
The usual wrong answer“Helium raises the pitch of your voice.” It does not. The fundamental is still there — your cords are still doing exactly what they were doing. What has changed is which formants the cavity reinforces, and that is a change in timbre, not pitch. It is M4R3's formants and M4R5's resonating tube, meeting.
Safety, brieflyBreathing helium displaces the oxygen you need, and doing it from a pressurised cylinder can injure the lungs directly. This is a demonstration to understand, not to repeat.
