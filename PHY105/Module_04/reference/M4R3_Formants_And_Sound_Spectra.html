Formants and Sound Spectra

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 105.

The ideaA spectrum is a plot of amplitude against frequency: one stick for each partial in a sound. This page is about what a spectrum tells you that a waveform does not — which frequencies are present and how much of each — what a Fourier transform does to get you from one to the other, and what a formant is: a band where the energy piles up, set by the resonator rather than by the note.

Why two instruments playing the same note sound different

Play A4 on a violin and on a trumpet and the fundamental is 440 Hz in both. What differs is everything above it: which partials are present and how strong each one is. That recipe is the sound spectrum, and it is what your ear is actually using to tell a violin from a trumpet.

Formant.  A frequency band where sound energy is largely concentrated. Formants are a property of the resonator — the body of the violin, the bore of the trumpet, your own vocal tract — so they tend to stay put when the played note changes.
Two things to notice on any real spectrumThe partials are usually equally spaced, because they are usually harmonics. And the sticks are never perfectly sharp — detuning, vibrato and the decay of the note all smear them out a little.

From a waveform to a spectrum

You can do this by hand for a pure tone. Measure one period off the trace, invert it, and you have the frequency — Trevor calls this the slow Fourier transform, and for a tuning fork it is enough, because there is only one frequency to find. A real instrument has a dozen, and the measurement by hand becomes hopeless.

Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).  A program that looks for repeating patterns in a waveform and reports the frequencies at which those patterns occur. Its output is the sound spectrum: the relative amplitude of each partial.
A period is not a frequencyThe period you measure off the trace is in seconds. Invert it to get hertz. Students lose more marks to skipping that step than to any other part of this topic.

One instrument, one spectrum

The deck walks through recorded spectra of a pipa, a guitar, a violin and a cello playing C3, each with its own distribution of partials. The pattern worth carrying away is not any one of them but the comparison: same note, same fundamental, different recipe above it.

Modern synthesiser software turns that observation into a control panel. You add oscillators to choose which harmonics are present, shape how the note starts and stops with attack, decay, sustain and release, then add effects such as detune and distortion. It is the vocabulary of M4R1 with knobs on it.

The spectra themselves are screen captures of recorded instruments and are not reproduced here, along with photographs of the instruments and of a synthesiser interface. Anything you can record, you can analyse the same way — a free spectrum analyser on a phone will show you your own voice's formants moving as you change vowel.
