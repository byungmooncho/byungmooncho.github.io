Harmonics, Overtones and Harmonic Frequencies

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 105.

The ideaA string fixed at both ends can only vibrate at the integer multiples of its fundamental, and this page is how to read one: count the antinodes to get the harmonic number, remember that each loop is half a wavelength, and keep the two namings — harmonic and overtone — from being confused. It also separates octaves from harmonics, which are not the same thing, and shows what two harmonics sounding together look like.

Reading a standing wave

Given a picture of a vibrating string, the first job is always the same: work out which harmonic it is. Two counting rules do all of it.

The two counting rules.
  •  The number of antinodes is the harmonic number.
  •  Each loop is ½ λ.

Two names for the same thing

The same vibration has two names in common use, and they are offset by one. Harmonics are counted from zero; overtones are counted from the fundamental. Nothing physical distinguishes them — it is bookkeeping, and it is worth ten minutes now rather than a wrong answer later.

That is the whole of it for a string: the harmonics are the integer multiples of the fundamental. Nothing else is allowed, because nothing else fits between two fixed ends.

Octaves are not harmonics

An octave is a doubling of frequency. Harmonics go up in equal steps of f1, so only some of them land on an octave — and the gaps between successive harmonics get musically smaller as you climb.

Two things to take from the tableOctaves are the powers of two: harmonics 1, 2, 4, 8. Everything in between is a harmonic that is not an octave. The 3rd harmonic lands on E — a perfect fifth above — so A3 to E4 is a 2:3 frequency ratio. The 5th and 7th do not land on a piano key at all; Trevor leaves them marked with a question mark on the slide, and they are the reason the Pythagorean scale needs its own lesson.

Partials

A partial is any component of a complex wave. The fundamental is the lowest partial, and usually — not always — the strongest; sometimes the second harmonic carries more energy than the first. The ones above the fundamental are often called upper partials.

All harmonics are partials. Not all partials are harmonics.
A partial that is not an integer multiple of the fundamental is called inharmonic. Strings and air columns give you harmonic partials; bars, plates and bells do not.

More than one harmonic at once

How a string is plucked decides which harmonics are excited, and several can be present together. The resulting shape is just the sum, moment by moment.

How to read one of theseCheck first that the first harmonic is present — the single large arch end to end. Then look at the middle traces: the number of loops there tells you which other harmonic is in the mix.
