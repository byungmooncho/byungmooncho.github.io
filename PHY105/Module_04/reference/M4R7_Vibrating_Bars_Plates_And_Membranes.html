Vibrating Bars, Plates and Membranes

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 105.

The ideaStrings and air columns give overtones that are whole-number multiples of the fundamental. Bars, bells, plates and drumheads do not — a free bar's first two overtones come at 2.76 and 5.40 times the fundamental, and a drumhead's at 1.59 and 2.13. This page is the inharmonic case: what sets a bar's pitch, why a chime rings from the metal rather than the air inside it, and what a Chladni figure is actually showing you.

Everything so far has been a string or a tube

Strings and air columns are the easy cases, and they share a happy property: their overtones are whole-number multiples of the fundamental. Start hitting bars, plates and drumheads and that stops being true. The overtones are still predictable — they are just not simple.

Three ways a bar can vibrate

Try it with a metre stickHold it loosely at a point about a fifth of the way in from one end and tap it: that is where the transverse fundamental has its node, so the note rings. Grip it in the middle instead and the same tap is dead.

What sets the pitch of a bar

f = k t / ℓ2
ffrequency
tthickness
ℓlength
kelasticity constant — a property of the material

Two things in that expression are worth stopping on. The length enters squared, so halving a bar's length does not double its pitch — it quadruples it. And the thickness is in the numerator, so a thicker bar of the same length sounds higher, which is the opposite of the intuition most people bring from strings.

This is what “inharmonic” means.  The partials of a bar are at 1, 2.76 and 5.40 times the fundamental — real frequencies, perfectly predictable, but not integer multiples. Your ear will not fuse them into one clear pitch the way it does for a string, which is why a struck bar sounds more like a clang than a note.

Tuning forks

In the laboratoryStrike a tuning fork on something soft, never on a bench edge. The clang mode dies away quickly but it is loud while it lasts, and it is enough to spoil a frequency measurement taken in the first second.

Tubes, chimes and Boom Whackers

A hanging tube is a bar rolled into a circle, and the mathematics gets worse — both the inside and the outside diameter affect the tone. Two practical points survive without the mathematics.

In a wind chime, the sound comes from the vibration of the tube itself — not the air inside it.  That is the opposite of everything in M4R4 and M4R5, and it is easy to get backwards. The air column is along for the ride.

So a chime is supported at a node and struck near its middle, and with the support in the right place the sound rings for a very long time. And if several tubes are the same thickness and the same material, the only thing left in f = k t/ℓ2 that can vary is the length — which is exactly how a set of Boom Whackers is tuned, and why the tubes in a set are cut to such precise lengths.

Worth doingA set of Boom Whackers will play ‘Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star’ with one person per tube. It is also the cleanest demonstration of an inverse-square length law you can hold in your hand.

Plates: Chladni figures

Scatter sand on a flat plate, bow or drive its edge, and the sand migrates away from the parts that are moving and settles along the lines that are not. What is left is a direct photograph of a two-dimensional standing wave — the nodal lines made visible.

What you are looking atSand collects where the plate is still. So the dashed lines are nodes, and the open areas between them are where the plate is moving hardest — the two-dimensional equivalent of the loops on a string.

Membranes: the drumhead

A circular membrane under tension — a drumhead — has nodal lines of two kinds: diameters across it, and circles within it. Combining them gives a family of modes whose frequencies are the real reason a drum does not play a note.

Why a drum has no clear pitch, and a timpano does.  With overtones at 1.59f and 2.13f the ear has nothing to lock on to. A timpani maker shifts those ratios — by loading the head, by the shape of the kettle and by where the drum is struck — until several of them fall close to a harmonic series, and the drum acquires a pitch.
