Lab 5 — Harmonic Synthesis

Synopsis In Note 1 you took a complex periodic wave apart and found a fundamental with a series of harmonics stacked on top of it. That was analysis: you were handed the wave and asked what was in it. This lab runs the same idea backwards. You start with nothing but pure sine tones, you choose their frequencies, amplitudes and phases yourself, and you watch a shape appear that none of them has on its own. By the end you will have built a sawtooth, a square and a triangle out of sine waves, heard each of them, and found out what happens when the frequencies you add are not whole-number multiples of one another.

Performance Expectations: Use a spreadsheet model to add up to four sine components of chosen harmonic number, amplitude and phase. Predict, then verify, the effect of a 180° phase shift on a sum. Construct sawtooth, square and triangle waveforms from their harmonic recipes, and state the rule that distinguishes the square wave's series from the sawtooth's. Relate the frequency of a sum to the frequencies of its components. Distinguish harmonic from inharmonic addition by the behaviour of the resulting waveform. Support every claim with a graph you generated and can point to.

Equipment: A laptop with Excel · the Wave Synthesis workbook (download below; a Google Sheets copy is linked if you prefer) · headphones or earbuds · the additive-synthesis applet and the online tone generator, both linked in the steps.

Questions for Discussion

Choose three of the following and answer those three fully, working them into your discussion section. Say which three you chose. Do not simply copy the question and answer it in a line — and do not limit yourself to these topics if you have other insights.

  1. In Part 1 you added two waves of the same frequency and the sum was still at that frequency. In Part 2 you added four waves of four different frequencies and the sum was still at the lowest one. Explain why adding higher harmonics cannot change the frequency of the sum, using your own graphs.

  2. Your square wave uses only some of the harmonics. Name which ones and explain the connection to the closed tube in Note 3 — two very different systems producing the same series is not a coincidence.

  3. A 180° phase shift changed your Part 1 sum dramatically, but in Part 2 the phases mattered much less to how the waveform sounded than the amplitudes did. Say what that tells you about what the ear is sensitive to.

  4. You only had four components to work with. Describe where your waveforms departed most from the ideal sawtooth, square or triangle, and say what adding a fifth, tenth and twentieth harmonic would do to that departure.

  5. From Part 3: what is physically different about a sum of inharmonic components, and why does a sound built that way tend to be heard as noise or as a clang rather than as a note with a pitch?

  6. A clarinet and a violin playing the same written note are instantly distinguishable. Using nothing but the controls in this workbook, say what would have to differ between them, and what would have to be the same.
