Standing Waves In Air Columns

SynopsisIn Module 2 you set up standing waves and met resonance: a wave reflects, the returning wave interferes with the outgoing one, and at the right frequency the two lock into a pattern that does not travel. Here the question is narrower and more useful. Put the air in a tube and the ends of that tube decide which standing waves are allowed at all — and, as you will see, a tube closed at one end allows only half of them. Then we use that fact to measure the speed of sound.
One thing worth restatingThe phase of a standing wave does not shift in space. A node is at zero at all times; an antinode swings from maximum positive to maximum negative. Neither of them moves along the tube. Run the simulation and watch the red forward wave, the blue reflected wave and the black resultant.
Recall from Module 2, Note 3At a closed end the air cannot move, so that end is a displacement node — zero amplitude. At an open end the air is free to move, so that end is a displacement antinode — maximum amplitude. Those two sentences are the whole of what follows.

1 · A tube closed at one end

Node at the closed end, antinode at the open end. The shortest wave that does that fits a quarter of a wavelength into the tube, so L = λ/4 and λ = 4L. Ask for the next one up and you do not get twice the frequency — you get three times it, because the next pattern that still has a node at one end and an antinode at the other fits three quarter-wavelengths.

λn = 4L / n    with n = 1, 3, 5, …    (odd only)

Why is there no f2?

The second pattern would put half a wavelength in the tube — a node at both ends, or an antinode at both ends. Neither matches a closed end and an open end, so the tube will not resonate there.

A tube closed at one end sounds its fundamental at 100 Hz. What are the next two frequencies it will sound?

300 Hz and 500 Hz — 3f1 and 5f1. Not 200 Hz and not 400 Hz.

There is a second way to hunt for a resonance, and it is the one the experiment below uses. Instead of changing the frequency, hold the frequency fixed and change the length. The same tuning fork will resonate in a short column, and again in a column three times as long, and again at five times.

2 · A tube open at both ends

Now take the stopper out. Both ends are open, so both ends are antinodes. The shortest wave that does that fits half a wavelength in the tube, and — unlike the closed tube — every harmonic after it is allowed.

λn = 2L / n    with n = 1, 2, 3, …    (all of them)

3 · The whole module in one picture

Three boundary conditions, three answers. Notice that the string and the open tube land on the same formula from opposite conditions — nodes at both ends, or antinodes at both ends — while the half-open tube is the odd one out.

Two pipes sound the same fundamental, one closed at one end and one open at both. Which one is shorter, and why?

The closed one, and by half. It only needs λ/4 to fit, where the open pipe needs λ/2. That is why the stopped pipes on an organ are half the length of the open ones sounding the same note.
Demonstration — the swinging tube

A corrugated tube swung in a circle drives air through itself, and it sounds whenever the airflow lands on one of the tube’s resonances. It is open at both ends, so what you are hearing is the series above. Swing it faster and it does not slide up smoothly — it jumps, one harmonic at a time.

Listen for which note it starts on. You will probably find you cannot get the fundamental at all, however gently you swing: there is a minimum airflow before the tube will speak. A mode can be allowed by the geometry and still not sound.

4 · The correction nobody mentions until the numbers come out wrong

An open end is not exactly an antinode. The air just outside the tube moves with the air inside it, so the wave behaves as though the tube were slightly longer than it is. That extra bit is the end correction, e, and it is roughly 0.6 times the tube’s radius for each open end.

Leff = L + e     e ≈ 0.6r

Use the bare measured length and you will under-estimate the wavelength, and therefore under-estimate the speed of sound. That is not a mistake in your measurement — it is a known, predictable, one-directional error, and in a moment you will measure it.

Worked example

A tube closed at one end has its first resonance at 440 Hz. The tube is 18.4 cm long. What is the speed of sound today?

First resonance, so L = λ/4 and λ = 4 × 0.184 m = 0.736 m.

v = fλ = 440 Hz × 0.736 m = 324 m/s.

That is about 5% below the 343 m/s in the Reference Table, and it is low rather than scattered — the signature of the end correction. Putting e = 0.6r back in for a tube of radius about 1.8 cm brings it to roughly 343 m/s.

A 10 cm tube resonates at 500 Hz. What should the tube length be for the next two resonances?

The tube resonates at λ/4, 3λ/4 and 5λ/4, so the next resonance is at 3 times the original length and the one after at 5 times: 30 cm and 50 cm.
The trick that removes it altogetherWrite the first two resonances out and subtract them: L1 + e = λ/4 and L2 + e = 3λ/4, so L2 − L1 = λ/2. The end correction cancels. The gap between successive resonances gives you a clean wavelength without knowing e at all — and then e = (L2 − 3L1)/2 tells you what it was.

6 · The experiment

You will set up standing waves with tuning forks of different frequencies in a column of air whose length you can change, with the aim of determining the speed of sound. The length of the air column is adjustable by filling the column with water and connecting it to a reservoir: raise the reservoir and the water rises, so the air column gets shorter.

  1. Draw the experimental setup above and label the components.
  2. Strike the tuning fork with a rubber hammer and hold it over the opening of the air column. Raise and lower the reservoir until you hear the sound amplify — that is resonance.
  3. Keep going. Lower the water further and find the next resonance for the same fork, and the one after it if the tube is long enough. Record every resonance length.
  4. Repeat for each tuning fork.
  5. Fill in the table, plot, and work out the speed of sound two ways.
