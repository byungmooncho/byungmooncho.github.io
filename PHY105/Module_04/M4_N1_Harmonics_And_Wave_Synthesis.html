Harmonics And Wave Synthesis

SynopsisIn the previous module, we focused on hearing the sound. We handled the sound intensity, sound intensity levels, and sound loudness. These quantities are the amplitude of the waves. In this module, we explore the production or generation of the sound, and here, the focus is the frequency/pitch. The terms we come across in this module mostly refer to the frequency aspect of waves: Harmonics, pure tones, partials, synthesis, and spectrum analysis are all related to the frequency of waves. This note will first explore essential terms for this module and beyond.
Performance ExpectationsBe able to describe the terms, fundamentals, harmonics, partial tones, pure tones, wave synthesis, formant, wave phases, and spectrum.

Questions and Instructions

Work from the slidesOpen Harmonics and Synthesis in Brightspace and answer the following questions.

What is the fundamental frequency?

The lowest frequency that a resonator can support.

What are the harmonic frequencies?

Integer multiples of the fundamental frequency. If f1 = 100 Hz, the harmonics are 2 × 100 Hz = 200 Hz, 3 × 100 Hz = 300 Hz, 4 × 100 Hz = 400 Hz, and so on.

How are harmonic frequencies related to the fundamental frequency?

The nth harmonic is n f1: the 2nd harmonic is 2f1, the 3rd is 3f1. In musical terms, if C4 = 261 Hz, then 2 × 261 Hz is C5 and 3 × 261 Hz is G5.

What are the overtones?

The harmonics above the fundamental, counted from the fundamental rather than from zero. The 1st overtone is the 2nd harmonic; the 2nd overtone is the 3rd harmonic.

How are the overtones related to the fundamentals and harmonics?

f1 = fundamental.   2f1 = 2nd harmonic = 1st overtone.   3f1 = 3rd harmonic = 2nd overtone. The two countings are offset by one.

What is a pure tone?

A single frequency — a simple sinusoidal wave with one clearly defined frequency.

What are partials?

The components of a more complex waveform — the individual frequencies that are present in it.

What does the process of wave synthesis entail?

Combining simple tones into more complex tones. The reverse — breaking a complex wave back into its partials — is called analysis.

What is a spectrum?

A plot of amplitude against frequency: one “stick” at each frequency present, whose height is how much of that frequency is in the sound.

When you look at a spectrum, what do you learn from it?

Which frequencies are present, and how much of each one. That is what makes two instruments playing the same note sound different.

What is a waveform?

The time trace — the resultant wave you get when a set of frequencies mix, plotted as a function of time. It is what an oscilloscope shows: amplitude against time, for a “complex wave.”

How does a waveform differ from a spectrum?

Same sound, two plots. The waveform is amplitude against time (seconds); the spectrum is amplitude against frequency (Hz).

How do you convert a waveform to a spectrum?

By a Fourier transform.
