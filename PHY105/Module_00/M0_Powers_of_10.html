Powers of 10

Viewing guide for the classic film Powers of Ten (1977) by Charles and Ray Eames, narrated by physicist Philip Morrison.

Why watch this This is a classic video from the late 1970s. It will help you get a better idea of the scale of the universe and how scientists use scientific notation to express very large and very small numbers. In nine minutes it travels across 40 powers of ten — from the edge of the visible universe down to the inside of a single atom.

How the film works

The film opens on a one-meter-wide view of a picnic in a Chicago park, seen from one meter away. Then a simple rule takes over: every 10 seconds, the camera moves 10 times farther out, so the square you see becomes 10 times wider. One meter becomes ten, ten becomes a hundred, and within a couple of minutes you are looking at the whole Earth, then the solar system, then the galaxy — until whole clusters of galaxies look like scattered dust.

Then the trip runs in reverse, returning to the picnic — and keeps going inward. Now each step shrinks the view by a factor of ten, down through the skin of a sleeping picnicker, into a cell, a strand of DNA, an atom, and finally the protons of a carbon nucleus, where the film reaches the edge of what physics could describe in 1977.

Watching once straight through is worth it just for the ride. Then look at the ladder below and notice what a factor of ten means at each rung.

One detail worth savoring on the way out: most of the universe looks like the last frame — nearly empty. As the narration puts it, “the richness of our own neighborhood is the exception.”

The point: scientific notation

The film is really a demonstration of how exponents tame enormous ranges of size. Each power of ten is one factor of ten:

Reading powers of ten
10⁶ = 1,000,000 (a 1 followed by six zeros)
10⁰ = 1 (our starting square, 1 m)
10⁻² = 1/100 = 0.01 (one centimeter is 10⁻² m)

Positive exponents multiply by ten; negative exponents divide by ten. The film's whole journey spans 10²⁴ m down to 10⁻¹⁶ m — a ratio of 10⁴⁰, a 1 followed by 40 zeros. Writing that range any other way would be hopeless.

Where does sound live on this ladder? This course happens in the middle rungs. The wavelengths of sounds you can hear run from about 2 cm (10⁻² m, the highest pitches) to about 17 m (10¹ m, the deepest bass) — human-sized scales. But the eardrum motion that lets you hear a faint sound can be smaller than the width of an atom (~10⁻¹⁰ m). You will use powers of ten constantly in this class.

As you watch, notice…

  • How fast the outbound trip escapes everything familiar — the Earth is gone barely a minute in. Ten seconds per power of ten adds up quickly.
  • The alternation between busy regions and long stretches of emptiness, on both the way out and the way in.
  • That the inward journey covers only 16 powers of ten while the outward one covers 24 — and yet the inside of an atom feels just as vast and empty as the space between galaxies.
  • How every scale got its own natural “yardstick”: meters for picnics, light-years for stars, angstroms for atoms — all just powers of ten in disguise.

Review

  • Scientific notation expresses very large and very small numbers as powers of ten.
  • Each step of one power of ten changes the scale by a factor of ten — forty such steps span the known universe, from galaxy clusters to quarks.
  • Choosing a sensible unit (km, m, cm, nm) is just choosing a convenient power of ten.
