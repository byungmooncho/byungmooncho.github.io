Trig Functions

A lesson by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman, adapted for PHY 105.

Why this matters The trig functions and the Pythagorean theorem let you find the missing sides and angles of right triangles — and right triangles turn up everywhere in physics. Get sin θ, cos θ, and tan θ down pat now and they will pay for themselves all semester.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able…

  1. To use the trig functions sin θ, cos θ, and tan θ.
  2. To find the missing sides and/or angles of a right triangle when given sufficient information.

Math you should know down pat

Every right triangle has a right angle, two other angles (call them θ and φ), and three sides. Relative to the angle θ, the sides have names: the hypotenuse (the longest side, across from the right angle), the opposite side (across from θ), and the adjacent side (touching θ).

The three trig functions — “SOHCAHTOA”
FunctionDefinitionFor θ aboveFor φ above
sinopposite / hypotenusesin θ = B/Csin φ = A/C
cosadjacent / hypotenusecos θ = A/Ccos φ = B/C
tanopposite / adjacenttan θ = B/Atan φ = A/B

And the Pythagorean theorem relates the three sides: A² + B² = C²  (equivalently x² + y² = r²).

Think about triangles Does the Pythagorean equation work for a triangle without a right angle? (No — it and the trig ratios above only apply to right triangles.) And what is the longest side of a right triangle? (Always the hypotenuse — if your computed “hypotenuse” comes out shorter than a leg, something went wrong.)

The “unit” circle

The unit circle shows us the relationship between the sides of a triangle. Draw a circle of radius r, and a line from the center at angle θ above the x-axis. The tip of that line sits at the point (x, y), and the line is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs x and y. It is typically taught with r = 1 — hence, the “unit” circle — and it is also where trigonometric functions are usually introduced.

Sin, cos, and tan on the circle

The three functions, in x, y, and r
sin θ = yr     cos θ = xr     tan θ = yx
tan θ = sin θcos θ = y/rx/r = yr × rx = yx

This same triangle is how a vector r⃗ gets split into its components: r⃗ₓ = r cos θ and r⃗ᵧ = r sin θ. Graphed from 0° to 360°, sin and cos gently wave between −1 and +1, while tan — being sin ÷ cos — blows up wherever cos θ hits zero, at 90° and 270°.

Quadrants and signs

The functions change sign as you go into the different quadrants of the circle — x and y can each be negative, and the signs of sin, cos, and tan follow along:

Signs by quadrant
QuadrantAngle rangesin θcos θtan θ
I0°–90°+++
II90°–180°+−−
III180°–270°−−+
IV270°–360°−+−

In practice, we often work with the triangle's acute angle and insert the sign “by hand” from the picture — a vector pointing down has a negative y-component, whatever the calculator says.

Be careful with angles

The definitions never change — opposite over hypotenuse is always sine — but which side counts as “opposite” and which as “adjacent” changes when you change the angle of interest, and changes again when the triangle is drawn in a different orientation. Always identify O and A from the angle you are using, not from habit:

Same definitions, re-read from the picture
sin θ = xr    cos θ = yr    tan θ = xy      sin φ = yr    cos φ = xr    tan φ = yx

Compare with the unit-circle triangle earlier, where sin θ was y/r. Neither is “the” formula — SOHCAHTOA plus a careful look at the triangle is the formula.

Review

  • The trig functions and Pythagorean theorem are useful when studying triangles. This happens often in physics.
  • Make sure to look at the triangle, and the placement of the angles, when determining the proper trig function.
  • The signs of the trig functions depend on angle ranges and can be determined by the quadrant.
