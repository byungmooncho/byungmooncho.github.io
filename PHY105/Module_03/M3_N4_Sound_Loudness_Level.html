Sound Loudness Level

SynopsisIn this note, we delve deeper into sound loudness. Recognize that sound loudness level is distinct from sound intensity level, which is measured in decibels (dB). Sound loudness is a subjective measure, while sound intensity level is an objective measure.
Performance ExpectationsPE1 Be able to describe the sound loudness level chart. PE2 Be able to describe the response of the human ear to different frequencies. PE3 Be able to identify the cause of extra sensitivity of the human ear around 3000 Hz.
InstructionsWork with a partner. You need the room tone generator (or szynalski.com/tone-generator) and your phone as a sound meter (phyphox → Acoustics → Audio Amplitude), the same setup as N3. Keep your phone in one spot for a whole table so the readings compare.

0 · Two tones, one number

I am going to play you two tones. A 100 Hz tone and a 1000 Hz tone. Your phone will read the same number of decibels for both of them — check it yourself.

Predict before you listen

Before you listen. Will they sound equally loud? Decide before you play anything — then play a 100 Hz tone and a 1000 Hz tone at the same reading and listen.

They do not. The 1000 Hz tone sounds much louder, and the meter said they were identical. So which one is wrong, the meter or your ear?

Neither. They are answering two different questions. The meter measures how much energy is arriving — that is sound intensity level, in decibels, and it is the same for both tones. Your ear reports how loud the sound seems — that is loudness, and it is not the same at all. This whole note is about the second one, and about the unit we need because decibels cannot do the job.

1 · Why decibels cannot do this job

Everything you have measured in Module 3 so far has been objective. Intensity in W/m², level in decibels — a meter reads them, and the number does not change depending on who is standing there.

Loudness is subjective. It is a fact about a listener, not about the air. It depends on frequency, as you just heard. It depends on the listener. There is no instrument you can point at a sound that reads loudness directly, because loudness only exists in a head.

So how do you put a number on something subjective? You cannot measure it. But you can compare it — a listener can say reliably whether two sounds seem equally loud, even when they cannot say how loud either one is. That comparison is the whole trick, and the phon is built on it.

Sound loudness is measured in phons (logarithmic scale) or sones (linear scale derived from phons). Both are perceived as loudness, or determined psycho-acoustically.

The distinction this note turns onSound intensity level, in dB, is objective — a meter reads it. Loudness is subjective — it is a fact about a listener. You cannot measure loudness directly. You can compare it.

2 · What a phon actually is

A phon is not a formula. It is a procedure — a recipe you carry out with a listener and a reference tone. Here it is in full.

  1. Take the sound you want to put a number on. Call it the mystery sound.
  2. Play a 1000 Hz tone beside it. This is the reference, always 1000 Hz.
  3. Turn the 1000 Hz tone up or down until the two sound equally loud to you.
  4. Read the sound pressure level of the 1000 Hz tone, in dB.
  5. That number is the loudness level of the mystery sound, in phons.

That is the entire definition. Nothing is calculated. The phon value of a sound is borrowed from whatever the 1000 Hz tone had to be set to in order to match it.

The phon scale is a perceived loudness reference to a specific sound volume at 1000 Hz. When you play a 1000 Hz tone at 40 dB — where we use sound pressure level (see the Sound Pressure Level reference page in Brightspace) — we say this is 40 phons. Our sense of loudness varies with the pitch or frequency. Our hearing is less sensitive to lower frequency (pitch) sound, so we must turn up the dial to match the same loudness at 1000 Hz.

Why 1000 Hz? No deep reason — it is a convention, picked because it sits in the middle of the range we hear best. It works the way sea level works for altitude: the choice is arbitrary, but once everybody agrees on it, every measurement can be compared.

One consequence falls straight out of the recipe, and it catches people. At 1000 Hz the mystery sound is the reference, so no adjusting is needed and the two numbers come out identical.

At 1000 Hz, and only there:   phons = dB SPL
Worked example

A 50 Hz tone reads 88 dB on the meter. You match it against a 1000 Hz tone and find that the 1000 Hz tone has to sit at 60 dB to sound equally loud. What is the loudness level of the 50 Hz tone?

60 phons. You read the number off the reference tone, never off the mystery sound.

Look at what that says: this one tone is 88 dB and 60 phons at the same time. Both numbers are right. 88 dB is how much energy is arriving. 60 phons is how loud it seems. The gap of 28 dB is the price your ear charges for the sound being low-pitched.

Check yourself.

A 1000 Hz tone reads 75 dB. What is its loudness level in phons?

75 phons. At 1000 Hz the two scales agree by definition — the reference is matching itself.

A 10 kHz tone reads 40 dB and a 1000 Hz tone at 25 dB sounds equally loud. Give the loudness level of the 10 kHz tone.

25 phons — the reference tone's level, not the 40 dB.

Can two sounds have the same dB and different phons? Same phons and different dB?

Yes to both. Same dB and different phons is the demonstration this note opened with. Same phons and different dB is every pair of points on one contour line in Figure 1 — which is exactly what that chart is for.

3 · The equal-loudness contours

Doing that matching procedure once gives you one number. Do it for every frequency, with a lot of listeners, average the results, and you get a line — every combination of frequency and level that sounds equally loud. Do it again at a different loudness and you get another line. That is Figure 1.

We can represent the same loudness level as contour lines.

Each line is named by the dB SPL it passes through at 1000 Hz — which is just the recipe again. The line through 60 dB at the 1000 Hz mark is the 60 phon line, and every point on it sounds equally loud.

In the above figure, the lines represent the same loudness we perceive given sound pressure levels. At 40 phons, you perceive the same sound loudness, 55 dB at 10 kHz and 40 dB at 1000 Hz.

Reading the chart, both ways

• Going one way (level → loudness): find the frequency along the bottom, go up to the sound pressure level, and see which contour you have landed on. That line's name is the loudness level in phons.

• Going the other way (loudness → level): find the contour you want, follow it to your frequency, and read the level off the left-hand axis.

Every contour sags in the middle and climbs at both ends. Where the line is low, little sound pressure is needed — the ear is sensitive there. Where it is high, you must turn the dial up for the same loudness — the ear is insensitive there. The lowest point sits near 3 kHz, which N5 takes up.

Worked example

A 100 Hz tone is playing at 60 dB SPL. Roughly what is its loudness level in phons?

Find 100 Hz along the bottom and go up to 60 dB. You land between the 20 and 40 phon lines, closer to the 40 — call it about 35 phons.

Read that back in words: a 100 Hz tone at 60 dB sounds about as loud as a 1000 Hz tone at 35 dB. The meter says 60 either way; the ear does not.

4 · Activity A — build your own contour

Figure 1 was built by having people match tones. You are going to do exactly that and plot your own 60 phon line.

Set the tone generator to 1000 Hz and adjust the volume until your phone reads about 60 dB. Do not touch that dial again — it is your reference.

Switch to the next test frequency. Listen, do not look. Turn the volume up or down until the tone sounds as loud as the reference did. One partner turns the dial, the other listens with eyes shut.

Now look at the phone and write down the level in dB. Repeat for every frequency.

Your numbers will not match Figure 1 exactly. Give two honest reasons why.

The contours are an average over many listeners and your ears are your own; the room reflects sound, so the level at your ear is not what the phone reads; a phone microphone is not calibrated and rolls off at both ends; and matching by ear has real uncertainty — repeat one frequency and see how far apart your two answers come out.

In sound intensity terms, how much larger is the intensity at 100 Hz compared to 1000 Hz at 60 phon, to perceive them as the same loudness?

80 dB − 60 dB = 20 dB, which is two powers of ten, so the intensity at 100 Hz must be 100× larger. Same loudness, a hundred times the intensity — that is how insensitive the ear is down there.

A hi-fi has a “loudness” button that boosts the bass when you listen quietly. Use Figure 1 to say why that is not a gimmick.

At low listening levels the contours are further apart at the bass end than at mid frequencies, so turning the overall level down drops the bass below threshold faster than the midrange. The button puts back what your own ears take away.

6 · Sones — the scale where twice means twice

Phons have a limit, and it is worth being blunt about it. Phons tell you which sounds are equally loud. They do not tell you how much louder one is than another. 80 phon does not sound twice as loud as 40 phon — it sounds about sixteen times as loud. The phon scale inherited that from the decibel, because it is read off a decibel axis.

So we build a second scale on top of it, designed so that the number behaves the way people expect: twice the number means twice as loud. That is the sone.

Sones is another loudness scale. Here, 40 phons is 1 sone. And every 10-phon increase doubles in sones. So, 50 phons is equivalent to 2 sones, 60 phons is equivalent to 4 sones, and so on (see table and graph below).

The whole sone scale, in two lines

• The anchor: 40 phon = 1 sone. One sone is roughly a quiet room.

• The rule: every +10 phon doubles the sones, and every −10 phon halves them.

Those two facts are the whole scale. You can climb the ladder in your head and never need the formula.

S = 2(P − 40)/10

S in sones, P in phons. The formula is only for values between the rungs. For anything on a rung, count the doublings — it is faster and harder to get wrong.

Worked example

A sound is 80 phon. How many sones? And how much louder does it sound than a 40 phon sound?

Count the doublings up from the anchor: 40 → 50 → 60 → 70 → 80 is four steps. Sones double each step: 1 → 2 → 4 → 8 → 16.

So 80 phon is 16 sones, and because the sone scale is built to behave this way, it sounds 16 times as loud as the 1 sone sound. Not twice — sixteen times.
Worked example

A sound is 20 phon. How many sones?

Count down from the anchor: 40 → 30 → 20 is two steps, and each step halves. 1 → 0.5 → 0.25 sones. A quarter as loud as the quiet room.

At 0.5 sones, how much loudness decreased in phons?

1 sone is 40 phon. Halving the sones is one step down the ladder, so 0.5 sone is 30 phon — a decrease of 10 phons.

A sound is 70 phon. How many sones, and how many times louder than a 40 phon sound?

70 phon is three doublings above 40: 8 sones, and it sounds 8 times as loud.

Your phone reads 70 dB for a 1000 Hz tone. Give its loudness level in phons, and its loudness in sones.

At 1000 Hz, dB and phons agree: 70 phons. Then 70 phon is three doublings above 40, so 8 sones. Three numbers for one sound — 70 dB, 70 phons, 8 sones — and each answers a different question.

Which is bigger, the difference between 40 and 50 phon, or between 90 and 100 phon? Answer in phons first, then in sones.

In phons they are the same, 10 either way. In sones they are nothing alike: 40 → 50 phon goes 1 → 2 sones, a rise of 1 sone. 90 → 100 phon goes 32 → 64 sones, a rise of 32 sones. This is the whole reason the sone scale exists.
