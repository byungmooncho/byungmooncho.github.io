Only Waves Can Interfere, Diffract, And Refract

SynopsisOne of the waves’ defining characteristics is that they interfere. Wave interference results from the simple addition of waves’ amplitudes. That is it. When waves overlap and add, the resultant wave’s amplitude reflects the two waves’ phase relationship: larger when they are in-phase, smaller when they are out-of-phase. Two waves of identical amplitude and wavelength give twice the amplitude while completely in-phase, and zero — complete cancellation — while completely out-of-phase.

A phase repeats every complete cycle, i.e. the wavelength. So waves are in-phase if they are shifted by exactly one wavelength. If one wave’s phase is shifted by half a wavelength with respect to the other, the two are 180° out-of-phase: the peaks in one align with the troughs in the other, and interference causes complete cancellation.

Recalling two waves rippling away from the sources in the previous PhET simulations: when two waves arrive at a position and overlap, the factor that decides constructive or destructive is Δd, the difference in distances from the sources. Integer multiples of a wavelength → constructive. Multiples of a wavelength plus a half → destructive.

Diffraction is a result of wave interference, with one added detail: the wave goes through an aperture, and diffraction describes how it propagates after it does. Why do we care? Diffraction lets waves reach behind obstacles — it is how you hear sound around a corner, pick up radio over a hill, or get a phone signal in a space full of things.

Performance ExpectationsDescribe constructive and destructive wave interference using waves’ phase relationships, and connect those phase relationships to the wavelength. Describe diffraction using wave interference, recognizing that waves spread out off the surfaces and edges they sweep over, and that a large aperture with a short wavelength gives the clearest fringe pattern.

Part I — Interference

  1. Open the PhET Wave Interference sim, Interference panel, Water. Leave the defaults (frequency middle, amplitude max, separation 3 cm). Put the grey and black probes where the traces are completely in-phase — black on the first bright ring, grey on the second. Do the traces confirm it?

    Yes. Both probes are at peaks to start with.

  2. What is the wavelength?

    λ ≈ 2.7 cm

  3. What is the frequency? (Find the period first. How?)

    Period 1.45 s, so f = 1 / 1.45 = 0.6250 Hz

  4. What is the amplitude? (One vertical tick mark is 1 cm.)

    A ≈ 0.4 cm

  5. What is the speed of the wave?

    v = λf = (2.7 cm)(0.6250 Hz) = 1.69 cm/s

  6. Black probe on the first bright ring, grey on the second. What is the difference in distances from the two water drops?

    Δd1 = 1.8 cm

  7. Move the grey probe to the third bright ring. What is the difference now?

    Δd2 = 4.5 cm

  8. How are Δd1 and Δd2 related to the wavelength?

    Δd = λ = Δd2 − Δd1 = 4.5 − 1.8 = 2.7 cm — exactly one wavelength.

  9. Now place the probes so the traces are completely out-of-phase — black on the first bright ring, grey on the first dark ring. Do the traces confirm it?

    Yes. Black on peak, grey on trough.

  10. Measure that difference, then move the grey probe to the second dark ring and measure again.

    Δd3 = 1.8 cm  ·  Δd4 = 5.9 cm

  11. How are Δd3 and Δd4 related to the wavelength?

    Δd = Δd4 − Δd3 = 5.9 − 1.8 = 4.1 cm = λ + ½λ

  12. Show from your in-phase answers that Δd = nλ, n = 0, 1, 2…

    Δd = d2 − d1 = 1 × λ

  13. Show from your out-of-phase answers that Δd = (n + ½)λ.

    Δd = d4 − d3 = λ + ½λ = (1 + ½)λ,  n = 1

  14. Turn on the second tap. Without pausing, put the black probe anywhere outside the fuzzy area and the grey probe wherever you see fuzziness. How can you say the black probe shows constructive interference? And the grey probe destructive?

    Black: amplitude larger.  Grey: zero amplitude.

  15. Water. Measure top-tap and bottom-tap distances to each probe and take the differences.

    Black probe 4.9 and 7.2 → Δdconstructive = 2.3 cm
    Grey probe 8.8 and 7.4 → Δddestructive = 1.4 cm

  16. Show that constructive happens where Δd = nλ, and destructive where Δd = (n + ½)λ. Explain using the phase relationship.

    Δdcon ≈ |7.2 − 4.9| = 2.3 ≈ 1λ, n = 1
    Δddest = |7.4 − 8.8| = 1.4 ≈ ½λ, n = 0

  17. Sound. Switch to the sound wave and find the wavelength, then repeat the measurement.

    λ = 120 cm
    Δdconstructive ≈ 100 cm ≈ 1λ
    Grey probe 378 and 326 → Δddestructive = 52 cm ≈ ½λ

  18. Light. Switch to the light wave and do it once more.

    λ ≈ 600 nm
    Black probe 4757 and 5367 → Δdconstructive = 610 nm ≈ 1λ
    Grey probe 5043 and 4754 → Δddestructive = 293 nm ≈ ½λ

Part II — Diffraction

  1. Run the PhET sim, Slits option, Light Generator. Check Screen and Intensity. Slide the frequency all the way left (reddest, longest wave), aperture about 1000 nm from the generator, One Slit. Set the slit width to maximum (1600 nm), send the planar waves, then set it to minimum (200 nm) and send again. Describe the difference in wavefront shape.

    Smaller slit: 200 nm width → rounder wave front.
    Larger slit: 1600 nm width → flatter wave front.

  2. Describe the difference in the intensity between the largest and smallest apertures.

    200 nm: low intensity.  1600 nm: high intensity.

  3. Repeat with the frequency slid all the way right (blue/purple, shortest wave). Wavefront shape?

    Red: rounder — reaches further behind the barrier.
    Blue: flatter.

  4. And the intensity?

    Red: broader but lower peak height.
    Blue: narrower but higher peak height.

  5. From the intensity profile, when do you see the clearest fringes?

    Largest slit width and shortest wavelength.

  6. The peaks in the intensity profile are bright fringes, the valleys dark fringes. Explain those terms using constructive and destructive interference.

    Bright fringe: constructive interference.
    Dark fringe: destructive interference.

  7. What are the wavelengths of the reddest and bluest frequencies? You need these below.

    Reddest: 780 nm  ·  Bluest: 450 nm

  8. This statement is critically important: diffraction is essentially understood by imagining the two edges of the hole to be new sources of waves. Set Two Slits, slit width minimum (200 nm), separation 1500 nm, and observe. What are the similarities between the one-slit and two-slit bright fringes, and why?

    Similarity: fringes — a diffraction pattern in both.

  9. What are the differences, and why?

    Difference: shape of the intensity profile.
    One slit: most light goes through.
    Two slits: pattern solely due to diffraction.

  10. Now the dark fringes in both profiles. Similarities, and differences?

    Similarity: fringes, the same diffraction pattern.
    Difference: intensity profile — one slit, most light gets through; two slits, the pattern is purely due to diffraction.

  11. One Slit, width back to maximum (1600 nm). Measure from the bottom edge to the first top bright fringe, and from the top edge to the same fringe. Take the difference and compare it to the wavelength.

    Bottom edge 6492 nm, top edge 5969 nm

  12. Repeat for the first top dark fringe.

    Bottom edge 4580 nm, top edge 3952 nm
    Δd ≈ 300 nm ≈ ½λ

  13. Two Slits, width minimum (200 nm), separation 1500 nm. Measure from the top hole and from the bottom hole to the first top bright fringe.

    Bottom 4880 nm, top 4454 nm
    Δd = 426 nm ≈ 1λ

  14. Repeat for the first top dark fringe.

    Bottom 4690 nm, top 4481 nm
    Δd = 209 nm ≈ ½λ
