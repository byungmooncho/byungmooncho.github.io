Lab 3 — Standing Waves on a String

Synopsis In Note 1 you drew the modes of a string fixed at both ends and wrote down that each one adds half a wave, so that f2 = 2f1, f3 = 3f1, and so on. Those were drawings. In this lab you make them appear on a real string and read their frequencies off a dial. You will find the fundamental, climb the harmonics one at a time, and plot frequency against harmonic number — and the slope of that plot is a number you have already predicted, which is what makes the plot worth making. Then you use one harmonic to get a wavelength, combine it with its frequency, and obtain the speed of a wave on the string.

Performance Expectations: Set up a driven string under measured tension and locate at least the first five resonances by adjusting the driving frequency. Record harmonic number and frequency as a data table, plot frequency against harmonic number, fit a trendline, and interpret its slope. Determine a wavelength from the geometry of a named harmonic and compute the wave speed from v = λf. Predict and then test how the tension changes the wave speed and the resonant frequencies. Distinguish what you measured from what you inferred, and cite your own data when you do.

Equipment: Pasco economy wave driver · Pasco sine wave generator · about 1.75 m of Mason’s twine · pulley and bracket · small lab stand · mass set (20 g, 50 g, 100 g, 200 g) · metre stick or tape measure · a laptop with Excel for the plot.

Setting up the apparatus

  1. Clamp the pulley and bracket firmly to the edge of the table. Place the wave driver about 1.5 m back from it along the table.

  2. Loop the string through the economy wave driver and tie the far end off to the small lab stand. Run the other end over the pulley and hang 200 g from it, making sure the mass hangs freely and is not resting on anything.

  3. Slide the wave driver so that the driver post and the centre of the pulley are 1.50 m apart. Adjust the pulley height until the string is horizontal.

  4. Measure and record L and the hanging mass. Then make your own sketch of the apparatus below, labelling the equipment and marking every distance and force you can identify. You will need this sketch in your report.

Part 1 — Find the fundamental

  1. Set the frequency to about 10 Hz. Raise it slowly, watching the string.

  2. At some point the string swings up and down as one single loop, with the largest motion at the centre and no motion at the ends. Fine-tune with the 1.0 Hz and 0.1 Hz knobs until the amplitude is as large as you can make it. That frequency is the fundamental, f1.

Questions for Discussion

Choose three of the following and answer those three fully, working them into your discussion section. Say which three you chose. Do not simply copy the question and answer it in a line — and do not limit yourself to these topics if you have other insights.

  1. What happened to the wavelength as the frequency increased? Express the relationship between the two as a proportionality, and name what was held constant to make it true.

  2. Why is the slope of your Part 3A graph the number it is? Answer in terms of the shapes on the string, not in terms of the trendline.

  3. The ends of the string are the one place where nothing moves. Explain why that single constraint is what forces the frequencies to come in a whole-number sequence instead of arbitrary values.

  4. From your Part 4 data: which of v, λ and f changed when you changed the tension, and which did not? Use that to say what physical property of the system the tension actually sets.

  5. The frequencies you found were the ones where the string responded with a large amplitude; at every other setting it barely moved. Connect that to the resonance argument you made about the Tacoma Narrows bridge in Note 1.

  6. A guitar string is tuned by turning a peg, and fretted by shortening it. Using your own two experiments, say which of those two changes v and which changes λ, and explain how each ends up changing the pitch.
