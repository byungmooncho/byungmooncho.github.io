Sound Is A Pressure Wave

SynopsisModule 1 asked what a wave is. This note asks what a sound wave is, and the answer is that it is a pressure wave. The air does not travel across the room with the sound — it crowds together and spreads apart in place, and what travels is the pattern. Everything else here follows from that: how fast the pattern moves, what the medium has to do with it, and what happens when it reaches a boundary and has to get into something else.
Performance ExpectationsDescribe a sound wave as a pressure variation about atmospheric pressure, and relate displacement to pressure in a standing sound wave. State what the speed of sound depends on and what it does not. Use the temperature relation to find the speed of sound in air, and the temperature from a measured speed. Use distance = speed × time. Explain in terms of impedance what happens when sound meets a boundary, and why an instrument needs a body.

Instructions and Questions

Module 1 asked what a wave is. A sound wave is a pressure wave. The air does not travel across the room with the sound — it crowds together and spreads apart in place. Where the molecules are crowded the pressure is above the everyday value P0; where they are spread out it is below. Those are the compressions and rarefactions.

If every molecule just moves back and forth about its own spot, what is it that actually travels across the room?

The pattern — the disturbance, and the energy it carries. No air makes the trip. This is the same point the slinky made in Note 1, and it is why sound in a room is not a draught.

Displacement and pressure are not in step

Here is the idea worth the most in this note. In a standing sound wave two things vary along the pipe: how far the molecules swing, and what the pressure does. They do not peak in the same places. They are a quarter of a wavelength apart.

Finish the sentence from your figure, both ways round:
a displacement node is a pressure ________, and a displacement antinode is a pressure ________.

A displacement node is a pressure antinode. A displacement antinode is a pressure node.

Why should that be? Think about what the molecules on either side of a displacement node are doing at the moment the node itself is still.

At a displacement node the air cannot move, but the air on either side is moving toward it and then away from it. Squeeze from both sides, then stretch from both sides — the biggest pressure swing in the pipe. At an antinode the molecules move together, as a block, so nothing is compressed and the pressure barely changes.
Keep this oneYou will need it in Module 4. When we close one end of a tube and ask where the harmonics fall, the closed end is a displacement node and therefore a pressure antinode. Students who have not separated the two words get every air-column question backwards.

What the speed of sound depends on

Sound needs something to travel in. Pump the air out of a jar with a bell ringing inside and the bell goes quiet while you can still see it striking. There is nothing left to compress.

Look down the table and write the rough pattern in one line each for gases, liquids and solids. Then say what hydrogen is doing there.

Gases around 300 m/s, liquids around 1,300, solids around 4,000. Sound goes faster in stiffer, more tightly bound media — the push passes along sooner when the neighbours are held closely. Hydrogen is the outlier: it is a gas, but so light that its molecules move very fast, and it carries sound at 1,269 m/s.

And on temperature

Room temperature is about 70 °F, which is about 20 °C. What is the speed of sound there?

v = 331.5 + 0.610(20) = 343.7 m/s. This is where the 343 m/s you have been using all semester comes from — it was never a constant, it was room temperature.

You run an experiment and measure 338 m/s. How warm was the room?

T = (338 − 331.5) / 0.610 = 10.7 °C, about 51 °F. Cold.

You see lightning and hear the thunder 10 seconds later. How far away did it strike? Light gets to you effectively instantly.

d = vt = 343 × 10 = 3,430 m, about 3.4 km or just over two miles. The rule of thumb — three seconds to the kilometre, five to the mile — is this calculation.

What happens when sound arrives at a boundary

Sound almost never stays in one medium. It leaves a string and enters wood, leaves the wood and enters air, leaves the air and enters your ear. At every boundary some of it goes on and some of it comes back, and which way that splits is set by impedance.

Impedance measures how poorly a wave gets from one medium into the next — think of an impediment. im- bad, ped- foot: something that slows you down impedes you. A big mismatch in impedance means most of the wave bounces back.

You want to soundproof a room so your drums do not get out. Should the wall material have a high, a matched, or a low impedance with the sound waves?

High. You want the wall to be as unlike air as possible so the sound bounces back into the room instead of travelling into the wall and out the other side.

The body of a guitar has to get the sound of the string out into the room. Should the guitar body have a high, a matched, or a low impedance with the air?

Matched — as close to air as you can manage, so the energy passes out instead of rattling around inside the wood.

Stretch a string between two posts with nothing else attached and pluck it. You can see it moving and you can barely hear it. Using the two answers above, say why — and say what the guitar body is actually for.

A bare string is thin and stiff and almost nothing like air, so the impedance mismatch between string and air is enormous and hardly any energy crosses. The string keeps its energy and rings for a long time, quietly. The body is an impedance matcher: the string drives the bridge, the bridge drives a large light plate of wood, and the plate is much better matched to air. It is louder and it dies away sooner, which is the same energy leaving faster.
Where this is goingEverything in Module 4 — strings, air columns, the way an instrument is actually built — is this note applied. The speed of sound sets the frequencies, the pressure/displacement pairing sets where the nodes fall, and impedance decides how much of it you ever hear.
