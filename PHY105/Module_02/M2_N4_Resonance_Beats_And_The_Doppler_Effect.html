Resonance, Beats And The Doppler Effect

SynopsisEvery object that can be pushed out of shape and spring back has one frequency it already wants — its natural frequency. This note is what follows from that. Drive an object at the frequency it wants and the pushes add up: that is resonance, and how sharply it happens is decided by damping. Sound two frequencies close together and the loudness itself starts to oscillate: those are beats. Move the source while it sounds and you change the spacing of the crests that reach the listener: that is the Doppler effect. Three different effects, all of them about frequencies arriving at the same place at once.
Performance ExpectationsState what a pendulum's period does and does not depend on, and what damping changes. Explain resonance in terms of a driving frequency matching a natural frequency, and read a resonance response curve for the effect of damping on the height and the width of the peak. Identify the mass and the spring in a Helmholtz resonator and predict which way the pitch moves when the volume of air changes. Find a beat frequency from two frequencies and use beats to tune. Explain the Doppler effect in terms of crest spacing, say what it does and does not change, and work a numerical case both ways.

Instructions and Questions

Note 3 was about a sound wave travelling. This one is about three things that happen when it does not simply travel: when something is driven at the frequency it already wants, when two frequencies arrive at once, and when the thing making the sound is moving while it makes it.

Every object already has a frequency

Pull a pendulum aside and let it go and it swings at one particular rate. Nobody chose that rate — the pendulum has it. That is its natural frequency, and every object that can be deformed and spring back has one: a string, a wine glass, a wooden plate, a column of air, a bridge.

Two pendulums hang side by side on strings of the same length. One has a heavy bob and one has a light bob. You pull both aside by the same small amount and release them together. Which one finishes its first swing first?

Neither — they stay together. The period of a pendulum depends on its length (and on gravity), and not on the mass of the bob. A heavier bob has more weight pulling it back, but it also has more inertia to be moved, and the two cancel exactly.

Now pull one of them aside twice as far as the other — still a small swing — and release them together. Which finishes first?

Still neither. The one pulled further has further to travel, but it also travels faster, and again these cancel. For small swings the period does not depend on amplitude. That is exactly why a pendulum works as a clock: as it runs down and the swings get smaller, it keeps time.

A real pendulum does run down. Air drags on it and the pivot rubs, and this is called damping. As a damped pendulum runs down, what happens to the size of its swing, and what happens to the time one swing takes?

The amplitude shrinks, swing after swing, until it stops. The period stays very nearly the same the whole way down. Damping takes the energy out; it barely touches the frequency. This is the same reason a plucked guitar string gets quieter without going flat.

Driving it: resonance

So far the object was let go and left alone. Now push it, over and over, at a frequency you choose. Call that the driving frequency. The object still has its own natural frequency f0. The whole of resonance is what happens as you change one against the other.

Drive it far from f0 and you are fighting it: you push while it is coming back at you and almost nothing builds up. Drive it at f0 and every push arrives exactly when the object is already going that way. The pushes add. That is resonance, and the frequency it happens at is the resonant frequency.

A child on a swing is a pendulum with a natural frequency of about one swing every three seconds. You want to get her as high as possible. Describe how you should push — and then say what happens if you push twice as often as that instead.

Push once every three seconds, at the moment she is moving away from you: you are driving at f0 and every push adds to what is already there. Push twice as often and half of your pushes arrive while she is coming back toward you — those take energy out. She goes nowhere. Driving at the wrong frequency is not merely less good, it actively fights the motion.

Two wine glasses, one thin crystal and one thick cheap glass. A singer can shatter one of them with a sustained note and not the other. Using the curves in Figure 1, say which one and why — and say why she has to hit the note almost exactly.

The thin crystal. It is the lightly damped one: its peak is very tall, so a note at its natural frequency builds the vibration up far enough to break it. But its peak is also very narrow, which is why she has to be almost exactly on pitch — a little off and the response collapses. The thick glass is heavily damped: it turns the energy into heat as fast as she puts it in, and never builds up.

A bottle is a resonator

Blow across the top of an empty bottle and it sounds one note. The bottle is a Helmholtz resonator, and it is the cleanest real example of a natural frequency there is, because you can see both of the parts it is made of.

Pour water into the bottle, a third full, and blow across it again. Does the note go up or down? Say why in terms of the spring.

Up. The water takes up some of the body, so there is less air left to act as the spring. A smaller volume of air is a stiffer spring — squeeze it the same distance and it pushes back harder — and a stiffer spring on the same mass gives a higher frequency. Keep filling and the note keeps climbing.
The trap in that questionTap the same bottle on the side with a spoon instead of blowing across it and the pitch goes the other way — it falls as you fill it. That is because tapping sets the glass and the water vibrating, which is a different oscillator with more mass in it. Blowing across the top is the air. Same bottle, two different natural frequencies, opposite answers. Read which one the question is asking about.

Two frequencies at once: beats

Play two notes of exactly the same frequency and you hear one steady note. Now move one of them a few hertz off. The two waves are no longer in step, and because they drift in and out of step the sound does something new: it throbs. It gets loud, then soft, then loud again, at a slow and perfectly regular rate.

fbeat = | f1 − f2 |The beat frequency is the frequency of the loudness oscillation, not of any tone you are hearing. The pitch you hear is the average of the two, fave = (f1 + f2)/2.

Two flutes play 440 Hz and 444 Hz together. How many beats will you hear per second, and what pitch will you hear throbbing?

fbeat = |444 − 440| = 4 beats per second. The pitch you hear is the average, 442 Hz — a note that neither flute is actually playing, throbbing four times a second.

You are tuning a guitar string against a 440 Hz tuning fork and you hear three beats a second. (a) What are the two frequencies the string could be at? (b) You tighten the string a little and the beats speed up to five a second. Which of the two was it, and which way should you have gone?

(a) The beat frequency is the size of the difference, so the string is three hertz either side: 437 Hz or 443 Hz. The beats alone cannot tell you which. (b) Tightening raises a string's frequency. The beats got faster, so you moved away from 440 — the string was already sharp, at 443 Hz, and you should have loosened it. Tune toward slower beats; in tune is when they stop altogether.

When the source is moving: the Doppler effect

A train comes toward you with its horn on and the pitch is high. It passes, and in that instant the pitch drops — the same horn, sounding lower. Nothing about the horn changed. What changed is where each crest was launched from.

The thing to be clear aboutThe speed of sound does not change. It is set by the air, not by the source. What the motion changes is the wavelength, and since v = fλ with v fixed, a shorter wavelength has to mean a higher frequency. Every Doppler question is that one sentence.

You are standing on a platform and a train approaches, sounding its horn. (a) Is the pitch you hear higher or lower than the horn's real pitch? (b) As the train gets closer and closer — still approaching — does that pitch keep climbing? (c) What happens the instant it passes you?

(a) Higher, because the crests reaching you are crowded together. (b) No — it stays steady. The shift depends on the train's speed, not on how close it is. At constant speed you hear one steady raised pitch the whole way in; it only gets louder. (c) The pitch drops, all at once, to a steady lowered pitch as it recedes. That sudden drop as it goes past is the Doppler effect, and it is a step, not a slide.

Now you are sitting on the train, next to the horn, as it does all that. What do you hear?

The horn's real pitch, unshifted, the whole time. You and the source are moving together, so the crests are not crowding up on you at all — each one is launched from the same place relative to you. Only someone the train is moving toward or away from hears a shift.

A train is coming toward you at 20 m/s and you measure its horn at 800 Hz. Take the speed of sound as 340 m/s. (a) What frequency is the horn actually sounding? (b) After it passes and is going away from you at the same 20 m/s, what will you measure?

Use fobs / (V − vobs) = fsrc / (V − vsrc), with velocities measured along the line from source to observer. (a) You are still, so vobs = 0, and the train comes toward you at vsrc = +20. fsrc = 800 × (340 − 20)/340 = 753 Hz. (b) Now vsrc = −20. fobs = 753 × 340/(340 + 20) = 711 Hz. The horn never changed: 753 Hz throughout. You heard 800, then 711.
