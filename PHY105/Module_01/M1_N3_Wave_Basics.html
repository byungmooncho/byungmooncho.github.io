FLCC Physics Module 1: Waves Note 3: Wave Basics

Every Wave Is Three Numbers And A Phase

Synopsis A wave in physics is an umbrella term encompassing all things repetitive. When physicists hear the word “wave,” they think of oscillations, repeating patterns, cyclical behaviours — things that extend or spread over space and time. They also think of the sinusoidal functions (sines and cosines) that can describe all such repetitions. And a sinusoid needs only three numbers to fix its shape: amplitude, wavelength and frequency — plus one more idea, phase, the moment you want to compare two waves. You have already met the three numbers and watched what each one does to a wave, and you will see before the end that the medium ties the last two of them together. In this note we pull them together, put them on a graph, and use them to work out one more thing the first two notes could not give you: how fast the wave actually travels.

Waves carry energy

Think about the energy in waves. If you see massive ocean waves crashing into shores, toppling buildings, trees and other structures, clearly there is an enormous amount of energy in those waves. That energy came from somewhere. Perhaps there was an undersea earthquake that generated a tsunami — so the energy in those ocean waves started as potential energy in locked-up tectonic plates, under tremendous tension and then abruptly released. The earthquake could have happened thousands of miles from the shore and still inflict massive damage, because the waves carry the energy. The water that does the waving serves as the medium that allows the energy to be transmitted. An ocean wave is an example of a mechanical wave: it can carry energy as long as there is an expanse of water that can “wave.” Many wave phenomena are mechanical waves and require a medium to propagate through. Waves on a string need the string as the medium to carry the energy of the shaker, as you saw in the PhET simulation in Note 2.

Not every wave works this way, and the exception is worth naming now because it will keep turning up. Light, radio, microwaves and x-rays are electromagnetic waves, and they need no medium at all — sunlight crosses 150 million kilometres of empty space to reach us. The classic demonstration is a ringing bell inside a jar: pump the air out and you can still see the bell shaking, but you can no longer hear it. Take away the medium and the mechanical wave dies; the electromagnetic one carries on. Everything else in this course is mechanical, so “needs a medium” is the safe assumption — but sound is mechanical and light is not, and that one line separates them.

What actually moves

Now that you see that waves carry energy, what does the mechanical motion of the medium look like? In other words, what motion carries the energy? Take ocean waves again, with water as the medium. If you could “chunk” sections of the ocean, the wave is nothing more than these chunks of water moving up and down individually — there is no flow of ocean water from the earthquake to the shore. A perfect analogy is the “Mexican wave” you sometimes see in a stadium when the spectators are bored. The key feature of that wave is that it sweeps through and goes right around the stadium without halting — and crucially, the spectators only stand up and sit back down. They never shift from their seats. They only need to stand and sit at the right moment, and that timing is what dictates the wavelength and frequency of the wave. So the medium transports the energy, but not the material the medium is made of.

Two ways for the medium to move

Ocean waves, sound waves and waves on a string are all mechanical waves in which the mechanical motion of the medium transmits the energy of the wave generator, while the material the medium is made of is not transported. Ocean waves and waves on a string are just like the Mexican wave: the wave moves, let us say, from left to right, but the spectators themselves move up and down. Here the direction of wave travel (“left to right”) is perpendicular to the medium’s mechanical motion (“up and down”). This type of wave is called a transverse wave (trans— across).

A sound wave, in contrast, is a repeating pattern of high and low air pressure moving through space. The high and low pressure is the bunching up of air molecules (high pressure) and the spacing out of air molecules (low pressure), caused by a chunk of air repeatedly moving “left–right, left–right.” The pressure pattern itself, meanwhile, travels from left to right. In other words, the direction of wave travel is parallel to the mechanical motion of the medium. This type of wave is called a longitudinal wave (longitudinal — along).

Phase

The last idea is the phase of a wave. When you hear “phase” in physics, picture a moon phase. A phase of the moon is a specific part — a specific place — in the cycle of moon shapes: the full moon phase is the spot in the cycle where the moon is a complete disc. A phase of a wave is a specific place in the wave, and that is all it is. You can say peak phase, or trough phase. And once you have the word, you can define the other terms with it: the wavelength is the distance between identical phases in adjacent cycles, and the period is the duration between identical phases in adjacent cycles. For example, the wavelength is the distance from one peak phase to the peak phase of the next cycle.

Phase is most useful when you are comparing two waves. Imagine two waves travelling in the same medium with the same wavelength (so, at the same speed, the same frequency too — why?), and lay one on top of the other. You might find that all the phases align: the peak phase of one sits on the peak phase of the other. That is in phase. Or you might find the peak phase of one lining up with the trough phase of the other — one wave is shifted in phase with respect to the other. Peak on trough is 180° out of phase, or completely out of phase. When the phases of two waves do not align at all, we simply say the waves are out of phase.

Where this is going You now have every number a single wave can have: A, λ, f, T, and the speed that ties them together. Notice what the last section quietly added — a way to talk about two waves at once. Once two waves are in the same place, their phases decide everything about what you see and hear: peaks on peaks reinforce, peaks on troughs cancel. That is interference, and it is where the rest of this course lives.
