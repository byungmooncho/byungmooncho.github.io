Shaking Faster Does Not Make The Wave Go Faster — Answer Key

Instructor copy. Do not hand out, and do not upload to byungmooncho.github.io — that site is public and the student handout lives beside it.
What this note is testing Describe a wave with three numbers — amplitude, wavelength and frequency — and generate waves with different values of each using the simulation’s controls. Measure a wavelength and a period from the simulation and combine them into the wave’s speed. Predict what changing the frequency, the amplitude and the tension does to that speed, and check every prediction against the simulation.

The one thing to grade on: whether the student comes out of Q14–Q16 saying the speed belongs to the medium. Raising the frequency shortened the wavelength and left the speed alone; raising the tension changed the speed. Nearly every wrong answer in this note is some version of “shake it harder or faster and it goes faster.”

Numbers to expect At 1.00 Hz, Tension Medium, Damping None the wavelength on the ruler is about 3.8 cm (accept roughly 3.5–4.2 cm — reading a peak centre off that ruler is worth no more than two significant figures). That fixes the speed at about 3.8 cm/s, and every row of the Q14 table should reproduce it. Do not grade the absolute number; grade whether their three speeds agree with each other.

Preamble — what their screen should look like

Check before they start Oscillate (not Manual, not Pulse) · No End on the right · Normal speed · Damping: None · Tension: Medium · Rulers, Timer and Reference Line all ticked · Amplitude 0.50 cm · Frequency 1.00 Hz. The two settings students skip are No End and Damping None, and skipping either wrecks the measurements: a Fixed or Loose End sends a reflection back and the standing-wave pattern that results has no single travelling wavelength to measure, and any damping makes the amplitude shrink along the string.

Instructions — answers

  1. Press Play. How is the moving wave generated? Compare with what your own hand did in Note 1.

    AnswerThe left-hand end of the string is clamped to a wheel that turns steadily and drives that end up and down, over and over. Each new up-and-down is handed along to the neighbouring bit of string, exactly as in Note 1 — the wheel is doing the job their hand did, but perfectly steadily and without getting tired, so the amplitude and the rhythm never drift.
    Watch for“The computer draws it” or “it just appears.” Push them back to the left-hand end: something physical is being shaken there, and everything downstream follows from that.

  2. Watch one bead. Which way does it move? How far does it get from where it started? Same as the twist-tie in Note 1, or different?

    AnswerThe bead moves straight up and down — across the string, at right angles to the direction the wave travels. It never travels to the right at all: it oscillates about its own rest position and keeps returning to it. The furthest it ever gets from rest is the amplitude, 0.50 cm above and 0.50 cm below. Same as the twist-tie in Note 1 — the medium stays home, the pattern travels.
    Watch for“The bead moves along with the wave.” This is the misconception of Module 1 and it survives Note 1 in a surprising number of students. Have them put a fingertip on the screen over one bead and hold it there for ten seconds.

  3. What does the Reference Line (the dashed line) indicate?

    AnswerIt marks the string’s rest (equilibrium) position — where the string would sit if nobody were shaking it. It is the line every displacement, including the amplitude, is measured from.
    Watch for“The middle of the wave” is acceptable only if they can say the middle of what. Steer them to “where the string would be at rest.”

  4. What does the amplitude slider change? Measured from where, to where?

    AnswerIt changes how tall the crests and how deep the troughs are — how far the string is displaced from rest. Amplitude is measured from the reference line to a crest (equally, from the reference line down to a trough). It does not change the wavelength, the frequency or the speed.
    Watch forCrest-to-trough. That distance is twice the amplitude and it is the single most common error on this page. If they wrote it, make them re-read the reference line question above — the two questions are deliberately adjacent.

  5. Freeze the wave and measure the wavelength.

    Answerλ ≈ 3.8 cm at 1.00 Hz with Tension Medium (accept about 3.5–4.2 cm). Any full repeat counts: peak to peak, trough to trough, or a crossing to the second crossing after it.
    Watch forHalf a wavelength — peak to the next trough — reported as λ. Also watch for students measuring while the wave is still running; the answer is only clean when it is paused.

  6. Which way is the wave moving?

    AnswerTo the right — away from the wheel, off the end of the screen and never back, because the far end is set to No End.

  7. Time ten peaks past the 3 cm mark. How long for ten? For one?

    AnswerAt 1.00 Hz, ten peaks take 10.0 s, so one peak-to-next takes 1.0 s. Timing ten and dividing is the point of the exercise: it spreads the student’s reaction time over ten intervals instead of one.
    Watch forCounting the starting peak as number one, which gives nine intervals for ten peaks. The timer starts as a peak passes and stops at the tenth one after it.

  8. Repeat at 1.5, 2, 2.5 and 3 Hz — table.

    Answer
    FrequencyTime for 10 peaksTime for one peak

    Hand-timing at 3 Hz is genuinely hard; Slow Motion is there for exactly this and students should be told to use it rather than to fudge.

  9. Relationship between f and the time for one peak to pass.

    AnswerThey are reciprocals: the time for one peak is 1/f. Multiply any row’s two numbers together and you get 1. The units say the same thing — Hz is 1/s, so (1/s)×(s) = 1.

  10. Write f and T both ways round, with units.

    Answerf = 1/T (hertz, Hz = 1/s) and T = 1/f (seconds, s).
    Watch forMissing units — the question asks for them twice and they are half the marks here. Also watch for f and T being used as though they were the same quantity; the whole reason the question is worded so heavily is that period and wavelength get conflated a page later.

  11. 10 peaks in 5 seconds — how many repeats per second? Frequency? Agrees with the table?

    Answer10 repeats in 5 s is 2 repeats per second, so f = 2 Hz. Yes — it is the 2 Hz row of their own table, where one peak took 0.50 s.

  12. f = 15 Hz: cycles per second, time for one repeat, period.

    Answer15 cycles in one second. One repeat takes 1/15 s ≈ 0.067 s, and that is the period: T ≈ 0.067 s (about 67 ms). f = 15 Hz, T = 0.067 s.
    Watch forAnswers of “15 s” for the period — the reciprocal not taken. And again, units.

  13. Show that v = λ/T is the same as v = fλ.

    AnswerFrom Q10, T = 1/f. Substitute: v = λ/T = λ/(1/f) = fλ. In words: one whole wavelength goes past you in one period, so the speed is one wavelength per period — and since f periods fit in a second, that is f wavelengths per second.
    Watch forStudents who assert the two forms are equal without using T = 1/f. The one substitution is the answer; accept the sentence version if the algebra is also there.

  14. Does changing the frequency change the speed? Predict, then measure.

    AnswerNo. The expected table, with λ measured off the ruler:
    FrequencyWavelengthSpeed = fλ
    1 Hz≈ 3.8 cm≈ 3.8 cm/s
    2 Hz≈ 1.9 cm≈ 3.8 cm/s
    3 Hz≈ 1.3 cm≈ 3.8 cm/s
    Doubling the frequency halved the wavelength and left the speed alone. Conclusion: changing how fast you shake the end changes how the wave is packaged, not how fast it travels.
    Watch forThe prediction is supposed to be written down first and most students predict “yes, faster.” That wrong prediction is the teaching moment — do not let them quietly erase it. Grade the honesty of the prediction, not its correctness. Also expect speeds that scatter by 10–15%; that is ruler-reading error, not a physics result.

  15. Should changing the amplitude change the speed?

    AnswerNo. The amplitude does not appear anywhere in v = fλ, and the simulation confirms it: slide the amplitude from end to end and the wave still arrives at the same rate. A bigger amplitude carries more energy, not more speed.
    Watch for“Bigger waves move faster” — the ocean-wave intuition. It is the same answer they gave in Note 1 about a harder flick, and the slinky already disproved it.

  16. Does the Tension affect the speed?

    AnswerYes. Tighten the string and the wave travels faster; slacken it and the wave slows down. At a fixed frequency this shows up as the wavelength stretching out when the tension goes up (since v = fλ and f is being held fixed by the wheel). This is the punchline of the whole note: the one control that changed the speed is a property of the string itself, not of the shaking.
    Watch forStudents who spot the speed change but attribute it to the wave rather than the medium. Ask them which knob they turned: it was a knob on the string.

  17. Slide Damping up. What does it do, what does it affect, and what does it put back?

    AnswerDamping drains energy out of the wave as it travels, so the crests get lower and lower the further along the string you look, and the whole thing dies away after the wheel stops. It affects the amplitude — not the frequency, not the wavelength, not the speed. What it puts back is friction and air resistance: the real-world energy losses in the actual slinky in Note 1, which is why that pulse faded instead of running forever.
    Watch for“It slows the wave down.” Watching a shrinking wave, that is an easy thing to believe. Have them keep the timer running: peaks still arrive at the same rate.

  18. Send a single pulse. Is it a wave?

    AnswerYes — and this is worth discussing rather than just marking. It is a disturbance that travels through the medium, carrying energy from one end toward the other while every bead returns to its rest position, and it travels at the same speed the oscillating wave did, set by the tension. What it does not have is a repeat: no frequency, no period, and no wavelength, because nothing happens twice. So it is a wave but not a periodic wave — which is precisely why Note 1’s single flick could not be measured and this note had to switch to Oscillate.
    Watch forBoth bare answers. “No, because it has no wavelength” has the right observation attached to the wrong conclusion; “yes” with nothing else has skipped the observation. Full credit needs both halves.

Check yourself — answers

Figures 2 and 3 are reproduced below for marking. In both, the hand is at the left and the wave travels to the right. The rule behind items 1 and 5: a wave moving right means every point takes on, a moment later, the displacement that currently sits just to its left.

Fig 2. Single pulse, knot at A on the leading (right-hand) flank. Items 1–4.
Fig 3. Continuous wave, knot at A on a rising flank between a trough and the next crest. Items 5–7.
#AnswerWhy
1b) higher A sits on the front slope, with the crest to its left. The shape shifts right, so the crest moves toward A and the rope at A rises.
2c) narrower The speed is fixed by the rope. A quicker flick lasts less time, so the pulse it launches occupies less length. Same height, shorter.
3b) taller Moving the hand further is a bigger displacement, which is amplitude. Width and speed are untouched.
4b) faster Tension is a property of the medium, and it is the one thing on this page that does change the speed — Q16 in the simulation.
5c) lower A is on a rising flank, so what lies just to its left is lower (back toward the trough). The wave moves right, so that lower displacement arrives at A.
6b) shifted right The pattern travels; the hand does not. That is what “the wave moves to the right” means.
7c) closer together Higher frequency, same speed, so λ = v/f shrinks. This is Q14 in words.
8b) increase its wavelength λ = v/f with v fixed by the string. (a) and (c) both claim the shaking sets the speed or the height; (d) has the period backwards — lower frequency means a longer period.
Marking noteItems 1 and 5 look like the same question and are not: the flank A sits on is reversed between the two figures, so the answers come out opposite. A student who answers both the same way has guessed rather than applied the rule — worth a conversation, and worth a quick class poll before moving on to Note 3.
