Shaking Faster Does Not Make The Wave Go Faster

SynopsisIn Note 1 you shook a slinky and watched a disturbance walk away from your hand while the ribbon stayed home. What you could not do was measure it. A stretched slinky will not hold still long enough to be measured — the pulse runs to your partner, bounces, comes back, and the whole thing is over in a second or two. So Note 1 stopped at “there is a repeat” and never put a number on it.

This note supplies the numbers, and it turns out there are only three of them. A moving wave repeats in both space and time. Freeze the motion — take a snapshot — and the repeat is laid out along the string as a distance: that is the wavelength, λ. Or stand still at one spot and let the wave come at you, and the repeat arrives over and over as a timing: that is the period, T, and its partner the frequency, f. Add how far the string swings from its rest position — the amplitude — and the wave is described completely. Everything else follows from those three, including the one thing most people guess wrong: how fast it travels.
About your slinkyYou are about to get those numbers out of a simulation instead of a slinky, and it is worth being honest about why. In Note 1 both ends of your slinky were held — you held one, your partner held the other. That is why your pulse ran to the far end, bounced, and came back at you, and why everything was over in a second. That is a practical limitation, and an unavoidable one — not physics. What we actually want is an infinite slinky: one that runs on forever, so a wave leaves your hand and never returns to muddle what you are watching. Nobody can buy one. The simulation can build one: set the far end to No End and the string runs off the screen forever, and set Damping to None so nothing drains the wave’s energy. So do not think of the simulation as a tidier slinky. Think of it as the slinky you could not build — the ideal your real one was trying to be.

Where this is goingQuestion 14 is the one worth carrying out of this note. Shaking faster did not make the wave go faster — it packed the same speed into shorter wavelengths. Tightening the string did. The speed of a wave is set by the medium it travels in, not by whoever is shaking the end. Now think about what that means for a room full of sound: high notes and low notes are shaken at wildly different frequencies, but they all travel through the same air, so they all arrive at the same speed. Strike a chord and it reaches you as a chord — not smeared out with the high notes first. That is the medium doing its job, and it is where we go next.
