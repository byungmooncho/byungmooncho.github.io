Standing Wave and Resonance

Synopsis We explore two remaining wave phenomena, standing wave formation and resonance. These two are related: we say, “A standing wave is formed within an object (or confined space) when a frequency of wave generated by an outside source traveling through the object (or into the confined space) matches the resonance frequency of the object.” See below.

Have you ever experienced feeling the sudden shaking of a car when the car hits a certain speed on a highway, and the vibration comes on only at that specific speed? Here, we say that the car has a natural frequency that comes into resonance at a certain speed when one of many vibrations that undoubtedly exist while driving on the road just happens to match this natural frequency. This natural frequency is called the resonance frequency.

All objects or any confined space have a set of natural frequencies or resonance frequencies, and any time a traveling wave happens to fit or match any one of these resonance frequencies, a standing wave forms within the object or confined space. When a standing wave forms due to resonance, the object will vibrate with a large amplitude.

In a confined space, the resonance will generate the standing wave, which will vibrate the object that created the confined space at that frequency. This is how all musical instruments work. For example, when you pluck a guitar string, the string will vibrate, and because the wave on the string is confined, you will have a standing wave matching the resonance frequency of the string tied at both ends. As this standing wave vibrates the string, the guitar’s body vibrates at the same frequency, and the hollowed-out interior acts as an amplifying chamber, making the tune audible.

When you tie a string at both ends, you confine the wave to zero amplitude at these ends. This is quite obvious: the ends are fixed in space. This limitation, the immovable ends, means that whatever standing waves happen to be present, the ends better have zero amplitudes. Subsequently, you can make standing waves with more cycles (thus higher frequencies) by adding half waves at a time. I emphasize that if you were to remember one thing about a standing wave, this would be it. See below.

Try it on an instrument Rest a finger lightly on a guitar string directly over the 12th fret — do not press it down — and pluck. You hear the note an octave up. You just forced a point that cannot move at the middle of the string, so the one-half-wave shape is no longer allowed and the two-half-wave shape sounds instead: exactly 2f. Over the 7th fret you force a node a third of the way along and get 3f. Figure 2, audible, on an instrument you already own.

What to think about while you watch

In this note, you will watch two videos showing what can happen when you generate standing waves in objects. When you watch the videos, I want you to think about:

  1. The traveling waves generated from a source are the driving waves that excite the object and whose frequencies are the so-called driving frequencies.
  2. The condition for exciting the object’s natural frequency creating resonance.
  3. When the resonance condition is met, what happens to the wave’s amplitude in the object?
  4. What happens when the resonance frequency of the object is inadvertently excited? Is the resonance phenomenon a good thing from a structural engineering perspective?

Video 1 — Tacoma bridge collapse, 1940

Watch · YouTube Tacoma bridge collapse 1940 youtube.com/watch?v=FfNBIRdreHg

  1. Briefly describe what happened.

    The deck of the bridge did not simply sway — it twisted, one side rising as the other fell, in a steady rhythm that kept growing for about an hour in a wind of only around 40 mph, until it tore itself apart. The wind was never strong; the motion was.

  2. Claim (Answer the question, providing hypothesis) — “Why did the bridge collapse?”

    Because the wind fed energy into the bridge at one of the bridge’s own natural frequencies — a twisting mode — so instead of the motion dying away, each cycle arrived in step with the motion already there and the amplitude grew until the steel failed.

  3. Evidence (Describe your observations or cite any relevant data.)

    • The motion had one steady frequency and one shape — a twist with the centre line staying put, exactly like a standing wave with a node down the middle.
    • The amplitude grew over time while the wind speed stayed roughly constant, so the energy was accumulating rather than being supplied in a single gust.
    • The motion carried on long after any one gust had passed.

  4. Reasoning (Providing scientific rationale for your claims, backing them up with your observations. Incorporate i)–iv) in your answer.)

    The wind flowing past the deck shed a regular train of vortices — that is the driving wave, and its rate is the driving frequency (i). The bridge, like every object, had its own set of natural frequencies; the resonance condition is the driving frequency arriving at one of them (ii). When that condition is met, energy is added in phase on every cycle while damping removes less than is added, so the amplitude grows rather than settling (iii). Here that was catastrophic — so from an engineering point of view resonance is a hazard to be designed against (iv), even though it is the very thing that makes a guitar work.

Video 2 — the opera singer and the glass

Watch · YouTube Failure is not an option for this Opera Singer youtube.com/watch?v=uDyri-KPOAc

  1. Briefly describe what happened.

    She finds the glass’s own pitch first, then sings that one note loudly and holds it. In slow motion the rim can be seen flexing — squashing into an oval one way, then the other — with the flexing getting bigger the longer she holds the note, until the glass cracks.

  2. Claim — “How did the opera singer break the glass?”

    By driving the glass at its own resonance frequency, loudly enough and for long enough that the amplitude of the rim’s vibration grew past what the glass could survive.

  3. Evidence

    • She tapped or listened to the glass first to find the pitch — the note is not arbitrary.
    • The slow motion shows a standing wave on the rim, with fixed points that do not move and an oval that alternates direction.
    • The glass breaks after the note is held, not the instant it starts: the amplitude had to accumulate.

  4. Reasoning (Incorporate i)–iv) in your answer.)

    Her voice is the driving wave and the pitch she sings is the driving frequency (i). Matching it to the glass’s natural frequency is the resonance condition (ii), and once met the rim’s amplitude grows cycle after cycle (iii). Same physics as the bridge, different scale: one broke a glass, one destroyed a bridge (iv). A thin, low-damping glass works best because less energy leaks away between cycles, so more of it accumulates.
