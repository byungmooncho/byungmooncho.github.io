Centripetal Force and Gravitation: Example

SynopsisAn optional in-class assignment, and a single question: confirm that the Moon's orbital period is about a month.

Nothing new is needed.  Gravity is the only force on the Moon, its orbit is very nearly circular, and a circular orbit is a body in uniform circular motion — so the gravitational force is the centripetal force.  Set the two expressions equal, cancel what cancels, and the orbital speed drops out.  Divide the circumference by it and you have the period.

The thing worth watching is what cancels.  The Moon's own mass appears on both sides and goes, which is why the answer does not depend on it — and that is the same cancellation that makes every satellite at a given radius orbit at the same speed, whatever it weighs.
Before you start — predict

Two satellites orbit the Earth at the same altitude, one ten times as massive as the other.  Write one sentence saying which of them takes longer to go round.

Performance expectations

Recognise a circular orbit as uniform circular motion with gravity supplying the centripetal force.  Set FG = Fc and solve for the orbital speed.  Convert an orbital speed to an orbital period.  State how the period depends on the central mass and on the orbital radius.

Set the two forces equal

The Moon goes round the Earth on a very nearly circular path.  One force acts on it, and it points at the Earth — which is exactly where a centripetal force has to point.  So the two are the same force called two names, and that is the whole method.

The Moon's own mass is on both sides of the first line and gone by the second.  That cancellation is the reason a heavy satellite and a light one at the same radius keep exactly the same company.

  1. Write the gravitational force on the Moon and the centripetal force on the Moon, set them equal, and cancel everything that cancels.  What is left?
  2. One of the four given quantities turns out not to be needed.  Which one, and why is that worth noticing rather than annoying?
  3. Work out the orbital speed vt, in m/s, and fill it into the table.
i) FG = GMEMm/r² and Fc = Mmvt²/r, and they are the same force, so
    GMEMm/r² = Mmvt²/r.  Mm cancels and so does one power of r, leaving vt = √(GME/rEM).
ii) The Moon's own mass.  It is worth noticing because it is a result, not an accident: the orbiting body's mass always cancels, so every object at a given radius round a given planet orbits at the same speed.  That is why an astronaut outside the station keeps pace with it.— it is the same cancellation that makes everything fall at the same rate near the ground, written out at orbital distance
iii) GME = (6.674 × 10−11)(5.972 × 1024) = 3.986 × 1014.  Divide by rEM = 3.844 × 108: 1.037 × 106.  Take the root: vt = 1018 m/s, about 1.0 km/s.

From the speed to the period

One orbit is one circumference at that speed.  That is the entire second half, and the only thing that can go wrong is the arithmetic of converting seconds into something you can recognise.

  1. How far does the Moon travel in one orbit, in metres?  Then find the period T, in seconds, and fill it into the table.
  2. Convert that to days.  Is it about a month?
  3. For the brownie point.  Without putting any numbers in, combine your two results into a single expression for T in terms of G, ME and r.  How does the period depend on the mass of the Earth, and how does it depend on the orbital radius?
i) One orbit is 2πr = 2π(3.844 × 108) = 2.415 × 109 m.  At 1018 m/s that takes T = 2.415 × 109/1018 = 2.37 × 106 s.
ii) 2.372 × 106 s ÷ 86 400 s/day = 27.5 days.  Yes — that is the sidereal month, the Moon's period against the fixed stars.  The familiar 29.5-day cycle of the phases is longer because the Earth has moved a twelfth of the way round the Sun meanwhile, so the Moon has to go a little further to line up again.— both numbers are right; they are answers to two different questions, and this calculation answers the first
iii) Substitute vt = √(GME/r) into T = 2πr/vt:
    T = 2πr3/2/√(GME),  or squared,  T² = 4π²r³/(GME).
    So T goes as 1/√ME — a heavier planet pulls harder, so everything round it hurries — and as r3/2.  Double the radius and the period goes up by 23/2 = 2.83, not by 2.  That is Kepler's third law, which Note 2 got the same way, and it is one of the few places in this course where you derive in three lines something that took someone twenty years of data.

Looking ahead

The same two lines answer a surprising number of questions.  Run them backwards and the Moon's orbit weighs the Earth; put in T = 24 h and they give you the altitude a communications satellite has to sit at; swap the Earth for the Sun and they give you the Earth's year.  Note 3 leaves orbits behind and turns to bodies that spin rather than circle.

Back to your prediction

Neither — they take exactly the same time.  The orbiting body's mass is on both sides of GMEM/r² = Mv²/r and cancels, so nothing about the satellite itself survives into the answer.  Only the Earth's mass and the orbital radius do.  If you reasoned “heavier means more gravitational pull, so faster”, you were half right: the pull is ten times bigger, and so is the inertia it has to overcome.
