Universal Gravitation and Weight Elsewhere

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea The force that drops an apple is the force that holds the Moon — that is what universal means, and it was a genuinely radical claim. One equation covers both, with a constant so small that measuring it took a torsion fibre, a mirror and a draught-free room. Once you have it, two things follow: you can weigh the Earth without leaving it, and you can say what a bathroom scale would read on Mars.
You already know this Weight as a force from Module 3, Fg = mg, and Newton's third law. This page replaces the constant g with something that depends on where you are standing — and then explains what sets it.

Newton's law of universal gravitation

Every pair of masses attracts, with a force that grows with both masses and falls off with the square of the separation.

The law, and its constant
FG = G m1m2r2
m1, m2— the two masses, in kg
r— the distance between them, centre to centre. Trevor's note: the letter is r rather than d because for orbits this separation is the orbital radius.
G= 6.674 × 10−11 N·m2/kg2 — the universal gravitational constant

Look at the size of G. Two 1 kg masses a metre apart attract with about 7 × 10−11 N, which is why you have never noticed the gravitational pull of a person standing next to you. Gravity only becomes obvious when one of the masses is planetary.

The Cavendish experiment

A constant that small cannot be measured by weighing things. Cavendish hung a light rod with two small masses from a thin fibre, brought two large masses up beside them, and measured the tiny twist.

Weighing the Earth from the ground
mg = G m mEr2  ⇒  g = G mEr2  ⇒  mE = g r2G

Your own mass cancels, which is the point: g is a property of the Earth, not of you. Put in g = 9.81 m/s2 and rE = 6.37 × 106 m and the Earth comes out at 5.97 × 1024 kg. Cavendish's measurement of G is what made that — and the masses of the other planets and the Sun — possible at all.

Newton and orbits

Newton used universal gravitation to explain what Kepler had only observed, and his own illustration is still the clearest argument ever made for why an orbit is not a special kind of motion. Fire a cannonball horizontally from a mountain-top. Fire it harder and it lands further away. Fire it hard enough and the ground curves away from it as fast as it falls.

Weight on different planets

Mass is the same everywhere; weight is not. The local acceleration due to gravity gets its own symbol, ag, so that g can keep meaning the value on Earth.

Fg = m ag
Fg—the force of gravity, the thing a scale reads
g—the acceleration due to gravity on Earth, 9.80 m/s2
ag—the acceleration due to gravity anywhere — the generic version

The Mars row differs from the slide, which gives 116 lb. A force of 256 N is 256 ÷ 4.45 = 57.5 lb; 116 is what you get from 256 ÷ 2.2, using the kilogram-to-pound factor on a quantity already in newtons. The Earth and Moon rows on the slide are both right, which is the tell. You can also check it without any conversion at all: Mars pulls 3.76/9.80 as hard as Earth, and 150 lb × 0.384 = 58 lb.

Find the mass first Trevor's standing advice for this whole family of problems. Mass is the constant — it is the same on Mars as in the lab — so convert whatever you are given into a mass, then multiply by the local ag. A useful bridge: 1.0 kg weighs 2.2 lb on Earth, and 1 lb = 4.45 N.

Worked example: the lunar rover

The Apollo lunar rover weighed 462 pounds on Earth. What did it weigh on the Moon, where the acceleration due to gravity is about one sixth of ours?

What sets the local acceleration

Nothing on the table above is arbitrary. Each value follows from that body's mass and radius, by exactly the same substitution used to weigh the Earth.

FG = G mpmor2 = moag   ⇒   ag = G mpr2

For something standing on the surface, r is the radius of the planet — the distance from its centre. Two bodies of the same mass but different size have different surface gravity, which is the same statement as saying their densities differ: ρ = m/V = m ÷ (4/3)πr3. Reduce the mass at fixed radius and the density falls; increase the radius at fixed mass and it falls too.

Review

The law— FG = G m1m2/r2, describing how mass and separation set the force between any two objects.
Equal and opposite— the force on each object is the same size and points at the centre of the other.
Measure centre to centre— and for an object above a surface, add the body's radius to the altitude.
Local gravity— ag = Gmp/r2 — set by the body's mass and radius, nothing else.
Weight elsewhere— Fg = m ag. Find the mass first; it is the quantity that does not change.
Orbit— gravity does not switch off up there — it is what supplies the centripetal force.
