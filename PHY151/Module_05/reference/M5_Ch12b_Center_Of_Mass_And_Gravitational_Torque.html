Centre of Mass and Gravitational Torque

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Every free-body diagram you have drawn since Module 3 has quietly shrunk an object to a point. That point is the centre of mass — the place where you may pretend all the mass sits. Once torques are in play, the pretence has to be justified, and the payoff is that an object's whole weight can be treated as one force acting at one point, giving it one lever arm and one gravitational torque.
You already know this Free-body diagrams from Module 3, where every object became a dot, and τ = rF sin θ from Ch10c. This page names the dot and shows what it is for.

Why the dot needs justifying now

A force applied to an object has to be applied somewhere. For ΣF = 0 that never mattered: forces add the same way wherever they act. For Στ = 0 it matters completely, because the lever arm is the whole question.

Where the centre of mass is

Uniform solid— at the geometric centre. A uniform plank's centre of mass is at its midpoint; a uniform disk's is at the axle. For most of this course you may assume this.
Odd shapes— find it by balancing the object: the point it balances on is the point its weight acts through.
A set of masses— use the formula below — a weighted average of their positions.
The centre of mass of a collection of masses
xcg = Σ ximiΣ mi = x1m1 + x2m2 + x3m3 + …m1 + m2 + m3 + …

Read it as an average of positions in which each position is counted as many times as it has kilograms. A real object is done this way in all three dimensions, giving xcg, ycg and zcg; everything on this page is one-dimensional, which is the only case Module 5 needs.

Trevor's first use of the formula is a sanity check: five identical 1.0-kg blocks in a row at 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5 and 4.5 m. The numerator is (0.5 + 1.5 + 2.5 + 3.5 + 4.5)(1.0 kg) = 12.5 kg·m and the denominator 5.0 kg, giving 2.5 m — dead centre, as it must be when every mass is the same.

The answer does not depend on where you put the origin

Two balls, 1.0 kg and 2.0 kg, joined by a massless rod 1.00 m long. Measure from either end and the formula puts the centre of mass in the same physical place.

Check yourself

Does the centre of mass have to lie inside the object?

No. For a doughnut it is in the hole; for a boomerang it is off in the air beside it; for the two balls above it is on the rod between them, in neither ball. The centre of mass is a location, not a piece of the object.

Gravitational torque

Gravity is a force like any other, so it produces a torque like any other — and because the whole weight may be taken to act at the centre of mass, there is only one arm to measure: from the axis to that point.

A board overhanging a pivot
A uniform board of length L and weight Fg, pivoted at ¾L from the left end. Its centre of mass is at L/2, so the arm is ¾L − ½L = ¼L:
Στ = rF sin θ = (¼L)(Fg) sin 90° = ¼LFg

One force, one arm, one line of arithmetic. That is the whole value of the centre of mass: without it you would have to add up the torque contributed by every gram of the board separately.

Trevor then does exactly that harder thing on purpose, to show it comes out the same. Treat the board as two pieces that meet at the pivot — a long left piece and a short right piece — and work out each one's weight and arm.

Don't actually do it the hard way Trevor's own note on the slide is “if we wanted to make this needlessly complicated (don't do this)”. The split calculation is there as a proof, not as a method. Its real lesson is that the centre of mass is not an approximation you are getting away with — it gives the exact answer.

Review

Centre of mass— the point at which all of an object's mass may be taken to be concentrated. Also called the centre of gravity.
Uniform object— its centre of mass is at its geometric centre.
Several masses— xcg = Σximi / Σmi — a mass-weighted average of positions.
Choice of origin— changes the number, not the place. Measure from wherever is convenient.
Gravitational torque— the whole weight acting at the centre of mass, with one arm: from the axis to that point.
Not an approximation— splitting the object into pieces and adding their torques gives exactly the same result.
