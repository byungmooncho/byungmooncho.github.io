Rotational Energy and Rolling Motion

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea A spinning object carries kinetic energy even when its centre is going nowhere, and the expression for it is the rotational twin of the one you already use: Krot = ½Iω2. An object that rolls has both kinds at once, locked together by v = ωr. Put that into conservation of energy and you can race a hoop against a disk down a ramp and know the winner before they start.
You already know this Conservation of energy from Module 4 — Ei = Ef when no friction removes energy — and from this module, v = ωr (Ch10a), the moment of inertia and its table of shapes (Ch10b), and τ = Iα (Ch10d). Nothing here is a new law; it is the old energy law with one more term.

Where ½Iω2 comes from

Trevor does not simply assert the formula — he does work on a disk and watches what the work turns into. Push tangentially on a stationary disk with a constant force while a point on the rim travels an arc length Δs. The force and the displacement are always in the same direction, so the angle in W = Fd cos φ is zero.

Trevor's chain, start to finish
W = Fd cos φ = FΔs cos 0° = FΔs
W = rFΔs 1r = τ Δsr = τΔθ = IαΔθ
ω2 = ω02 + 2αΔθ, and ω0 = 0, so αΔθ = ½ω2
W = ½Iω2  ⇒  Krot = ½Iω2

The step Trevor labels “sneak in a factor of 1” is the whole trick: multiplying by r/r changes nothing, but it regroups rF into a torque and Δs/r into an angle. Compare the result with K = ½mv2: same shape, with I for m and ω for v.

Keeping track of all the energy

A football dropping while it spins has three stores at once, and they are simply added.

The term students drop Up to now, “conservation of energy” has meant mgh + ½mv2. From here on every object that turns carries ½Iω2 as well. A ball that slides down a ramp arrives faster than one that rolls, because the rolling one has had to spend part of the same mgh on spinning itself up.

Rolling without slipping

Deck 10e adds the constraint that makes rolling problems tractable. Roll a wheel along the ground and its motion is a translation of the centre plus a rotation about that centre. Add the two velocity fields and the numbers at top and bottom are striking.

Check yourself

If the bottom of a rolling tyre is momentarily at rest, why does the tread wear out?

Because “momentarily at rest” applies only to the one point touching the road at that instant, and only if the tyre is not slipping. A new point arrives at the road every instant, each one pressed hard against it, and real tyres deform and scuff rather than touching at a mathematical point. The physics claim is about velocity, not about contact being gentle.

One useful consequence: because the contact point is not sliding, the friction at the contact is static friction, not kinetic. That is why Trevor's review slide insists on μs for a tyre that is rolling and μk only once it slips.

Rolling on a level surface

Write conservation of energy for a ball rolling along level ground with no air resistance. The height does not change, so mgh appears identically on both sides and cancels:

Level surface, no losses
½mvi2 + ½I vi2R2 = ½mvf2 + ½I vf2R2  ⇒  vf = vi

Trevor asks the class whether the object slows down or speeds up, and the equation answers: neither. A rolling object on level ground keeps its speed. Anything that actually slows it — rolling resistance, air drag, a bearing — is a loss term the equation does not contain, which is the point of asking “what would cause this?”

Worked example: a rolling solid ball

A solid ball rolls without slipping on a level surface at 6.0 m/s. Its mass is 2.0 kg and its radius 0.25 m. Find (a) its translational kinetic energy, (b) its angular speed, and (c) its rotational kinetic energy.

Three parts, in order
(a)  K = ½mv2 = ½(2.0 kg)(6.0 m/s)2 = 36 J
(b)  rolling without slipping ⇒ v = ωr, so ω = vr = 6.0 m/s0.25 m = 24 rad/s
(c)  Krot = ½Iω2 = ½25MR2 v2R2 = 15Mv2 = 15(2.0)(6.0)2 = 14 J

Part (c) is worth watching twice. Substituting ω = v/R makes the R2 cancel, and the radius disappears from the answer altogether. For a rolling solid sphere the rotational share is always Krot/K = 2/5 of the translational part, whatever its size — here 14 J against 36 J, so the ball carries 50 J in all and about 29% of it is spin.

Rolling down an incline

Now the useful case. A ball starts from rest at height hi and rolls to the bottom. Starting from rest kills both kinetic terms on the left; ending at the bottom kills the potential term on the right.

From Ei = Ef to a formula you can use
mghi + 0 + 0 = 0 + ½mvf2 + ½I vf2R2
mghi = ½mvf2  (1 + ImR2)
vf = √ 2ghi / (1 + ImR2)

The second line is the one to remember. Trevor gets there by multiplying the rotational term by m/m — the same “factor of one” move as before — which turns the clutter into the single dimensionless group I/mR2. That group, and nothing else about the object, decides how the energy is split.

Shape beats mass and size

Read I/mR2 off the moment-of-inertia table from Ch10b and the mass and radius both cancel. What is left is a pure number that depends only on how the mass is distributed.

Worked example: the hoop and the disk race

A disk and a hoop of different materials have the same mass and the same outside diameter. Both start from rest and roll without slipping down the same incline. Which one wins?

 Hoop (ring)Disk
moment of inertia MR2 ½MR2
I/mR2 1½
energy equation ghi = ½vf2(1 + 1) ghi = ½vf2(1 + ½)
result vf2 = ghi vf2 = 4⁄3 ghi
final speed 1.00 √(ghi) 1.15 √(ghi)

The disk arrives about 15% faster — and because the same reasoning applies at every height on the way down, it is ahead the whole way, not only at the finish. Note what never entered the calculation: the mass, the radius, the material, and the angle of the ramp.

A clean demonstration This is the race worth doing in front of a class with real objects, because the result is so counter-intuitive and so robust. Any solid sphere beats any disk, which beats any hoop, no matter how heavy or how large. If a sphere ever loses, it was slipping.

Worked example: a dot on a bicycle tyre

A bicycle with 0.80-m-diameter tyres coasts at a constant 5.6 m/s. A small dot is painted on the tread of the rear tyre. Find (a) the angular speed of the tyres, (b) the linear speed of the dot at the top of the tyre, and (c) its linear speed at the bottom.

Diameter first — the classic slip
d = 0.80 m ⇒ r = 0.40 m
(a)  ω = vcmr = 5.6 m/s0.40 m = 14 rad/s
(b)  vtop = 2vcm = 2(5.6 m/s) = 11.2 m/s
(c)  vbot = 0.0 m/s

Parts (b) and (c) need no arithmetic at all once you have the previous figure: at the top, translation and rotation both carry the dot forwards; at the bottom they oppose and cancel exactly. The only place to go wrong is the first line — the problem gives a diameter, and v = ωr wants a radius.

Check yourself

The dot is at the top for an instant and moving at 11.2 m/s — twice the bicycle's speed. Does that break any speed limit on the energy of the wheel?

No. Points on the wheel range from 0 at the contact to 2vcm at the top, and the average works out to vcm. The wheel's total kinetic energy is ½mvcm2 + ½Iω2, which is finite and modest — the fast points are balanced by the slow ones.

When it does slip

Everything above rests on v = ωr. Break that link and the translational and rotational speeds become independent, which makes the problem much harder — and much more interesting.

Billiards— striking the cue ball off-centre gives it spin unrelated to its forward speed; players call it putting “English” on the ball.
Bowling— a ball released with side spin slides at first, and the friction acting on the slipping surface curves its path before it settles into rolling.
A car spinning out— the tyres have broken static friction and are slipping; the wheel's rotation is no longer tied to the car's motion over the ground.

In each case the friction is kinetic, energy is lost to the surface, and the clean conservation equations of this page do not apply until the object stops slipping and begins to roll.

Review

Rotational KE— Krot = ½Iω2 — the twin of ½mv2, with I for mass and ω for speed.
Total energy— E = mgh + ½mv2 + ½Iω2. Anything that spins needs all three.
Rolling condition— vcm = ωr. Contact point at rest, top of the object at 2vcm, static friction at the contact.
Down a ramp— mghi = ½mvf2(1 + I/mR2).
Who wins— the smallest I/mR2: sphere (0.40) > disk (0.50) > hoop (1). Mass and radius do not matter.
Slipping— breaks v = ωr, switches the friction to kinetic, and removes energy.
