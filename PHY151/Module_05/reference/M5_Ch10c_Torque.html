Torque

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea A force makes something accelerate; a torque makes something turn. How much turning effect a force has depends on three things: how hard you push, where you push, and which way you push — and the one equation τ = rF sin θ carries all three. Everything else on this page is a way of reading that equation.
You already know this Resolving a force into components from Module 2 — that is the whole of sin θ here — and the idea of an axis of rotation from Ch10a. If you have ever pushed on a door near its hinge and felt it barely move, you already have the physics; this page gives it a number.

Three things, one equation

Trevor states it as a list before he states it as a formula. Torque depends on the force F, on the distance r from the axis to the point where the force is applied, and on the direction θ in which the force points.

Torque
τ = rF sin θ    (the vector form is τ = r × F, a cross product)
τ— torque. The Greek letter tau; Trevor's mnemonic is that it rhymes with cow, not with saw.
r— distance from the axis to the point where the force acts.
F— the applied force.
θ— the angle between r and F.

Trevor's aside about the angle is worth keeping: it does not matter whether you measure the angle between the force and the lever arm or between the force and the lever arm extended, because the two are supplementary and sin θ = sin(180° − θ). Any angle you can reasonably call “the angle” here gives the same sine.

Units: newton-metres, and never joules

Multiply metres by newtons and the unit is N·m. A newton-metre is also a joule — but torque is never written in joules. Trevor is emphatic about this, and the reason is that the two quantities are built differently: work is a force times a distance along the force, while torque is a force times a distance perpendicular to it. Writing 21.7 J for a torque invites exactly the confusion the separate name is there to prevent.

In the shop The imperial unit is the foot-pound (ft·lb), and it is the one printed on a torque wrench. If a wheel nut calls for 100 ft·lb, that is 136 N·m — the same physical demand, written in the other system.

Two ways to read the same equation

Trevor devotes a whole slide to the fact that the same formula is taught two different ways, and that neither is more correct than the other. The brackets go in different places, and the picture you draw changes to match.

Which to use If the problem hands you a force at an angle to a bar, resolving it (left panel) is usually quicker. If it hands you a line of action and a geometry — a rope over a pulley, a weight hanging off a beam — the moment arm (right panel) is usually quicker. r⊥ is often called the perpendicular moment arm, or just the moment arm.

Adding torques, with signs

Torques about the same axis add, and because a torque can turn an object either way, they add with signs. Trevor's convention is the standard one.

Before you compute, look Trevor's instruction on this slide is to think about which way each torque would make the object spin, one force at a time, before writing anything down. Decide the signs from the picture; then the algebra has nothing left to get wrong.

Worked example: pushing a door at an angle

A force of 50.0 N is applied at 60.0° to a door, 50.0 cm from the hinge. What is the torque?

Straight substitution
r = 50.0 cm = 0.500 m,  F = 50.0 N,  θ = 60.0°
τ = rF sin θ = (0.500 m)(50.0 N) sin 60.0° = (0.500)(50.0)√32 = 21.7 N·m

Two habits worth copying from the slide. The centimetres are converted to metres in the given list, before anything is multiplied. And sin 60° is carried as √3/2 rather than as a rounded decimal, so only the final answer is rounded — here to three significant figures, matching the data.

Worked example: the same equation, run backwards

How far from the hinge must you push, with 50.0 N perpendicular to the door, to produce a torque of 25.0 N·m?

Solve for r instead
θ = 90.0° (perpendicular), so sin θ = 1
r = τF sin θ = 25.0 N·m(50.0 N)(1) = 0.500 m

The word perpendicular in the question is doing real work: it is what sets θ = 90° and makes sin θ = 1. Read it as data, not as scene-setting.

Check yourself

Push on a door exactly along its surface, straight towards the hinge. What is the torque?

Zero. The angle between r and F is 0° (or 180°), sin of which is 0. In the left-hand reading there is no perpendicular component to resolve out; in the right-hand reading the line of action passes straight through the axis, so the moment arm is zero. Both readings agree, which is the point of the figure above.

Review

Torque— τ = rF sin θ; vector form τ = r × F.
Depends on— how hard (F), how far out (r), and which way (θ).
Units— N·m, or ft·lb in imperial. Never joules.
Two readings— rF⊥ (resolve the force) and r⊥F (find the moment arm) — equivalent.
Signs— counterclockwise positive, clockwise negative; then Στ is an ordinary sum.
Zero torque— when the force points at the axis, or acts at the axis (r = 0).
