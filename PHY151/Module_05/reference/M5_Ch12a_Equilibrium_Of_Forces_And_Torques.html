Equilibrium of Forces and Torques

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea An object is in equilibrium when two things are true at once: the net force on it is zero, and the net torque on it is zero. The first condition you have had since Module 3. The second is new, and it is what stops a beam from rotating even when the forces already balance. Two conditions means two equations, which is exactly what you need to find two unknown support forces.
You already know this ΣF = ma and free-body diagrams from Module 3, and torque — τ = rF sin θ, with counterclockwise positive — from Ch10c. This page is just those two put side by side.

The two conditions

Equilibrium
1.  ΣF = 0 = F1 + F2 + F3 + …   (so a = 0)
2.  Στ = 0 = τ1 + τ2 + τ3 + …   (so α = 0)

Both are statements that nothing is accelerating — the first that the object is not picking up linear speed, the second that it is not picking up spin. An object can satisfy one and fail the other: push the two ends of a bar in opposite directions and the forces cancel perfectly while the bar spins up.

Trevor splits the two conditions across two lectures, and deals with the first on its own before combining them. From ΣF = ma, the first condition is the same thing as a = 0 — and a = 0 leaves two possibilities.

A correction to the slides Trevor's own dynamic-equilibrium panels are labelled “v = 0 m/s” on three slides (09a slides 3, 4 and 5), copied through from the static panel beside them. It should read v ≠ 0: an object in dynamic equilibrium is moving, at constant velocity. The figure above has it corrected.

Rows 1 and 2 both have v = 0, and only one of them is in equilibrium. Rows 3 and 5 both have v ≠ 0, and only one of them is. What does that tell you?

That speed is irrelevant. A ball at the very top of its flight is momentarily motionless and still accelerating downwards at g; a puck sliding on frictionless ice is moving fast and not accelerating at all. Equilibrium is a statement about a, so it is a statement about forces, not about motion.

Where the forces act starts to matter

Once the second condition is in play, a free-body diagram is no longer enough on its own. You need to know not only what the forces are but where on the object each one acts, because that is what sets each torque.

Choosing the pivot Trevor's rule has two halves. First, any point works — the object is not rotating about anything, so the net torque is zero about every axis you could pick. Second, some choices are far less work: put the axis where an unknown force acts and its moment arm is zero, so that unknown drops straight out of the equation. A hinge, a support, or a pivot the object would actually turn about are the usual good picks.

Worked example: the force on each support

A uniform beam 4.0 m long and weighing 200 N rests on supports at its two ends. A 300-N crate sits 1.0 m from the left support. Find the force each support exerts.

Torques first, then forces
Pivot at the right support, so F2 has no arm:
F1(4.0 m) = (300 N)(3.0 m) + (200 N)(2.0 m) = 1300 N·m
F1 = 325 N
ΣF = 0:  F1 + F2 = 300 N + 200 N = 500 N  ⇒  F2 = 500 − 325 = 175 N

Two conditions, two equations, two unknowns — and worth checking by re-running the torque equation about the left support instead: F2(4.0) = (300)(1.0) + (200)(2.0) = 700, giving F2 = 175 N again. The answer cannot depend on where you put the axis, so that is a free check on your arithmetic.

Worked example: how far out can you walk?

A uniform plank 4.0 m long and weighing 250 N rests horizontally on two supports, with 1.0 m of it overhanging the right support. How far out along the overhang can a person weighing 500 N walk before the plank begins to tip?

Take the pivot where it is about to turn
Στ = 0 about the right support. Both forces act perpendicular to the plank, so sin θ = 1, and Fs1 and Fs2 contribute nothing — the first because it is zero, the second because its arm is zero:
rpFplank = riFperson  ⇒  ri = rpFplankFperson = (1.0 m)250 N500 N = 0.50 m

Half a metre onto a one-metre overhang. The plank's own weight is the only thing holding the far end down, and it is only half the person's weight, so it can only balance them half as far out. Notice too how little of the problem mattered: the 4.0 m length entered only through the position of the centre of gravity, and the left support's position never entered at all.

“Hidden knowns” Trevor's closing warning. Equilibrium problems often hide a number inside a phrase rather than stating it: just begins to tip means one support force is zero; on the verge of slipping means the friction is at its maximum μsN; uniform means the weight acts at the geometric centre. Reading the sentence carefully is part of the physics.

Review

Condition 1— ΣF = 0, so a = 0.
Condition 2— Στ = 0, so α = 0. Both must hold.
Static— in equilibrium and at rest.
Dynamic— in equilibrium while moving — constant velocity, zero acceleration.
The pivot— any point works; choose one where an unknown acts so its arm is zero.
Method— two conditions give two equations; solve for two unknowns. Check by moving the pivot.
Hidden knowns— “about to tip”, “uniform”, “on the verge of slipping” each supply a number.
