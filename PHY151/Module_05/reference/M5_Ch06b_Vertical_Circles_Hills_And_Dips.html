Vertical Circles, Hills and Dips

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Turn the circle on its side and gravity stops being a bystander. At the top of a loop it helps pull you round; at the bottom it fights the thing that is pulling you round. The physics does not change — Fc = mv2/r still holds — but the other force has to make up the difference, and it is the one you feel. That is why you float over a hill crest and are pressed into your seat at the bottom of a dip, and it is why a roller-coaster loop has a minimum speed.
You already know this From Ch06a: the centripetal force is the net force in the centre-seeking direction, never an extra arrow on the diagram. Everything here is that one rule applied four times, with a sign convention doing the bookkeeping.

Start with the question

Trevor's question

A ball tied to a string is moving in a vertical circle at a constant speed. How do the tensions at the top and the bottom of the path compare?

1. The tension at the top is larger.  ·  2. The tension at the bottom is larger.  ·  3. The tensions are the same.

2 — the tension at the bottom is larger, and the rest of this page is why. Trevor's own footnote is worth keeping: constant speed in a vertical circle is tough, but not impossible, to produce.

The tension changes all the way round

Assume the speed really is constant. Then ac = v2/r is constant too, so the net centre-seeking force is the same at every point on the circle. But gravity always points down, whatever the ball is doing — so the tension has to change from place to place to keep that net force constant.

In a roller coaster the normal force from the track does exactly what the tension does here. Trevor's summary is the one students remember: this is why you feel as though you will fall out at the top, and feel crushed at the bottom.

Let the sign convention do the bookkeeping

Pick + to mean towards the centre and − to mean away from it. The convention changes meaning as the ball goes round — at the top, “towards the centre” is downwards; at the bottom it is upwards — and that single fact produces both results.

The same equation, written twice
Top of the circleBottom of the circle
+Fg + +FT = mac −Fg + +FT = mac
FT = mac − Fg FT = mac + Fg

Gravity is helping at the top, so the string has less to do; it is opposing at the bottom, so the string has to supply the centripetal force and hold the weight up as well. Subtracting one line from the other puts a number on the check question: FT,bot − FT,top = 2Fg = 2mg, whatever the speed is.

The minimum speed at the top

Trevor's question: what is the minimum speed for a person not to fall out of a roller coaster car at the top of a loop, with no safety harness? Replace the string with the track, so the tension becomes a normal force — and a track can only push, never pull.

Setting the normal force to zero
+Fg + +FN = m v2r   with FN = 0  ⇒   mg = m v2r
g = v2r   ⇒   vmin = √g r

The mass cancels, which is the surprise: the minimum speed is the same for everybody in the car. And the condition is not “gravity is balanced” — it is gravity alone supplies the whole centripetal force. Below that speed the circle the rider would need is bigger than the track, and they leave it.

“Somewhat” circular motion

Everything so far assumed a full circle at constant speed. Real roads do neither: a car goes over a crest or through a dip along part of a circular path, usually with a changing speed. Trevor's point is that none of that matters for the instant you care about. Fit a circle to the curve at that point — its radius is the r in v2/r — and the same mathematics applies.

Going over a hill

At the crest the centre of the fitted circle is below the road, so “towards the centre” means downwards. The weight points that way and the normal force points the other way.

At the crest
m v2r = −FN + Fg   ⇒   FN = Fg − m v2r

Trevor asks it as a question — why is FN smaller than Fg? — and the equation answers it: part of your weight is being spent on turning you downwards along the curve, so less of it is left pressing you into the seat. The seat pushes back with less force, and that is the quantity your body reads as “how heavy am I”. Hence the lift in your stomach over a slight rise. Faster, or a tighter crest, and the feeling is stronger.

Making a jump

Push that far enough and FN reaches zero: the road stops touching the car.

FN = Fg − m v2r = 0   ⇒   mg = m v2r   ⇒   v = √g r
The same equation twice v = √g r is both the minimum speed to stay on the track at the top of a loop and the maximum speed to stay on the road over a crest. One equation, two readings, because the physical condition is identical: the surface force has gone to zero and gravity alone is bending the path. Which side of it you want to be on is what differs.

Going down into a dip

In a dip the centre of the fitted circle is above the road, so the sign convention flips and so does the answer.

At the bottom of the dip
m v2r = FN − Fg   ⇒   FN = Fg + m v2r

The road now has two jobs: hold the weight and supply the centripetal force. So it pushes harder than your weight, and you read the extra as being pressed into the seat. There is no upper limit on this one — nothing goes to zero — which is why a dip is a structural problem rather than a staying-on-the-road problem.

Review

SituationCentre is…Surface or string forceWhat you feel
Top of a vertical circlebelow you F = mac − Fg light; may fall out
Bottom of a vertical circleabove you F = mac + Fg crushed
Crest of a hillbelow you FN = Fg − mv2/r light; leaves the road at v = √gr
Bottom of a dipabove you FN = Fg + mv2/r heavy
The one rule— choose + towards the centre, add the real forces with their signs, and set the sum equal to mv2/r. Everything in the table above is that sentence, four times.
Losing contact— FN = 0 gives v = √g r — a minimum at the top of a loop, a maximum over a crest. Independent of mass.
Partial circles— fit a circle to the curve at the point of interest and use its radius. The speed may be changing; the instantaneous analysis is unaffected.
