Orbits, Weightlessness and Artificial Gravity

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Astronauts float, so there must be no gravity up there. It sounds like a deduction and it is a myth — Ch13a already showed that at Space Station altitude gravity is still about 88% of what it is on the ground. The resolution is that floating has nothing to do with gravity being absent. It has to do with what a bathroom scale actually measures, and what happens to that reading when the floor falls away.
You already know this From Ch13a: gravity at 400 km is barely reduced. From Ch06a: the centripetal force is whatever real force points at the centre. From Ch06c: a rotating frame invents an outward force for the observer inside it. This page is those three facts pointed at one misconception.
Trevor's opening question

The reason an astronaut in orbit around the Earth feels “weightless” is…

  1. the astronaut is too far away from Earth to feel its gravity
  2. the astronaut is falling
  3. a psychological effect associated with motion
  4. the astronaut's acceleration is zero
  5. the gravity from the Moon and the Earth cancel each other out
2 — the astronaut is falling. Note that 4 is also wrong in an interesting way: the astronaut's acceleration is emphatically not zero. It is g, directed at the centre of the Earth, which is exactly what keeps them in orbit.

What a scale actually measures

Stand on a bathroom scale on a level floor. You are not accelerating, so the forces balance, and the number you read is the size of the force between you and the scale — the normal force.

What if the floor accelerates?

Now the two forces are no longer forced to be equal, and the number on the scale moves even though your mass and the Earth's pull have not changed at all. Take + towards the ceiling.

Where the zero comes from
+FN + −Fg = m(±a)  ⇒  FN = Fg + m(±a)
in free fall, a = ag downwards:    FN = Fg − mag = mag − mag = 0

Trevor's conclusion, and the whole point of the deck: that is “weightlessness”. Not the absence of gravity — the absence of contact. Gravity is still pulling with its full strength; there is simply nothing pushing back, because the floor is falling at exactly the same rate you are.

The honest name for it What the astronaut experiences is better called free fall than weightlessness. The word “weightless” invites the very mistake the deck is about, because it sounds like a statement about gravity when it is a statement about the scale.

An orbit is falling — and missing

Trevor reaches for Newton's own argument here: a projectile fired horizontally fast enough will miss the ground and go into orbit. That figure is on Ch13a and is not repeated. What matters for this page is the consequence: everything in orbit is in free fall, so everything in orbit reads zero on a scale, while gravity does all the work of bending the path.

How fast is that?

Set the gravitational force equal to the centripetal force it has to supply, and the mass cancels.

Orbital speed just above the surface
v = √agr = √(9.8 m/s2)(6.37 × 106 m) = 7900 m/s = 7.9 km/s

For scale, the speed of sound is 343 m/s — so a low orbit is about 23 times the speed of sound. That number is the real reason getting to orbit is hard. Altitude is cheap; the speed is what costs.

Does Hermes provide “full” Earth gravity?

The way to avoid free fall on a long voyage is to spin the ship, so the outer wall becomes a floor that can push — the arrangement set out on Ch06c. The interesting question is how well a particular design would work, and Trevor runs the numbers on a fictional one: the Hermes from The Martian.

His method is worth more than the answer. The film gives no specification, so he measured the ring off a frame of the film, using a window of known height (about 5.25 ft) as the scale, and timed one rotation from the footage: r = 52.5 ft = 16.0 m, T = 31.60 s. The still itself is copyright and is not reproduced here.

Spin rate to surface gravity
ag = v2r  with  v = 2πrT  ⇒  ag = 4π2rT2
= 4π2(16.0 m)(31.60 s)2 = 0.63 m/s2

Earth is 9.81 m/s2. Pluto is 0.60. So the Hermes ring, as filmed, delivers Pluto gravity — about one sixteenth of Earth's, and less than half the Moon's. Enough to let a dropped pen find the floor eventually; not enough to stop the bone loss the mission was presumably spinning to prevent.

Review

Weight— what a scale reads is the normal force, not the gravitational force. On a level, stationary floor they happen to be equal.
Weightlessness— FN = 0, which happens when the floor is falling as fast as you are. Gravity is unchanged. Free fall is the better word.
Orbit— free fall that keeps missing the ground. Gravity supplies the centripetal force, so v = √agr — independent of the orbiting mass.
Numbers to hold— low orbit is 7.9 km/s, about 23 times the speed of sound, and one lap takes roughly 85–93 minutes.
Use the orbital radius— not the altitude. Add the planet's radius first.
Artificial gravity— ag = 4π2r/T2. Spinning a ring is the only way to get a floor that pushes, and the numbers are demanding — Hermes manages Pluto.
