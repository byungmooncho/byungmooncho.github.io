Newton's Second Law for Torques

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea F = ma has a rotational twin, and it is the equation the whole chapter has been building towards: τ = Iα. Net torque in place of net force, moment of inertia in place of mass, angular acceleration in place of linear. Once you have it, a spinning problem is solved the same way a sliding one is — and the only new skill is choosing which quantity to find first.
You already know this Torque from Ch10c — τ = rF sin θ, the turning effect of a force about an axis — and the moment of inertia from Ch10b, including the table of standard shapes. This page is those two put together.

The law itself

Trevor opens with the parallel stated plainly: forces make objects accelerate; unbalanced torques make objects spin faster or slower. Mass resists the first; the moment of inertia resists the second.

Worked example: spinning a disk

How much torque is required to give a disk of mass 2.0 kg and radius 25 cm, spinning about its centre, an angular acceleration of 35 rad/s2? And how much force is required?

Torque first, then force
I = ½MR2 = ½(2.0 kg)(0.25 m)2 = 0.0625 kg·m2
τ = Iα = (0.0625)(35 rad/s2) = 2.2 N·m
τ = rF sin θ = rF sin 90° = rF  ⇒  F = τr = 2.2 N·m0.25 m = 8.8 N

Two equations, used in opposite directions. The first turns a desired motion into the torque it needs; the second turns that torque into a force you could actually apply. Trevor's closing note on the slide is the caution that comes with the second: the angle at which the force is applied changes the torque required. Pull at 30° to the radius instead of 90° and you need twice the force for the same effect.

What the moment of inertia does to the answer

Trevor's cleanest demonstration of why I deserves its own symbol. Two identical masses on a light rod, the same force applied the same way — but the masses sit at different distances from the axis.

τ = I1α1 = 2(Mr12)α1   and   τ = I2α2 = 2(Mr22)α2

The two masses are each Mr2 about the axis, hence the factor of two. For the same torque, moving the masses out by a factor of two makes I four times larger and the angular acceleration four times smaller. It is the same physics as the figure skater pulling her arms in, run in reverse.

When there is more than one route

Trevor flags a genuine change of difficulty here. Problems from this point can be given the other way round: you are told the final speed and asked for the torque. There is no single equation for that, so the work is in choosing the order.

You are givenRoute
the angular acceleration straight to τ = Iα
a final angular speed and a time rotational kinematics first — α = Δω/Δt (Ch10a) — then τ = Iα
a final angular speed and an angle turned ω2 = ω02 + 2αΔθ for α, then τ = Iα
a force and where it acts τ = rF sin θ first, then α = τ/I
More than one way is a feature, not a trap Trevor's point is that you have reached the stage where several routes reach the same answer. That is worth using deliberately: solve it one way, then check the result by another. If the two disagree, the error is usually a wrong row of the moment-of-inertia table or an angle left out of the torque.

Review

The law— τnet = Iα, the rotational form of Newton's second law.
Each piece— net torque in N·m, moment of inertia in kg·m2, angular acceleration in rad/s2.
Torque from a force— τ = rF sin θ — the angle matters, and is 90° only when the force is perpendicular to the radius.
Same torque, bigger I— smaller α. Moving mass outwards by 2 raises I by 4.
Choosing a route— if you are not given α directly, get it from rotational kinematics first.
