Comparing Trendlines and Models

Adapted from the lecture deck by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman · physicsthisweek.com

Synopsis A big part of physics is creating and exploring equations (models) that describe the behavior of systems. In lab, we gather data, graph it, and fit a trendline — and the trendline must be compared to the model equation. A rookie mistake is to fit any data with a trendline and assume the slope is "the answer." This note shows how to pick the right trendline for the model you are testing, and how to extract physical quantities from the fit.
Performance Expectations
  • PE1Pick the trendline type that matches the form of the model equation being tested.
  • PE2Match trendline constants (slope, intercept, prefactor, exponent) to model constants and extract physical quantities from them.
  • PE3Analyze the same data two ways — linearizing vs. fitting directly — and use the comparison (including the fitted exponent) to judge how well the model describes the data.

Trendlines vs. models

Trendlines are the "fits" that we add to a graph. Models are the equations that describe physical situations. Spreadsheet software offers several trendline types, each with its own equation form:

Trendline typeEquation formA physics model with this form
Lineary = m x + bHooke's law: Δx = (1/k) F
Polynomialy = A x² + B x + CKinematics: x = ½at² + v₀t + x₀
Powery = A xBOscillation period: T = (2π/√k) m0.5
Exponentialy = A eBxRC discharge: V = V₀e−t/RC
Logarithmicy = A ln x + BSound level vs. intensity

The trendline you pick should represent the model you are testing. The fit's job is not to look pretty or maximize R² — it is to put your data in the same mathematical form as the model, so you can read physical quantities out of the fit constants.

Worked example 1 — stretching a spring (static)

Hang masses from a spring and record the stretch Δy for each applied force F. The model is Hooke's law: F = k Δx. Solved for what we graphed (stretch vs. force): Δx = (1/k) F — a straight line through the origin whose slope is 1/k, not k.

Watch out The slope itself (0.0414) is not the spring constant — its units are m/N, the reciprocal of what k should be (N/m). Matching the trendline to the model is what tells you to take the reciprocal. Always carry the units through.

Check yourself: a different spring gives the fit Δx = 0.0250·F − 0.001 (SI units). What is its spring constant?

Worked example 2 — oscillating spring (dynamic)

Now hang a mass on the same kind of spring and let it oscillate. The model for the period is T = 2π√(m/k). Measure the period T for several masses:

The model is not linear in m — so there are two ways to graph the data.

Way 1 — linearize: graph T² vs. m

Square both sides of the model: T² = (2π)²(m/k) = (4π²/k)·m. Graphed as T² vs. m, this is the form y = mx + b — so a linear trendline is now the right choice.

y = 1.7062x − 0.0106 · R² = 0.9947

Period squared vs. mass with a linear trendline.

Matching trendline to model: slope = 4π²/k, so

k = 4π²/(slope) = 4π²/(1.7062 s²/kg) = 23.14 kg/s² = 23.14 N/m

Way 2 — fit directly: graph T vs. m with a power trendline

Rewrite the model as a power law: T = (2π/√k)·√m = (2π/√k)·m0.5. This matches the power trendline form y = A xB, with A = 2π/√k and the model predicting B = 0.5.

y = 1.4833x0.581 · R² = 0.9869   y = 1.2308x0.4733

Period vs. mass with two power-law trendlines (as in the original lecture).

Matching the prefactor to the model, A = 2π/√k, so k = (2π)²/A² = 4π²/A². Using A = 1.2308:

k = 4π²/(1.2308)² = 26.06 kg/s² = 26.06 N/m

Model check The fitted exponent is a free bonus check: the model says B should be 0.5, and the fits give 0.4733–0.581. Close, but not exact — real data has scatter. And notice the three k values for the same spring: 24.15 (static), 23.14 (linearized), 26.06 (power fit). No single fit hands you "the answer" — comparing methods, exponents, and units is what tells you whether to trust a result. A high R² alone does not mean the model is right.

Review

  1. Pick the trendline from the model, not from habit. Rearrange the model until it matches one of the trendline forms; that form is the fit to use.
  2. The slope is rarely "the answer." Match fit constants to model constants (slope = 1/k, slope = 4π²/k, A = 2π/√k, …) and solve for the physical quantity — units included.
  3. Cross-check. Linearizing and direct fitting should give consistent values, and a fitted exponent near the predicted one is evidence the model actually describes your data.

Practice
