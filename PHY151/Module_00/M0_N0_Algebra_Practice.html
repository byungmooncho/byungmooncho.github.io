Algebra & Calculus Practice: Two Moves That Rearrange Any Equation — and One New Idea

Synopsis Math is an integral part of doing physics at any level — we handle equations and algebraic expressions constantly. And you are already good at this, even if you don't know it. Imagine you're driving on a highway at 60 mph and your destination is 120 miles away. How long will it take? You immediately say 2 hours — and to get that answer, you performed an algebraic manipulation in your head. In this note we slow that reflex down, name the two moves it uses — cross over (for multiplication and division) and throw over (for addition and subtraction) — and practice them until rearranging any physics equation is as automatic as the road-trip answer. Because PHY 151 is calculus-based, we finish with one new idea on top of the algebra: the derivative as a rate of change, and the single rule — the power rule — that handles almost every derivative you will take this semester.
Performance Expectations After working through this note, you should be able to…
  • 1Distinguish terms from factors in any equation: terms are separated by + or − signs; symbols multiplied or divided together form a single term made of factors.
  • 2Use the cross-over move: when a factor crosses the equal sign, upstairs (numerator) becomes downstairs (denominator) and vice versa.
  • 3Use the throw-over move: when a term is thrown over to the other side of the equal sign, its sign flips — positive becomes negative and vice versa.
  • 4Follow the two-step sequence to solve for any symbol: first isolate the term that contains it (throw everything else over), then bracket the opposite side and divide out the unwanted factor.
  • 5Recognize the real physics equations on the Algebra Review Sheet — W = Fd cos θ, v = v0 + at, P = E/t, U = ½kx² — as equations you will rearrange all semester. (You'll master their physics in Modules 1–4; here we only care about the algebra.)
  • 6Take a derivative with the power rule — ddt(tn) = ntn−1 — term by term, and recognize that differentiating position gives velocity and differentiating velocity gives acceleration.

💡You already know this

You do algebraic manipulation in your head every day. You just haven't been calling it that.

🚗 The road trip 120 miles at 60 mph → 2 hours. You took distance and divided by speed — you rearranged s = d/t into t = d/s without writing anything down.
🧑‍🎓 The headcount A class of 25 has 10 males. Females? 15 — you did 25 − 10. You rearranged t = m + f into f = t − m instantly.
🍴 Splitting the bill A $60 dinner among 4 friends is $15 each. Total = (share)×(people), and you solved for the share: share = total/people. Cross-over, again.

Guiding questions

  1. What is the difference between a term and a factor?
  2. When a factor crosses the equal sign, what changes about its position?
  3. When a term is thrown over the equal sign, what changes about its sign?
  4. In what order should you apply the two moves to isolate a chosen symbol — and what must you do before dividing out a factor?
  5. What does a derivative measure, and what does the power rule do to the exponent and the coefficient of each term?
 

Move 1 — Cross over (multiplication and division)

Remember that speed is the distance covered divided by the time taken:

Speed (miles/hour) = Distance (miles)Time (hours)

To calculate the time taken on the road trip, you took the total distance of 120 miles and divided it by the speed, 60 miles per hour:

Time (hours) = 120 (miles)60 (miles/hour) = 2 hours

Now use symbols — Speed = s, Distance = d, Time = t — and write both versions side by side:

s = dt   and   t = ds

Notice what changed from the first expression to the second: s in the numerator ("upstairs") became the denominator ("downstairs"), and t in the denominator ("downstairs") became the numerator ("upstairs") when s and t crossed over to the other side of the equal sign. To see it clearly, write every side as a fraction — any number divided by 1 is itself, so s = s/1:

s1 = dt   ⟶   t1 = ds

That manipulation involved two simultaneous crossings. But it doesn't have to. Cross only t over and leave s where it is:

s1 = dt   ⟶   st1 = d1   ⟶   d = st

You just derived the distance covered when you travel at speed s for time t. Check it with the road trip: (60 miles/hour) × (2 hours) = 120 miles. ✓

This "trick" — when you cross over the equal sign, you go from downstairs to upstairs and vice versa — will always work, because it shortcuts the many steps of multiplying or dividing both sides that you're used to doing. Convince yourself once that the middle-school procedure gives the same answer; then don't bother with the middle-school procedure again.

Move 2 — Throw over (addition and subtraction)

There is a similar shortcut for addition and subtraction, and again you already do it in your head. A class of 25 has 10 males; you know instantly there are 15 females. What manipulation did you do? Let total = t, males = m, females = f, so t = m + f, that is, 25 = 10 + f. To find f you did 25 − 10 = 15. Algebraically: f = t − m.

The trick is this: if you take a term and throw it over to the other side of the equal sign, its sign flips. (A number without an explicit sign in front is positive.)

Start(+t) = (+m) + (+f)
Throw over(+t) − m = (+f)m crossed the equal sign; + became −

Now let's do something weird. Throw t itself over to the right side:

Start(+t) = (+m) + (+f)
Throw over0 = (+m) + f − tyou must write a zero when a side has no terms left — never leave it blank
Check0 = 10 + 15 − 25 ✓it makes total sense with the class numbers

The key message: throw a term over, and positive becomes negative and vice versa.

Putting both moves together

Now try an equation with both addition/subtraction and multiplication/division:

d = d0 + vt

Let's solve for v (also said as "isolate v" or "make v the subject"). First, count the terms — pieces separated by + or − signs. The right side has two terms (d0 and vt); the left side has one (d). Symbols that are multiplied or divided together are grouped as a single term: vt is one term consisting of the factors v and t. Here is the sequence of operations you ought to follow:

Startd = d0 + vt
1·Throw over−d0 + d = +vtisolate the term containing v: throw d0 over and switch its sign
2·Bracket(−d0 + d) = vtbefore you divide out, bracket together all terms on the opposite side
3·Cross over(−d0 + d)t = vdivide out the factor you don't want: t crosses over, upstairs → downstairs
Tidyv = d − d0tthe same expression, written more neatly

One new idea: the derivative as a rate of change

PHY 151 is a calculus-based course, and here is the entire reason why: physics is the study of change, and the derivative is the mathematical machine that measures change. On the road trip, your speed was the rate at which your position changed — s = d/t works when the speed is steady. When the speed is not steady, we shrink the time interval until it is instantaneous, and that limit is the derivative:

v = dxdt  ("velocity is the derivative of position with respect to time")

For nearly everything this semester, one rule does the differentiating — the power rule, applied term by term:

ddt(tn) = ntn−1

In words: the exponent hops down front as a multiplier, and the exponent drops by one. Two consequences you will use constantly: the derivative of a constant is zero (a constant doesn't change, so its rate of change is nothing), and a constant coefficient just rides along in front.

Startx(t) = x0 + v0t + ½at²the position equation from the Algebra Review Sheet — differentiate it term by term
Constant → 0ddt(x0) = 0x0 is a fixed number; it doesn't change with time
Power ruleddt(v0t) = v0t = t¹: the 1 hops down, the exponent drops to 0, and t⁰ = 1
Power ruleddt(½at²) = atthe 2 hops down and cancels the ½; t² becomes t¹
Collectv(t) = dxdt = v0 + atdifferentiating position produced the velocity equation — equation 4 on the Review Sheet!
Once morea = dvdtdifferentiate again and only at survives: the acceleration. Position → velocity → acceleration, one derivative at a time.

Going the other way — from acceleration back to velocity, or velocity back to position — is integration, which undoes the power rule (the exponent goes up by one, and you divide by the new exponent). We will build that up carefully in Module 1; for now, being comfortable with the derivative direction is all you need.

For x(t) = t² − 6t + 4, find the time at which the velocity is zero.

Summary: which move, when?

You see…The moveWhat happens
Symbols joined by × or ÷ (factors in one term) Cross over Crossing the equal sign sends upstairs → downstairs and downstairs → upstairs. Bracket all terms on the opposite side first.
Pieces separated by + or − (terms) Throw over Throwing a term over the equal sign flips its sign: + becomes − and vice versa.
A power of t to differentiate (tn) Power rule The exponent hops down front as a multiplier and drops by one: ntn−1. Constants differentiate to zero; coefficients ride along.
A side with no terms left Write 0 You must write a zero — an empty side is never left blank.

Four rules to remember

🔁 s = d/t  ⇆  st = d Cross over. A factor crossing the equal sign goes from downstairs to upstairs and vice versa.
➕ t = m + f  ⇆  t − m = f Throw over. A term thrown across the equal sign flips its sign.
🧱 (d − d₀) = vt Bracket first. Before dividing out a factor, bracket together all terms on the opposite side.
📈 d/dt (tⁿ) = ntⁿ⁻¹ Power rule. The exponent hops down as a multiplier and drops by one. Position → velocity → acceleration, one derivative at a time.
