Verifying Newton's Second Law with a Half Atwood Machine

Synopsis Newton's second law fits in five characters, but it makes a testable promise: double the net force on an object and its acceleration doubles; double the mass and the acceleration halves. This note puts that promise on trial with a cart on a track pulled by a hanging mass. The interesting part is that the obvious experiment does not work — and finding out why is most of the physics.
Before you read on — predict The cart is pulled by a hanging mass and accelerates. You now double the hanging mass, which doubles the force pulling the system along.
Does the acceleration double? Commit to yes or no, with one sentence of reasoning, before you turn the page.

The apparatus

What the string does

Over a light string and a frictionless pulley, the tension is the same at both ends. The pulley changes the direction the string pulls, not how hard it pulls.

That single fact is what ties the two free-body diagrams together: the same symbol T appears in both, which is what makes two equations solvable for two unknowns.

Two bodies, two diagrams

Each body gets its own free-body diagram, with its own positive direction chosen along the way it moves. The cart's positive direction is along the track; the hanging mass's is straight down. That is allowed, and it is what keeps both accelerations positive.

  1. Draw the setup in your guided notes — track, cart, pulley, string, hanging mass. Getting the geometry down is half of getting the physics right.
  2. Draw both free-body diagrams, one per object, and mark +a on each.
  3. Write Newton's second law for the cart, along the track.
  4. Write Newton's second law for the hanging mass, taking down as positive.

Putting them together

  • T=mca the cart — tension is its only horizontal force
  • Fg,H−T=mHa the hanging mass — gravity wins, but only by mHa
  • a=Fg,Hmc+mH substitute the first into the second — the driving weight over the total mass
  1. Substitute the cart equation into the hanging-mass equation and solve for a. Show every step.

Why your prediction was probably wrong

Look at where the two masses appear. The hanging mass sits in the numerator, because its weight is what drives the system. But it also sits in the denominator, because it is part of what has to be accelerated. The cart is being dragged along, and so is the hanger.

So doubling the hanging mass does not double the acceleration. You increased the force, but you increased the total mass at the same time, and the two effects partly cancel. This is the trap in the apparatus: with this setup you cannot change the force without also changing the mass. Which would seem to make it useless for testing F = ma — except for one trick.

The trick that makes the experiment work

Do not add mass to the hanger. Move it there from the cart.

Take a small mass Δ off the cart and hang it on the string instead. The system becomes (mc − Δ) and (mH + Δ). The total mass in the denominator has not changed by a gram — but the driving weight in the numerator has gone up. Now the only thing varying is the net force, which is precisely the experiment Newton's second law asks for.

  1. Describe the verification strategy in your own words: what do you change, what do you hold fixed, what do you measure, and what pattern must the data show if F = ma is telling the truth?
  2. Now design the other half of the test — the one that checks a ∝ 1/m. What would you vary this time, and what would you keep constant?
  3. Run it. Transfer the mass in equal steps, record the acceleration each time, and plot a against the hanging weight. What shape should the graph be, and what does its slope represent?
Where this sits. The shared-tension idea used here is developed properly in Note 8, where a hanging object is held by two strings at an angle and you resolve each tension into components. Here you only need the one fact — same string, same tension — because both bodies move along a single line. The two-body method itself, writing one equation per object and linking them through a shared unknown, is the pattern behind every connected-system problem you will meet.
