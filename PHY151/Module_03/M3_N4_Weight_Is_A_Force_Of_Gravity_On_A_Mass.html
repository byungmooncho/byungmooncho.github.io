Weight Is a Force of Gravity on a Mass

Synopsis In everyday speech “weight” is a number on a bathroom scale, reported in pounds — a unit of mass. In physics it is something else entirely: weight is a force, measured in newtons, the pull of a planet on a mass. Mass belongs to the object and travels with it. Weight does not: it is what a particular planet does to that object. This note measures the relationship between the two, finds that it is a straight line, and shows why the slope of that line is the reason a feather and a bowling ball fall together in a vacuum.
Before you read on — predict You hold a 20 kg mass in one hand and a 10 kg mass in the other, and release them at the same instant. Gravity pulls twice as hard on the 20 kg mass — that much is not in dispute.
Does it reach the floor first? Commit to yes or no, and write one sentence saying why, before you read further.
Performance expectations
  1. Distinguish mass from weight by unit, by definition, and by what happens to each when the object is moved to another planet.
  2. Measure weight against mass with a spring scale, plot the two, and interpret the slope of the line, including its unit.
  3. Explain why every mass near a planet falls with the same acceleration, in terms of force and inertia.
  4. Identify the third-law partner of an object’s weight, and state which body it acts on.
  5. Calculate weight, mass, normal force, or g given the other quantities, on Earth or on any other planet.

Movement 1 — weight is not a property of the object

Where does weight come from?

Take a mass far out in deep space, nowhere near a star or a planet. It still has all the mass it ever had. Push it and it still resists being pushed. But nothing is pulling on it, so it has no weight. Bring that same object near a planet and a force appears on it, directed toward the planet’s centre. That force is its weight.

A trap worth naming now

When an object rests on a table, the normal force pushing up on it is not the third-law partner of its weight. Weight’s partner is the object’s pull on the planet, and that force acts on the planet — it is not even in the object’s free-body diagram.

The normal force is the partner of something else: the object pushing down on the table. That push acts on the table; the normal force is the table pushing back, and it acts on the object. Two different pairs, four different arrows, easily confused.

Naming a third-law partner is a skill, not a fact, so practise it here rather than taking my word for it. For each situation below, name the partner of the force given, and — this is the part that decides whether you have it — say which body that partner acts on.

  1. A book rests on a table. Force given: the table pushes up on the book. partner acts on
  2. The same book on the same table. Force given: the Earth pulls down on the book. partner acts on
  3. The book is now in free fall, halfway to the floor. Force given: the Earth pulls down on the book. partner acts on Has your answer changed from the previous question? Should it have?
How to check yourself

A third-law pair is always the same interaction read in both directions: if the first force is “A pulls B,” the partner is “B pulls A” — same interaction, same size, opposite direction, and never on the same body. Two forces that appear in the same free-body diagram are therefore never a third-law pair, no matter how neatly they cancel.

The result

w = Fg = mg

Where this holds

This is the weight of a mass at or near the surface of a planet, where g is effectively constant. Near Earth’s surface g = 9.8 m/s²; far from the surface it falls off, and on a different planet it is a different number entirely.

Note that N/kg and m/s² are the same unit. The slope you measured on a scale and the acceleration you measured in free fall in Module 2 are the same quantity — which is the claim the next movement has to justify.

Movement 3 — why everything falls together

Your prediction, settled

The proportional relationship between weight and mass is the important thing here. It says that near the surface of a planet, everything — light or heavy — falls at the same acceleration, once air resistance is out of the way. That qualifier is not a dodge: a sheet of paper loses to a book because the air pushes on it, not because gravity treats it differently, and crumpling the paper into a ball is enough to make it keep up. The claim below is about gravity alone.

Even stated that carefully it is a strange claim, because gravity really does pull harder on the 20 kg mass. Twice as hard, exactly.

The resolution is that you only counted half of it. Newton’s first law says a larger mass has more inertia, so it takes more force to accelerate it by the same amount. The 20 kg mass gets twice the force — and needs twice the force. Write the second law and watch the mass leave:

  • Fg=ma the only force on a freely falling mass is its weight
  • a=Fgm=mgm=g the mass cancels — the acceleration does not depend on it

Doubling the mass doubles the numerator and the denominator together. The ratio is fixed, and that fixed ratio is g. This is why the slope of your graph had to come out as the free-fall acceleration: the slope is Fg/m.

Worth stopping on — the m that cancelled was two different things

Look again at what cancelled. The m on top came from gravity: it says how strongly a planet pulls on this object. The m underneath came from inertia: it says how hard this object is to accelerate. Those are different properties. Nothing you have learned so far requires them to be the same number.

Suppose they were not. Suppose an object could be twice as hard to pull on as another and only 1.5 times as hard to accelerate. Then the ratio would not cancel, heavier things really would fall faster, and Galileo would have been wrong. The only reason free fall is universal is that gravitational mass and inertial mass come out equal, for every material anyone has ever tested, to about one part in 1015.

Nobody in this course will explain why. It is one of the few coincidences in physics that turned out not to be a coincidence — Einstein took it as the starting point of general relativity. File it next to the claim from N1: velocity is relative, acceleration is absolute. This is the same kind of statement about what nature does not let you distinguish.

  1. “You drop a 20 kg and a 10 kg mass. The 20 kg mass experiences twice as large a force of gravity as the 10 kg mass, but they still accelerate at the same rate, 9.8 m/s².” True or false?
  2. Give the reason for your choice in one sentence. Your sentence must mention both force and inertia — an answer that mentions only one of them is not an explanation.
  3. Go back to your prediction in the box at the top. Was it right? If not, name the step in the reasoning where it went wrong.
Open phenomenon 4, from N2 — now closed A target is released at the exact instant a ball is fired straight at it. Why do you hit it every time, however fast you fire and however far away the target is?

Before you read the resolution, find what you wrote in N2 and put it where you can see it.

The resolution is two moves, and you already have both of them.

From Module 2: the horizontal and vertical parts of a projectile’s motion do not interfere with each other. Firing the ball horizontally does nothing whatever to how it falls. In the time t it takes to cross the room, it drops ½gt² below the line you aimed along.

From this note: the target drops ½gt² too — the same ½gt², because every object near the Earth falls with the same acceleration whatever its mass. A steel ball and a stuffed toy, a 20 kg target and a 10 kg one: same g, same drop, same time.

So both of them fall away from the aim line by exactly the same amount at every instant. The drop cancels out of the aiming problem entirely. Aiming straight at where the target was is correct, not approximately but exactly, and it stays correct for any launch speed and any distance. That is why it never misses.

  1. Compare this with the guess you wrote in N2. Which part did you already have, and which part were you missing?
  2. Fire the ball twice as fast. Do you still hit the target? What changes about where and when you hit it?
  3. Now break it. Name a change to the demonstration that would make the shot miss — and say which of the two moves above your change destroys.

Movement 4 — weight, normal force, and other planets

An object at rest on a level floor

A mass sitting still on a floor is not accelerating, so the forces on it must cancel. There are two: its weight pulling down, and the normal force from the floor pushing up. They must be equal in size and opposite in direction.

Where FN = mg holds — and where it does not

The general statement is always ΣF = ma applied along the vertical. The shortcut FN = mg follows from it only when the surface is level and the object has no vertical acceleration.

Tilt the surface and it fails — that restriction is lifted in N7, where FN becomes mg cos θ. Accelerate the object vertically and it fails again — lifted in N9, where a scale in a moving elevator stops reading mg. Do not carry FN = mg into either of those situations.

  1. An obese cat with a mass of 46 kg rests on the floor. Find its weight and the magnitude of the normal force on it, and draw its free-body diagram.
  2. A cat weighs 57.3 N. What is its mass? Resting on a floor, what normal force acts on it? If the cat is sent to another planet, what is its mass there?
  3. On the Moon, a person of mass 70 kg weighs 112 N. What is the acceleration due to gravity on the Moon?
  4. A 65 kg astronaut weighs 638 N on Earth. What is the astronaut’s mass on Mars, where g = 3.4 m/s²? What is the astronaut’s weight there?
The habit to build

Mass is given in kilograms and never changes. Weight is given in newtons and changes with the planet. If a quantity in a problem is in kg it is a mass; if it is in N it is a force. Check the unit before you decide which one you have been handed.
