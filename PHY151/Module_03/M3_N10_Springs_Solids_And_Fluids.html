Springs, Solids and Fluids: Forces You Have to Measure

Synopsis Nine notes of this module have done the same thing: pick an object, draw the forces on it, apply ΣF = ma. This one is different in kind. It introduces three forces — a stretched spring, a squeezed solid, the air pushing back on a falling body — whose strengths do not follow from Newton’s laws at all. Each carries a constant that nobody can derive and everybody has to measure. That is not a gap in the theory. It is how physics meets real materials.

Movement 1 — a different kind of force law

Laws you derive, and laws you measure

Almost everything in Module 3 has come from one place. ΣF = ma is the engine; FN = mg cos θ on an incline, a = g sin θ, T = mg/(2 sin θ) for a hanging sign — every one of those was derived. Given the geometry, you could have worked them out on paper with no laboratory at all.

The three forces in this note are not like that. How hard a particular spring pulls back, how much a steel bar shortens under load, how hard the air pushes on a falling body: no amount of reasoning from Newton’s laws will tell you. You have to go and measure the material.

You have already met one of these

In N5 you met μ, the coefficient of friction. Notice what was never said about it: where it comes from. There is no formula for μ — it is a number belonging to two particular surfaces, and the only way to get it is to slide one on the other and see.

k, Y, S and CD in this note are the same kind of quantity. A constant that stands in for material behaviour we cannot derive, determined by experiment and then looked up. Physics is full of them, and recognising one when you meet it is a real skill.

Movement 2 — springs

Hooke’s law

Hang a spring from a hook and it settles at some length with nothing on it. That is its natural length, and at that length it pulls on nothing. Now hang a mass on it. The spring stretches until it is pulling up exactly as hard as gravity is pulling down — and because the mass then hangs still, you know the spring’s force must equal the weight. That is what makes the measurement possible.

  • Fk=kΔx double the extension and the spring pulls back twice as hard
Where this holds, and what Δx is

Δx is the extension, measured from the natural length. A spring 30 cm long that was 22 cm unloaded has Δx = 8 cm, not 30. This is the single most common way to get a spring problem wrong.

And the law holds only up to the spring’s elastic limit. Stretch it far enough and it stops springing back to its natural length: the graph stops being straight, k stops meaning anything, and the spring is permanently deformed. Everything in this note assumes you have stayed inside that limit.

The spring force is a restoring force: it always points back toward the natural length. Stretch the spring and it pulls; compress it and it pushes.

Measuring k

Hang known weights, record the extension each time, and plot one against the other. If Hooke’s law is right the points fall on a straight line through the origin, and the slope of that line is the spring constant.

Movement 3 — solids

Hooke’s law, spread over an area

A spring is a special object, wound to be stretchy. But every solid does the same thing to a smaller degree: push on a steel bar and it shortens, twist a rubber block and it leans. The relation is Hooke’s law again, with two changes. The force is spread over an area, so what matters is force per unit area. And the stretch is compared with the object’s original length, so what matters is the fractional change, not the absolute one.

In both cases

stress = modulus × strain

Y is the Young’s modulus and S the shear modulus. Both are properties of the material, not of the particular block — steel has the same Y whatever shape you cut it into, which is exactly why writing the law in terms of F/A and ΔL/L₀ rather than F and ΔL was worth the trouble.

  1. Compare F = kΔx with F/A = Y(ΔL/L₀). Which symbol in the second plays the part that k plays in the first? What did the other two changes buy you?
  2. Two bars of the same steel, same length, one twice the cross-sectional area. Under the same force, which shortens more, and by what factor?
  3. A material with a large Y — is it stiff or floppy? Give an example of each.

Movement 4 — fluids

Drag, and why falling stops speeding up

Drag is friction in a fluid — air, water, honey. It opposes motion, exactly as surface friction does. But it differs from the friction of N5 in one decisive way, and that difference is the whole of this section.

The difference that matters

Surface friction from N5 is f = μFN. Look at what is missing: there is no v in it. Slide a block slowly or quickly and kinetic friction is the same size.

Drag is not like that. Drag grows with speed, and at the speeds in this note it grows as the square of it. Go twice as fast and the air pushes back four times as hard. A force that changes as the motion changes behaves very differently from one that does not.

Settling an argument with N4

N4 said every mass near the Earth falls with the same acceleration, whatever its mass, and hedged it with “once air resistance is out of the way.” This note is where that hedge gets paid for.

Terminal velocity depends on m, A and CD — on how heavy you are, how big you are and what shape you are. A skydiver and a feather have wildly different ones. So N4’s claim was never a claim about falling in the real world; it was a claim about gravity alone, and it is exactly true as such. The paper loses to the book because of the air, and this equation is the air.

Looking back. This is the last note of Module 3, and it is the one that steps outside the pattern. Everything from N1 to N9 was Newton’s laws applied to a new geometry. Here the geometry is easy and the force laws themselves are new — three of them, each with a constant that had to be measured. When you meet springs again it will be as oscillators, and when you meet drag again it will be in a fluids course; the constants will still be measured, and you will still have to look them up.
