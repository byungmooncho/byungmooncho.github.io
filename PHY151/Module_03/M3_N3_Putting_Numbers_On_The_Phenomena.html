Putting Numbers on the Phenomena

Synopsis In N2 you watched things happen and said which law was responsible. That is half of physics. The other half is how much — and it is the half that decides whether a bridge stands up. This note goes back to the same demonstrations and works out the numbers: what the penny weighs, how hard each person on a trolley accelerates, and where to point the launcher. The physics is not new. What is new is the discipline of getting an answer you can defend.
Before you read on — predict You are on a trolley with a total mass of 100 kg. You push on someone else whose trolley and body come to 200 kg. Two separate questions, and they do not have the same answer:
Do the two of you feel the same size force?
Do the two of you get the same acceleration? Answer both, yes or no, with one sentence each, before you read on.

Movement 1 — the rules of the road

How to get an answer you can defend

Every calculation in this module follows the same four steps, and the marks are in the steps, not the final number. A right answer with no working is worth less than a wrong answer whose working shows exactly where it went off — because the second one can be repaired and the first one cannot be trusted.

Every time

convert → substitute → carry the units → check it is reasonable

The unit rule

Physics equations are written for SI units: kilograms, metres, seconds, newtons. Grams, centimetres and minutes must be converted before they go into an equation, not afterwards. A mass of 2.5 g put into w = mg as “2.5” gives an answer a thousand times too big, and nothing later in the calculation will warn you.

Carry the unit on every line. If the units of your answer come out as something that is not a newton when you were looking for a force, the algebra is wrong — and you have just caught it for free.

Movement 2 — what the penny weighs

From mass to weight

In N2 the penny fell into the cup once the card was gone. It fell because of a force: the pull of the Earth on it, which we call its weight. Weight is a force and is measured in newtons. Mass is the amount of stuff and is measured in kilograms. The two are related by

  • w=Fg=mg g = 9.8 m/s² near the Earth’s surface
Borrowed for now — earned in N4

You are being handed w = mg here as a tool to calculate with. Why weight should be proportional to mass, why the constant is the same g that appears in free fall, and why that means a heavy object and a light one fall together — all of that is N4’s job, and you should not take it on trust for longer than that.

This note is about the arithmetic. Do not let a clean calculation persuade you that you have understood the physics behind it; those are two different achievements.

Movement 3 — the third law, with numbers

Equal forces, unequal accelerations

N2 established that you cannot get yourself moving without pushing on something else, and that the push and the push back are equal in size and opposite in direction. Now put numbers on it, because the numbers reveal something the words hide.

Task 3 — both sides of the push
  1. You and your trolley come to 100 kg. You push with 100 N. Find your acceleration.
  2. The other person and their trolley come to 200 kg. What force acts on them? State it before you calculate anything.
  3. Find their acceleration.
  4. Compare the two accelerations. Write the comparison as a ratio, and say what that ratio is the ratio of.
The thing worth taking away

Equal and opposite forces do not mean equal and opposite effects. The force is shared; the acceleration is not, because a = F/m and the two bodies bring different masses to the same interaction.

This is why the wall in N2 did not visibly move when you pushed it. The wall felt exactly the force you felt. It is simply attached to a building, and a = F/m with an enormous m gives an acceleration too small to see. Nothing in that sentence requires the forces to have been unequal.

Push the wall to see the limit case: the same force, and a response too small to see.

  1. Go back to the wall in N2 and finish the argument properly: name the quantity that makes the wall’s response invisible, and say what would have to be true for you to see the wall move.
  2. In N1 you met the claim that velocity is relative but acceleration is absolute. You and the other person disagree about who is moving. Do you disagree about the accelerations? Use your two numbers to answer.
One line only — so far

Every calculation in this note has had its forces along a single line: the weight straight down, the pushes straight along the floor. That is why one equation was enough each time.

When the forces are not all in a line, ΣF = ma does not change and does not get harder. It just gets applied once in each of two perpendicular directions:

ΣFx = max   and   ΣFy = may

The two are independent: what happens along x has no say in what happens along y. Breaking a slanted force into those two pieces is the resolution you did in Module 2 — same trigonometry, nothing new. N7 and N8 are where this gets used, and N7 makes the further point that you get to choose which two perpendicular directions to use.

Movement 4 — aiming the launcher

Where to point the barrel

The last of N2’s demonstrations was the falling target: aim straight at it, fire as it is released, and you hit it every time. Before you can fire you have to aim, and aiming is a geometry problem with no physics in it at all.

Task 4 — the launch angle
  1. Draw the setup in your own notes and label X, H and θ.
  2. Which pair of sides does θ sit between, and therefore which trigonometric ratio do you need? Write the ratio before putting any numbers in.
  3. With X = 2.5 m and H = 2.0 m, find θ.
  4. Check it: is your angle less than or greater than 45°, and does that match the picture?
Aiming is not the same as hitting

You have just worked out where to point the launcher. You have not shown why the shot lands — because both the ball and the target fall away from that aim line while the ball is in flight, and nothing here says why those two falls should match.

That is the open question from N2, and N4 closes it. Do the geometry now; collect the physics there.
