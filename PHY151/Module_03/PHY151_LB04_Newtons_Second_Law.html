Newton's Second Law: Measuring the Mass of a System From a Graph

Synopsis Newton's second law says a = F / M. Written that way it is a claim about a straight line: if you hold the mass of a system fixed and increase the force driving it, the acceleration should rise in proportion, and the slope of acceleration against force should be 1 / M — one over the total mass of everything that is moving. This lab puts both halves of that claim on one graph. A cart on a track is pulled by a hanging mass over a pulley. To raise the driving force without changing the mass being driven, you move mass from the cart to the hanger rather than adding any. Five runs of position-versus-time data give you five accelerations; plotting them against the five driving forces gives you a line whose slope is a mass you never put on a scale. Then you weigh the system and see whether the graph was telling the truth.

The physics you are using

Sign convention here. This system bends around a pulley, so a single pair of axes cannot serve both bodies. Choose a positive direction for each body separately, along its own direction of motion: for the cart, take the direction it travels along the track as positive; for the hanging mass, take downward as positive. Both bodies then have the same positive acceleration a, and the string carries the one tension T that connects them. This is the convention your note uses, and it is the reason the two equations combine as cleanly as they do.

The cart feels three forces, but only the tension acts along its direction of motion. Vertically the normal force and the weight cancel, so the cart's equation is just:

T  =  MC a Where this holds: a horizontal track with negligible friction, with the string pulling horizontally. If the track is not level, or if friction is not negligible, a term is missing from this equation — and you will see the evidence for that in your own graph.

The hanging mass feels its weight downward and the tension upward. Taking down as positive for this body:

Fg,H − T  =  MH a    where Fg,H = MH g Note what this says before you solve it: because the right-hand side is positive, T must be smaller than Fg,H. The tension is not the weight of the hanging mass. If it were, the net force on the hanger would be zero and nothing would accelerate.

Eliminate T between the two and solve for the acceleration:

a  =  MH g ⁄ (MH + MC)  =  Fg,H ⁄ MT Read the denominator carefully. It is the total mass — cart, the masses riding on it, and the hanging mass, all of it. The whole system accelerates together, so the whole system's mass resists. This is the single most common place to go wrong in this lab.

Now rearrange it the way the note does, into the form of a straight line:

a  =  ( 1 ⁄ MT ) × Fg,H This is y = mx. Put a on the vertical axis and Fg,H on the horizontal axis, keep MT fixed across every run, and the data should fall on a straight line through the origin whose slope is 1/MT. The slope has units of (m/s²)/N = 1/kg, so one divided by the slope is a mass in kilograms.
◎The two things you must confirm

"Verifying Newton's second law" is not a vague aim. In this lab it means two specific, checkable claims, and your Discussion has to address both by name:

① The relationship is linear Acceleration rises in proportion to the driving force. Not merely "increases" — in proportion, a straight line, and one consistent with passing through the origin.
② The slope is 1 / MT The number the graph gives you is the total mass of the system, and it agrees with the mass you get by weighing the components.

A straight line alone does not verify the second law — plenty of wrong theories predict straight lines. It is the slope matching a mass measured independently that turns a graph into a verification.

Predict first. The hanger carries 100 g and the system accelerates at some rate. You now take a further 100 g off the cart and put it on the hanger, doubling the hanging mass. What happens to the acceleration?

Why that question is the whole design of this lab. Had you added 100 g from outside instead of transferring it, the acceleration would not have doubled: the extra mass would appear in the numerator and in the denominator, and you would be changing two things at once with no way to tell which caused what. Transferring mass changes the driving force while leaving MT alone, which is the only reason the slope of your graph means anything. Your Experimental section must say this in your own words.

The apparatus

The equipment is from Vernier. A low-friction cart runs on a level track about 2.0 m long. An ultrasonic motion sensor at one end records the cart's position many times a second. At the far end the string passes over a pulley at the table edge and down to a hanger. The hanging mass falls, the string pulls the cart, and the two move together.

Part I — The data

Five runs were recorded, with 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 g on the hanger. In each run the cart begins at rest near the sensor, is released, accelerates along the track, and finally stops when the hanger reaches the floor or the cart reaches the end of the track.

  1. Download the data (the cards at the foot of this page). Open a run in Graphical Analysis, or open the workbook if you prefer to work in a spreadsheet — they contain the same numbers.
  2. For each run, compute the driving force Fg,H = MH g, in newtons, from the hanging mass in kilograms. Do this by hand once, in full, with units, as your sample calculation.
  3. Plot position against time and look at the shape before you fit anything. You should be able to see the three phases with your eye: a flat stretch before release, a curved accelerating stretch, and whatever happens after the motion ends.

Part II — Getting an acceleration out of each run

Nothing in this experiment measures acceleration directly. What you have is position against time, and for motion from rest under a constant acceleration, that is a parabola:

x(t)  =  x0 + v0 t + ½ a t² Where this holds: constant acceleration only — which is why the fit window matters so much. Fit a parabola to a stretch of data where the acceleration was not constant and the number you get is not an acceleration, it is an average of several things.

Fit a quadratic to each run. The coefficient of t² in that fit is ½a, so:

a  =  2 × (quadratic coefficient)
Fit only the part of the run where the system is accelerating. Do not include the flat stretch before release, and do not include anything after the hanger lands or the cart reaches the end — the sensor produces genuine nonsense there, including dropouts and reflections. Say in your report what time window you used for each run and how you chose it. This is not a formality: refitting these runs over slightly different windows shifts the individual accelerations by a few percent, and an examiner cannot check your work without knowing which points you kept.

Record for each run: the hanging mass, the driving force, the fit window you used, the quadratic coefficient, the acceleration, and the uncertainty the fit reports for that coefficient.

Part III — The graph that weighs the system

Plot your five accelerations on the vertical axis against the five driving forces on the horizontal axis, and fit a straight line. From the rearranged equation, the slope of that line is 1/MT.

Getting the mass, and its uncertainty, from the slope

MT  =  1 ⁄ slope Check the units as you go. A slope in 1/kg inverts to a mass in kg. If your slope came out in some other combination of units, you have mixed grams with kilograms somewhere, and the mass you report will be wrong by a factor of a thousand.

Your fitting tool reports an uncertainty on the slope. Because the mass is one over the slope, that uncertainty propagates as:

δMT  =  δ(slope) ⁄ (slope)² Equivalently, the fractional uncertainty carries straight across: δM/M = δ(slope)/slope. Quote your experimental mass as M ± δM, with both numbers to a sensible precision.

The comparison

Compare the mass from your graph with the accepted mass of 1548.02 g:

% error = |Mexperimental − Maccepted| ⁄ Maccepted × 100%

Then ask the question the percentage on its own cannot answer: is your experimental mass higher or lower than the accepted mass, and is that the direction you would expect? Work out what a real half Atwood machine does that the ideal one in Note 6 does not, and decide which way each of those effects would push your result. A percentage with a sign attached and a mechanism behind it is worth far more than a small percentage on its own.

Additional Questions

All three are required, and all three are calculus questions — they ask you to derive things this handout has so far simply handed you. Answer them in a section of their own headed Additional Questions, in sentences and paragraphs with the working shown, never as a bare numbered list and never by restating the question before answering it. This section is worth 5 points on the rubric.

  1. AQ1 — where the uncertainty formula comes from. You obtain the total mass from the slope as MT = 1/s, and Part III handed you δMT = δs / s² without justifying it. Derive it. MT depends on one variable, so a small error in the slope produces δMT ≈ |dMT/ds| δs — differentiate MT = s−1 and show the formula follows. Then show that the fractional uncertainty is unchanged by the inversion, δM/M = δs/s, and state what the same argument gives for any quantity depending on the slope as sn. Finish by applying it to your own fitted slope and quoting MT ± δMT.
  2. AQ2 — why the quadratic coefficient is half the acceleration. You have been using a = 2 × (quadratic coefficient) as a recipe. Justify it: differentiate x(t) = x0 + v0t + ½at² twice and say what each coefficient of the fit physically is. State precisely what that rule assumes about d²x/dt², and use that to explain what actually defines a correct fit window — not "the part that looks curved," but a condition on the second derivative. Then differentiate your own fitted parabola once to get v(t), evaluate it at both ends of your window, and compare those two numbers against velocities taken straight from the raw data by central differences, v(ti) ≈ (xi+1 − xi−1) / 2Δt. They should agree. Say which of the two you would trust more, and why, given that the sensor's position noise is a few millimetres.
  3. AQ3 — the experiment you were forbidden to do. Because mass is transferred, the total stays fixed and a = (g/MT)MH exactly; da/dMH = g/MT is a constant, which is the real reason your graph is a straight line. Now work out what the other experiment would have produced. Had mass been added from outside, the cart and its load would have stayed at MC = 1048.02 g while the hanger grew, giving a(MH) = MHg / (MC + MH). Expand this about MH = 0 to second order; show the leading term is a straight line of slope g/MC and that the first correction bends it downward. Compute how far below that initial tangent the true curve falls at each of the five hanging masses used here, and find the hanging mass at which the deviation first reaches 10%. Then answer the question that matters: given the scatter you actually see in your own five accelerations, would that curvature have been visible over the range 100–500 g — or would the wrong experiment have passed for a straight line?

Prompts for your Discussion

These are not the Additional Questions. Work them into your Discussion section as part of interpreting your own results, in sentences and paragraphs. Do not limit yourself to these; add any other insight you have.

  1. Look at your intercept. In an ideal experiment it is zero, because zero force should produce zero acceleration. Is yours zero within its uncertainty? If it is not, work out what constant force would account for it, and say whether the sign of that force makes physical sense for this apparatus.
  2. What factors would make the mass from your graph differ from the mass on the scale? For each one you name, say which direction it pushes the result, and estimate its size if you can. At least one thing in this apparatus has mass, is being accelerated, and never appears anywhere in your total — find it.
  3. Refit one of your runs using a slightly different time window — a few points more at one end, a few fewer at the other. How much does the acceleration change? What does that tell you about the precision you are entitled to claim?
One thing the rubric will not accept: "human error." It explains nothing, and in a virtual lab where the data were collected by a machine it explains even less. If your percent error is under 10%, attribute it to ordinary experimental uncertainty and say so explicitly, backed by the uncertainty on your slope. If it is over 10%, you must identify specific error sources and quantify them — a named mechanism with a number attached.
★ Optional bonus — up to +3 points Harder than anything else on this page. Skipping it costs you nothing.

Your line is straight, your fit is good, and your mass is still several percent off. Two quite different mechanisms produce exactly that symptom, and this problem asks you to tell them apart.

  1. Suppose the only flaw is that something in the apparatus has mass, is accelerated, and is missing from your MT — the pulley and the string. Show that this multiplies every acceleration by one constant factor, so it changes the slope of your graph while leaving the motion exactly parabolic.
  2. Now suppose instead there is a drag force proportional to speed, so the system obeys M dv/dt = F − bv with v(0) = 0. Solve that differential equation for v(t), and integrate once more for x(t). Expand v(t) for small bt/M and show that a parabola fitted over a window of mean time t̄ returns not the true a = F/M but approximately a(1 − b t̄/M).
  3. Here is what makes it awkward: that bias is still proportional to F. On an a-against-F graph, drag therefore counterfeits an inertia excess of roughly Δm ≈ b t̄. Estimate the value of b that would account for your own percent error.
  4. Design the measurement that separates the two. One of them predicts something the other does not, and you already have the data to look for it — the third Discussion prompt above is the hint.

End by saying plainly whether your own data settle the question or are simply too noisy to. Either answer earns full credit if it is argued from the numbers.
