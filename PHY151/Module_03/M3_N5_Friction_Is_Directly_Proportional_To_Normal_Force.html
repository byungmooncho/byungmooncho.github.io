Friction Is Directly Proportional to the Normal Force

Synopsis Without friction, you can't walk. To move forward you push backward on the ground, and it is the ground's grip — friction — that pushes you forward in return: Newton's third law in action. That grip depends on just two things: the normal force pressing the surfaces together, and the surface roughness, μ. Note carefully which one: the normal force, not the weight. On a level floor they happen to be equal, which is why the two are so easily confused. And note the odd geometry — friction and the normal force are directly proportional yet perpendicular to each other: the one case where a vertical force sets the size of a horizontal one.
Before you read on — predict You drag a loaded sled across the bench at a steady speed, pulling it with a spring scale, and the scale reads some value. Now you double the mass riding on the sled and drag it again, the same way.
What does the scale read — the same, twice as much, or somewhere in between? And what happens to the coefficient of friction? Commit to an answer for both before you turn the page. You will measure it yourself.

The key relationships

Two regimes, one structure: friction is a coefficient times the normal force. The inequality in the static case matters — static friction adjusts itself up to a maximum.

  • fk= μkN kinetic friction — surfaces sliding past each other
  • fs≤ μsN static friction — grips as hard as needed, up to its limit
  • μs> μk breaking the grip is harder than keeping the slide — why wheels lock and joints creak loose
  • f=μmg level surface only — the shortcut you get by substituting N = mg
Where these hold

The first three are always true. The fourth is a shortcut, and it is only true on a level surface, because that is the only place where N = mg.

Tilt the surface and the normal force shrinks — on an incline it becomes N = mg cos θ, so the friction becomes μmg cos θ, not μmg. You will derive that in Note 7. Until then, the safe habit is to find N first, then multiply by μ, and never to write friction in terms of the mass directly.

Measure μ for yourself

A coefficient of friction is not something to look up — it is something you can measure in about ten minutes. You will drag a loaded sled across the bench with a spring scale and find μk as the slope of a graph.

  1. Set up. Put the sled on the bench with the spring scale hooked to it, the scale held horizontally.
  2. Pull at a steady speed. This part matters more than anything else in the procedure. Only when the sled moves at constant velocity is the acceleration zero, so that the forces balance and the scale reading equals the friction force exactly. If you are speeding it up, the reading is bigger than the friction and your data is wrong. Practise until you can hold it steady.
  3. Watch the scale as it breaks loose. Pull slowly from rest and note the reading at the instant the sled starts to move — then the lower, steadier reading once it is sliding. Record both.
  4. Add mass and repeat. Load the sled with five different masses, dragging steadily each time, and record the scale reading for each.

Plot the friction force against the normal force. On a level bench the normal force is just the total weight, N = mg — so the third column of your table is the x-axis and the fourth is the y-axis.

  1. Is your plot a straight line through the origin? If it is, you have just shown that friction is directly proportional to the normal force — which is the title of this note, and now it is your measurement rather than my claim.
  2. Find the slope. What are its units? The slope is μk, and the answer to the units question is why μ is just a number.
  3. Compare your two readings from step 3 — the one at the instant it broke loose, and the steady one while sliding. Which is bigger, and what does that tell you about μs and μk?
  4. Now turn the sled on its side, so it rests on a smaller face, and drag the same load again. Predict first, then measure. Does the reading change?
  5. Now check it. You doubled the mass. What happened to the scale reading, and what happened to μ?
