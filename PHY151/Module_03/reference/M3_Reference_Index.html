Module 3 Reference Pages

Web versions of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

How to use these These are reference pages, not the module notes. Each one is a self-contained walk-through of one slide deck — worked examples, figures you can look at as long as you like, and Dr. Johnson-Steigelman’s own “check yourself” questions with the reasoning spelled out. Read the note for a topic first; come here when you want the idea explained a second way, or when you are working a problem and need to see the method laid out step by step. Nothing here is graded.

Chapter 5 — Newton’s Laws of Motion

  1. 05a Forces and Newton’s First and Second Laws Friction is what stops things, not the absence of a push. Inertia, free body diagrams, and how much change in motion you get per newton — worked on a thrown football and two carts.
  2. 05b Newton’s Third Law — Action and Reaction Why plenty of equal-and-opposite pairs are not third-law pairs, and how the law supplies the extra equation that closes a two-body problem. A truck meets a car.
  3. 05c Mass and Weight Mass travels with the object; weight is a force it happens to be experiencing where it stands. Converting either way, and what the lunar rover weighed on the Moon.
  4. 05d Normal or Contact Forces The surface supplies whatever force is needed, perpendicular to itself — so the normal force does not have to point up, and is not the object’s weight.
  5. 05e Normal Forces When Something Is Accelerating A bathroom scale measures the normal force, nothing else. Riding in an elevator, what “weightlessness” actually is, and recovering an acceleration from a scale reading alone.
  6. 05f Verifying Newton’s Second Law from Experimental Data Checking the law rather than assuming it: pull the acceleration out of a fitted parabola, plot it against force, and read the mass off the slope. The method behind Note 6 and the lab.
  7. 05g Equilibrium Equilibrium is “not accelerating”, not “not moving” — six situations sorted, then the hanging-sign problem worked with two ropes at different angles.

Chapter 6 — Applications of Newton’s Laws

  1. 06a Drag Forces The first force that depends on how fast you are already going — and it goes as the square of speed. Terminal velocity, why a parachute works, and Stokes’ law for very small objects.
  2. 06b Drag Forces in Liquids · calculus In a liquid the resistance goes as v, not v², which turns Newton’s second law into a differential equation. Solved end to end by separation of variables.

Chapter 12 — Static Equilibrium and Elasticity

  1. 12a Elasticity and Springs Springs are a force that changes in a predictable way. Hooke’s law two ways, which axis the spring constant is the slope of, and the step from springs to stress and strain.
  2. 12b Deformation: Stress, Strain and Shear Solid objects are not rigid. Young’s modulus for stretching and squashing, the shear modulus for sliding one face past another, and why both are Hooke’s law with the size divided out.

One caution across the whole set Several of these pages carry a domain of validity that is easy to read past. The drag equation on 06a is a simple model — speeds below about 200 m/s, objects no more than a few metres across, and air rather than liquid doing the resisting; 06b exists because water breaks all of that. The normal-force result FN = Fg cos θ on 05d assumes gravity is the only force acting; lean on the object or accelerate the floor and 05e takes over. Hooke’s law on 12a and 12b holds only while the deformation is small enough to be reversible. A formula used outside its conditions is worse than no formula.

One gap, on purpose There is no reference page for objects on inclines or for friction on a ramp yet. Those decks are being held until Note 7 settles, so that the reference and the note agree from the start rather than being reconciled afterwards. The frictionless normal-force decomposition on an incline is on page 05d in the meantime.
