Forces and Newton’s First and Second Laws

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Everyday experience says that motion needs a cause — stop pushing and the cart stops. Galileo noticed that the stopping is the friction, not the motion, and that if you keep taking friction away the cart keeps going further. Push that to its limit and you get the opposite rule: an object does not need a force to keep moving, only to change how it is moving. Newton’s first law says that; the second law says how much change you get per newton of force.

You already know this Everything in Module 1 and Module 2. This chapter does not add new kinematics — it supplies the cause of the acceleration you have been computing all along. The constant-acceleration equations, vector components, and free-fall all get used here unchanged.

Friction is what stops things, not the absence of a push

Reducing friction lets an object slide further. Objects do eventually stop — but they stop because of the frictional force, not because motion runs out on its own. Galileo was among the first to simplify the system this way and arrive at the law of inertia.

Inertia— the tendency of something to remain as it is. In physics, the tendency of an object to keep its current state of motion.
  Inertia is directly proportional to the mass of an object.
Don’t confuse Inertia is not momentum. They are closely related and both scale with mass, but inertia is a tendency, not a quantity you compute and conserve.

Newton’s First Law — inspiration from Galileo

Two statements, one law

An object at rest remains at rest as long as no net force acts upon it.

An object moving at a constant velocity continues to move at that same velocity as long as no net force acts upon it.

Both say essentially the same thing: you change an object’s velocity by applying a net force — or, read the other way, if the net force on an object is zero, its velocity is constant. Forces cause accelerations.

Where this sits in Module 3 This is the claim Note 1 asks you to test. If a constant velocity needs no force, then no experiment done inside a smoothly moving vehicle can tell you how fast it is going — only whether it is speeding up, slowing down or turning. Velocity is relative; acceleration is not. That is why the second law reads F = ma and not F = mv.

Free body diagrams

Sketch the situation where it helps — but do not draw the picture in the free body diagram itself. Use a dot for the object and draw only the forces acting on it.

Check yourself

A projectile is in free fall. Which represents its free body diagram?

Answer: 1. “Free fall” means gravity is the only force acting. There is no forward force — the projectile’s horizontal velocity needs no force to keep it (first law), so option 2 is the classic trap. An object in free fall has exactly one non-zero component of a: the vertical one. Its horizontal speed stays constant for the whole flight while its vertical speed falls steadily through zero at the top and turns negative on the way down.
A wording flag Trevor’s deck adds here that “for the most part, the force of gravity causes the weight of an object — however, sometimes we refer to the support force as though it were the weight.” In PHY 151 we do not use “weight” that second way. The gravitational force is the weight; the support force is the normal force, and the whole point of Note 9 is that the two are equal only in the special case of no vertical acceleration.

Newton’s Second Law

The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to its mass.

Three ways to write the same law
a⃗ = F⃗netm     a⃗ = ΣF⃗m     ΣF⃗ = m a⃗

The first two are the “real” relationship — acceleration is the thing that results. The boxed third form is how we usually use it, because in a problem you normally know the mass and want the force, or know the forces and want the acceleration.

Acceleration and net force point in the same direction. Since both are vectors, the law holds component by component:

ΣFx = m ax      ΣFy = m ay      ΣFz = m az

This is the step that makes every incline, pulley and hanging-rope problem tractable: one vector equation becomes two (or three) ordinary ones.

Worked example — throwing an American football

How much force is required to throw an American football at a speed of 20.0 m/s? The mass of a regulation inflated ball is 0.400 kg, and your throwing motion accelerates it over Δx = 80.0 cm.

Newton’s second law needs an acceleration — so find one first
v2 = vo2 + 2a(x − xo)  →  v2 = 2a(Δx)
a = v22(Δx) = (20.0 m/s)22(0.80 m) = 250 m/s2
ΣF⃗ = m a⃗ = (0.400 kg)(250 m/s2) î = 100 N î

The bar over the final zero marks it as significant — three sig figs, not one. Trevor’s deck writes the direction as x̂; PHY 151 uses î ĵ k̂, matching the textbook and WebAssign, so it is written that way here.

Two carts, same force, same time

Check yourself

A cart is initially at rest on a frictionless track. You apply a force for a short time, giving the cart a final speed of v1. A second cart has twice the mass of the first. If you apply the same force for the same amount of time, what is the speed of the second cart?

  1. ¼ v1
  2. 4 v1
  3. ½ v1
  4. 2 v1
  5. v1
Answer: 3 — half. The final speed is proportional to both the force on the cart and the time over which it acts, and inversely proportional to the mass.
Why, in two columns
Cart 1  (m1 = m) Cart 2  (m2 = 2m)
F = m a1  →  a1 = F/m F = 2m a2  →  a2 = F/2m
v1 = 0 + a1t = (F/m)t v2 = 0 + a2t = (F/2m)t = ½ v1
Check yourself — the follow-up

Same two carts, same force, same amount of time. The total displacement of the heavier cart is…

  1. ¼ x1
  2. 4 x1
  3. ½ x1
  4. 2 x1
  5. x1
Answer: 3 — half again. With x = xo + voΔt + ½a(Δt)2 and both carts starting from rest at the origin, only the a differs: x2 = ½(F/2m)(Δt)2 = ½ x1. Halving the acceleration halves the displacement in the same time — the squared Δt is identical for both, so it does not change the ratio.

The first and second laws are consistent

N I: Forces cause accelerations.    N II: ΣF⃗ = m a⃗

If ΣF⃗ = F⃗net = 0, then a⃗ = 0.

If a⃗ = 0, then the velocity is constant.

If a⃗ ≠ 0, then the velocity — magnitude and direction — changes, and therefore the state of motion changes as well.

The first law is not a separate fact bolted onto the second. It is the a⃗ = 0 case of it, stated first because it is the one that contradicts everyday intuition.

No net force implies no change in velocity

Remember No acceleration does not imply no velocity. A skydiver at terminal speed has zero net force and is falling at 50 m/s.

Trevor closes the deck with two pictures of this. A falling body with air resistance has a drag force F⃗drag pointing up against a gravitational force F⃗g pointing down; once the two grow equal the net force is zero and the speed stops changing — terminal velocity. A projectile with air resistance does not trace a symmetric parabola at all: it rises less high, falls more steeply, and lands well short of the drag-free range. Both cases are taken up quantitatively on the sibling page Ch06a · Drag Forces.
