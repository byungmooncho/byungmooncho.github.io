Deformation: Stress, Strain and Shear

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Solid objects are not rigid. Push on them and they change shape — usually by far too little to see, but by an amount that is entirely predictable and, for a structural engineer, entirely the point. Three moduli cover the cases: Young’s for stretching and squashing along an axis, and shear for sliding one face past another. Both are Hooke’s law with the specimen’s size divided out.

Tension and compression

Hooke’s law, with the specimen’s size divided out
F = kΔL   →   F ∝ ALoΔL   →   F = Y ALoΔL   →   FA = Y ΔLLo
TermDefinitionUnits
StressF/A — force per unit cross-sectionN/m2 = Pa
StrainΔL/Lo — fractional change in lengthdimensionless
YYoung’s modulus — the material’s stiffnessPa

Strain is a fraction, so a 1 m rod and a 100 m cable of the same material under the same stress stretch by the same proportion, not the same distance. That is why long suspension cables sag so much more than intuition suggests.

Tension—the external force pulls; ΔL is positive
Compression—the external force pushes; ΔL is negative

Newton’s third law is what makes either possible: the external force on one end and the reaction at the support are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. Without something holding the far end, the object accelerates rather than deforming.

Shear deformation

Now apply the force along a face instead of into it, with the opposite face held fixed. The object does not get longer or shorter; it skews, the top sliding a distance Δx relative to the bottom.

Same algebra, different letter
F = kΔL  →  F ∝ ALoΔx  →  F = S ALoΔx  →  FA = S ΔxLo   i.e.   Shear stress = S × Shear strain

S is the shear modulus. The structure of the equation is identical to Young’s — only the geometry of “which way the force points relative to the face” has changed. For most materials S is roughly a third of Y: solids resist being sheared rather less than they resist being stretched.

Review

  • Stress is force per unit area; strain is the fractional deformation; the modulus is the constant relating them.
  • Young’s modulus Y governs tension and compression — force perpendicular to the face.
  • Shear modulus S governs shear — force parallel to the face.
  • All three are Hooke’s law with the specimen’s own dimensions divided out, which is what makes them properties of the material.
