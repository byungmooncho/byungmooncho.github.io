Objects on Inclines

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea An object on a ramp is a two-dimensional problem only because of how the axes are drawn. Turn the coordinate system so that x runs along the slope and y runs perpendicular to it, and the motion becomes one-dimensional: the object accelerates in x and never moves in y. The price is that gravity — which does not care how you drew your axes — must now be split into two components.
Performance Expectations By the end of this lesson, you will be able to…
  • PE1Find the parallel and perpendicular components of gravity for an object on an incline.
  • PE2Solve problems involving objects on inclines.
  • PE3Justify why the angle between the weight and the perpendicular axis is the same θ as the incline itself.
Friction on a ramp is the sibling page 06c · Friction. Everything here is the frictionless case.

Rotate the axes

On level ground you put x horizontal and y vertical because that is where the motion is. On a ramp the motion is along the ramp, so put the axes there instead:

  • the x-axis parallel to the incline;
  • the y-axis perpendicular to the incline.

Nothing physical changed. What changed is that the object now has acceleration in one axis only, and the y-equation collapses into a statement about the normal force.

The two components of gravity

F⊥ = Fg cos θ      F∥ = Fg sin θ

Cosine goes with the perpendicular component and sine with the parallel one — the opposite of what most students guess the first time. The reason is in the geometry below: the angle θ sits between the weight and the perpendicular direction, so the perpendicular piece is the adjacent side.

Locating θ

Why is the angle at the block the same as the angle at the bottom of the ramp? Because of a right triangle and the fact that angles in a triangle add to 180°.

The one-line version Rotating the axes by θ rotates every direction by θ, including “straight down”. So the weight, which was along the old y-axis, now sits at θ from the new one.

Mass on a ramp

With the components in hand, Newton’s second law is written once per axis. Nothing accelerates perpendicular to the surface — the object neither sinks into the ramp nor lifts off it — so that equation has a zero on the left.

Two directions, two equations — frictionless
x-direction (along the slope) y-direction (perpendicular)
m ax = ΣFx m ay = ΣFy
m ax = Fg∥ m ay = FN + Fg⊥
m ax = Fg sin θ 0 = FN − Fg⊥
ax = g sin θ FN = m g cos θ

The mass cancels out of the x-equation. A heavy block and a light block slide down a frictionless ramp with exactly the same acceleration — the same reason they fall at the same rate, which is the θ = 90° case of this very result.

Two sanity checks worth doing every time Set θ = 0 and the ramp is level: ax = 0 and FN = m g, as they must be. Set θ = 90° and the ramp is a cliff: ax = g and FN = 0 — free fall, with the surface touching nothing. An answer that fails either limit has a sine and a cosine swapped.

Review

  • Rotating the coordinate system lets you treat motion on an incline as a one-dimensional problem.
  • The cost is that the gravitational force has to be broken into two perpendicular components.
  • F⊥ = Fg cos θ and F∥ = Fg sin θ.
  • On a frictionless ramp, ax = g sin θ and FN = m g cos θ.
