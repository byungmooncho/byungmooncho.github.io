Normal Forces When Something Is Accelerating

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea A bathroom scale does not measure your mass, and it does not measure the Earth’s pull on you. It measures the normal force between you and the floor — nothing else. Stand still and that number happens to equal your weight. Put the same scale in an accelerating elevator and it reads something different, with your mass and the Earth both unchanged. The reading tracks acceleration, and this page is where that shows up first.

  • PE2Determine the normal force on a body when the surface it rests on is accelerating.
  • PE3Interpret a scale reading to recover the acceleration and its direction.
Where this sits in Module 3 This is Note 9 in Trevor’s words, and the far end of the module’s Galilean-relativity arc. Note 1 asks what motion you can detect from inside a closed box. This page answers it with an instrument: a scale detects acceleration, and is completely blind to how fast the elevator is travelling or which way it is going.

Standing still

A bathroom scale measures the normal force between you and the floor
ΣF⃗ = m a⃗  →  F⃗N + F⃗g = m a⃗
+FN − Fg = 0  →  +FN = +Fg

You stand on the floor and you do not accelerate, so the net force is zero and the two are equal. This is the special case, not the rule. It is also the only case in which the scale reading and the weight agree.

Trevor’s own note on vocabulary “I seldom use the term weight when working on these types of problems. I usually use the term force due to gravity, Fg.” That is the convention PHY 151 follows, and it is worth knowing that it is Trevor’s too — the looser wording that turns up on the Chapter 5 opening deck is not how he works once the numbers start.

On an incline

Put the scale on a ramp instead of level ground and the reading drops below your “normal” weight, by the factor cos θ derived on the sibling page Ch05d · Normal or Contact Forces. Same person, same planet, smaller number.

What if the floor accelerates?

Check yourself

A person is in an elevator that is accelerating upward. How does the upward normal force FN caused by the floor and acting on the person compare to the force of gravity Fg acting on the person?

  1. FN > Fg
  2. FN = Fg
  3. FN < Fg
Answer: 1. Accelerating upward means the net force points upward. Gravity is fixed, so the only force that can supply that net upward push is the floor — the normal force has to grow. This is the pressed-into-the-floor feeling as a lift starts up.
The general result
F⃗N + F⃗g = m a⃗  →  +FN − Fg = m(±a)
+FN = +Fg + m(±a)

The normal force and the force of gravity are not equal to one another if the floor and/or the person is accelerating. The sign of a carries the whole story: accelerating up adds to the reading, accelerating down subtracts from it. Note what is absent from this equation — the velocity. A lift cruising at 6 m/s upward and one cruising at 6 m/s downward give identical readings, and both match standing still.

What if the floor falls out from under you?

Free fall, worked through
+FN − Fg = −mg  →  +FN = −mg + Fg = −mg + mg
+FN = 0

Set the acceleration to −g — the lift cable has snapped, or you are in orbit, which is the same thing — and the normal force goes to exactly zero. This condition is what people call “weightlessness”. Gravity has not switched off; Fg is unchanged in every line above. What vanished is the floor’s push, and therefore the scale reading, and therefore the sensation.

A defensible piece of pedantry Trevor points out that if a textbook wants to be consistent about the word “weight” — consistent with “weightlessness”, and with what a scale reads — it should call FN the weight, and a few books do. PHY 151 does not: here weight is the force of gravity, and what the scale reads is the normal force. Both conventions are internally coherent; mixing them is what produces the confusion, so we name the two forces separately and never use one word for both.

Worked example — riding in an elevator

You are standing in an elevator on a bathroom scale. When the elevator is moving at a constant speed the scale reads 700. N. When the elevator accelerates, the reading is 800. N.

a)What is the acceleration of the elevator?
b)  Is the elevator accelerating upwards or downwards?
The constant-speed reading is what gives you the mass
m = Fgg = 700. N9.80 m/s2 = 71.4 kg
+FN − Fg = m a  →  a = FN − Fgm = 800. N − 700. N71.4 kg = +1.4 m/s2

b) Since a came out positive, and up was chosen as positive, the elevator is accelerating upwards. The constant-speed reading did double duty here: it was the calibration that turned a force into a mass.

A small inconsistency in the source Trevor’s slide divides by 70.0 kg rather than the 71.4 kg that 700. N and 9.80 m/s2 actually give — the same 700. N example on his Mass and Weight deck gets 71.4 kg. It makes no difference to the printed answer (1.43 versus 1.40, both +1.4 m/s2 to two figures), but the page uses the consistent value.
Read the answer backwards You recovered the elevator’s acceleration, and its direction, from a scale reading alone — with no window, no reference to the building, and no knowledge of how fast the lift was moving or whether it was going up or down. That is Note 1’s claim made operational: acceleration is detectable from inside; velocity is not.

Review

  • The reading on a scale is essentially the normal force between you and the floor.
  • The normal force can change. The gravitational force generally does not.
  • They are equal only when there is no acceleration along the direction the surface pushes.
  • Zero normal force is “weightlessness” — and it is a statement about the floor, not about gravity.
