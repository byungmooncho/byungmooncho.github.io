Friction

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Friction comes in two kinds and they behave quite differently. Kinetic friction, once something is sliding, has a fixed size: μkFN, and that is that. Static friction, while nothing is moving, supplies exactly whatever is needed to prevent motion — up to a ceiling of μsFN, past which the object breaks loose. The inequality is the whole subject: static friction is a ≤, kinetic friction is an =.

Two types of friction

Kinetic frictionStatic friction
Acts while the surfaces are sliding past each other. Acts while the surfaces are not sliding.
Fk = μkFN — a fixed size, set by the surfaces and the normal force. Fs ≤ μsFN — whatever is needed to hold things still, no more.
Direction: opposes the sliding. Direction: opposes the impending sliding.
The sign that does the work Almost every friction mistake is an equals sign where an inequality belongs. A block sitting still on a ramp does not have μsFN of friction on it; it has exactly as much as it needs, which is usually less. The maximum only shows up at the instant it starts to slip.

Where friction comes from

  • Very few surfaces are actually smooth.
  • Indentations in each surface become interlocked.
  • Even for very smooth surfaces, weak molecular bonds form wherever two surfaces touch.

Two things are worth reading off this table rather than memorising it. μs is larger than μk on every row but one — it is always harder to start something sliding than to keep it sliding. And Teflon on steel is the exception where they are equal, which is roughly what “non-stick” means.

μ is just a number A coefficient of friction has no units. It is a ratio of two forces, so the newtons cancel — which is why the same μ works whether you measure in newtons or pounds, and why a friction-versus-normal-force plot has a dimensionless slope.

A mass on a ramp with friction

Same rotated axes as the frictionless case. The only change to the free-body diagram is one more force, along the surface, opposing the motion or the impending motion.

How big is it? The force of friction is smaller than both FN and F∥ in the figure — but which μ to use, and whether to use an equals sign at all, depends on what the object is doing. The next three cases are the three answers.

Case 1 — while slipping

The object is already sliding down the ramp. Friction is kinetic, so it takes an equals sign.

An object slides down an incline. What is its acceleration?
y-direction (perpendicular) x-direction (along the slope)
m ay = FN + Fg⊥ m ax = F∥ + Ffk
0 = FN − Fg⊥ m ax = m g sin θ − μkFN
FN = m g cos θ m ax = m g sin θ − μkm g cos θ
— ax = g (sin θ − μk cos θ)

The masses cancel out. Just as on the frictionless ramp, the acceleration does not depend on how heavy the block is — because friction is proportional to the normal force, which is itself proportional to the mass. Both terms carry an m and both lose it.

Case 2 — sitting still at an arbitrary angle

The object is on the ramp and is not moving. What is the force of friction acting on it?

An object sits on an incline and does not move

m ax = F∥ − Ffs   and   ax = 0

⇒  Ffs = F∥ = m g sin θ

Note what did not happen: μs never appeared. The static friction here is determined entirely by the requirement that nothing moves. It is whatever balances the parallel component of gravity — and all we know about μs is that it is big enough to allow this, i.e. m g sin θ ≤ μsm g cos θ.

Case 3 — just starting to slip

Now tilt the ramp up slowly until the object breaks loose. At that exact angle, static friction is at its maximum and the object is still (just) not accelerating. That is the one moment when the inequality becomes an equality.

At what angle does it start to slip?
y-directionx-direction
0 = FN − Fg⊥ m ax = F∥ − Ffs
FN = m g cos θ 0 = m g sin θ − μsFN
— 0 = m g sin θ − μsm g cos θ
— 0 = sin θ − μs cos θ

μs cos θ = sin θ   ⇒   μs = sin θcos θ = tan θ

Both the mass and g cancel. The angle at which something slips depends on nothing but the two surfaces — not on how heavy the object is, and not on what planet you are on.

This is a measurement, not just an identity μs = tan θ turns a protractor into a coefficient-of-friction meter. Put the object on a board, tilt until it goes, read the angle, take the tangent. It is an entirely independent route to μ from the drag-it-with-a-force-sensor method, and the two agreeing is a genuinely good check.

Review

  • Friction is caused by uneven surfaces and by weak molecular bonds where they touch.
  • Kinetic friction is constant: Fk = μkFN.
  • Static friction is just what is needed, up to a maximum: Fs ≤ μsFN.
  • Slipping down a ramp: ax = g (sin θ − μk cos θ).
  • At the angle where it breaks loose: μs = tan θ.
