Drag Forces in Liquids

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea In a liquid the resistive force is proportional to v, not v2. That looks like a smaller change than it is: because the force now depends on the very quantity you are solving for, Newton’s second law stops being an algebra problem and becomes a differential equation. This page works that solution through from end to end — and the answer, an exponential approach to a limiting speed, is a shape you will meet again in circuits, in cooling, and in radioactive decay.
PHY 151 only This page is calculus-based and has no PHY 118 counterpart. The algebra-based course stops at the sibling page Ch06a · Drag Forces and its terminal-velocity result, which needs no calculus.

A different symbol, on purpose

The resistive force in a liquid
fR = −bv

Lower-case f with a subscript R, to keep it separate from the FD of drag in air. The negative sign denotes that the force is in the opposite direction to the velocity. Draw a free body diagram and insert the proper signs into Newton’s second law carefully — this is the step where the whole calculation is usually lost.

Driven through a liquid

Newton’s second law becomes a differential equation
m a⃗ = ΣF⃗ = F⃗ + f⃗R  →  ma = F − fR  →  m dvdt = F − bv

The driving force F is assumed not to depend on the speed of the object. Only the resistive force does. That assumption is what makes the equation solvable in closed form.

Solving it — separation of variables

m dv = (F − bv)dt  →  dt = m(F − bv)dv
1m∫0tdt = ∫0v1(F − bv)dv   using ∫dx/(A−Bx) = (1/B)ln(A−Bx)
−bmt = ln(F − bv) − ln(F) = ln F − bvF
e−bt/m = F − bvF = 1 − bFv  →  v(t) = Fb(1 − e−bt/m)

F is generally greater than bv, so the absolute value inside the logarithm is not “absolutely” necessary. These are the methods taught in a Differential Equations course; you are seeing one worked instance of them, not the general theory.

Read the answer At t = 0 the exponential is 1 and v = 0, as it must be. As t → ∞ the exponential dies and v → F/b — the terminal velocity, reached asymptotically rather than at any finite time. The time constant is m/b: heavier objects take longer to get there, and a thicker liquid gets them there faster.

Position from the velocity

dxdt = Fb(1 − e−bt/m)  → integrate once more →
x(t) = Fbt + mFb2(e−bt/m − 1)

A straight line at the terminal speed, minus a fixed offset that the early sluggish start cost you. The second term saturates at −mF/b2, so long after release the object is exactly that far behind where a no-drag object launched at terminal speed would be.

Sinking — the same method, a different force

An object sinking through water is acted upon by the force of gravity and the resistive force of the water. Nothing about the method changes; only the identity of the driving force does:

ma = Fg − fR  →  m dvdt = Fg − bv = mg − bv

From here you would solve just like before — the only difference is the form of the force. Substituting F → mg in the result above gives a terminal speed of mg/b directly, with no new work.

Review

  • The resistive force through water depends on the speed of the object, to the first power.
  • That makes Newton’s second law a differential equation.
  • The methods of differential equations — here, separation of variables — let us separate it and integrate.
  • The result is an exponential approach to a terminal speed F/b, with time constant m/b.
