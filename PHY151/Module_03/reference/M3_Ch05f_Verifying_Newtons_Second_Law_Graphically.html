Verifying Newton’s Second Law from Experimental Data

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea ΣF⃗ = m a⃗ is a claim about the world, so it can be checked. Apply a known constant force to a cart, record where it is as a function of time, and the shape of that curve hands you the acceleration. Do it again at eight different forces and plot acceleration against force: if the law holds, the points fall on a straight line through the origin, and the slope of that line is 1/m. You never put the cart on a balance — you weigh it with a graph.
Performance Expectations By the end of this lesson, you will be able to…
  • PE1Use experimental position-versus-time data to extract an acceleration.
  • PE2Plot acceleration against driving force and interpret the slope.
  • PE3Determine an object’s mass from trendline data alone.
This is the analysis method behind Note 6 and the Newton’s-second-law lab.

Step 1 — plot position against time

Imagine an experiment where you apply a constant force to an object and measure position as a function of time. Under a constant force the acceleration is constant, so the kinematics you already have says the curve must be a parabola:

Reading the acceleration off a fitted parabola
x = xo + vot + ½at2   ↔   y = c + bt + at2 fit

Fit a quadratic to the data and match it term by term against the kinematic equation. The coefficient of t2 is ½a, so the acceleration is twice the quadratic coefficient. That one line is the whole trick.

Step 2 — compare the plots at different forces

Run it again at several forces. The curves look nearly identical — but the y-axis has a different scale on each. At a larger force the object is moving farther in the same amount of time.

A graph-reading habit worth keeping Two plots of the same shape are not two plots of the same thing. Always read the axis scale before reading the curve. This is the single most common way to misread a lab plot.

Why the straight line is the point
F = ma  →  a = 1mF   compared with   y = (slope)x
→  slope = 1m   →   m = 1slope

Plotting a on the vertical axis against F on the horizontal is a deliberate choice. It puts the measured quantity on y and the controlled one on x, and it makes the unknown you actually want — the mass — fall out of the slope.

Step 4 — determine the mass

Trendline: y = 0.420x + 0.001
m = 1slope = 10.420 (m/s2)/N = 2.38 kg

The units do the checking for you: N ÷ (m/s2) = kg m/s2 ÷ m/s2 = kg. If the slope’s units do not reduce to an inverse mass, the axes were plotted the wrong way round.

Two things the fits quietly tell you The intercept of the trendline is +0.001, not zero — but it is a thousandth of the largest acceleration, so the line passes through the origin as well as the data can show. That is the real result: a is proportional to F, with no offset. And each position fit has a negative linear term and a small positive constant: the cart was still sitting at rest when the clock started and only began moving a second or so in, which shifts the parabola’s vertex off t = 0. The quadratic coefficient is unaffected, which is exactly why this method is robust.
A question worth carrying into the lab If your acceleration–force line does not pass through the origin — if you need a finite force before you get any acceleration at all — what might that mean? Friction is the usual answer, and the intercept measures it.
