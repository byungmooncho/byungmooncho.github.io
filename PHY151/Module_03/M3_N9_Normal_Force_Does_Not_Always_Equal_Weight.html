Normal Force Does Not Always Equal Weight

Synopsis Every scale on Earth measures the normal force — how hard the surface pushes back on you — not your true weight. The two agree only when you are not accelerating. In this note you will alter a scale's reading with your own motion, record the normal force with a Vernier force platform while riding an elevator, and explain every feature of that graph with Newton's second law.
Before you read on — predict You step on a bathroom scale in an elevator on the second floor and ride down to the ground floor, where the elevator comes to a stop. During that ride down, does the scale ever read more than your true weight? Write down yes or no, and one sentence saying why, before you turn the page. You will check it against the recorded graph.
Performance expectations
  1. Apply ΣF = ma to an object on a scale, with up as positive, and solve for the normal force.
  2. State, for a given phase of an elevator ride, whether the scale reads more than, less than, or equal to mg, and which way the acceleration points.
  3. Read a normal-force-versus-time graph and label each plateau with the motion that produced it.
  4. Explain why a scale reads zero in free fall.
  5. Determine the normal force on an object on a level surface when an applied force is directed at an angle.

The four experiments

  1. Spring scale: hang a mass, then accelerate the scale up and down. Watch the reading at every phase — starting, moving, stopping.
  2. Digital scale, tilted: the reading drops as the angle steepens. Your free-body diagram explains why — the scale reads a force component, not a mass.
  3. Bathroom scale squats: stand still, then squat down and up. Which way does the needle deflect at each phase of the motion, and what does that tell you?
  4. The elevator ride — your data: you ride with the Vernier force platform under your feet, starting from the second floor and descending. It traces normal force vs. time for the whole trip. Every bump and dip in that graph is Newton's second law speaking, and you will plot and tabulate it yourself below.

The key relationships

All of these come from one idea: apply ΣF = ma to the object standing on the scale, with up as positive.

  • N=mg at rest or at constant velocity — the only case where the scale shows true weight
  • N=m(g+a) a is signed: positive when it points up (scale reads heavy), negative when it points down (light)
  • N=0 when a=−g free fall — apparent weightlessness, the scale reads zero
  • N=mg± F⁢sin⁡θ level surface, no vertical acceleration, applied force at angle θ: plus when you push down on it, minus when you pull up

The rule that explains the whole graph

Remember this one

The sign of a is set by whether you are speeding up or slowing down — not by which way you are moving.

An elevator going down is not automatically "lighter." It reads light while it is picking up speed downward, and heavy while it is slowing to a stop at the bottom, because slowing a downward motion means accelerating upward. That is why one trip produces four different plateaus rather than one, and why two of them are indistinguishable from the other two.

So: your prediction. Look at what you recorded on the way down. The reading does rise above your true weight — during the slowdown at the bottom. Anyone who predicted "no, going down always feels lighter" was reading the direction of motion instead of the direction of the acceleration.

Free fall: the scale reads zero

If the cable is cut, the elevator, you and the scale all fall with a = −g together. Put that into N = m(g + a) and you get N = 0. In your own words from class: the reading is zero because you cannot push on the floor — the floor is falling away from you exactly as fast as you are. Your mass has not changed and gravity has not switched off; only the contact force has gone.
