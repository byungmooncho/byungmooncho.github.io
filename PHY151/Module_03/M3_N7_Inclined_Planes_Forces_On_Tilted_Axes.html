Inclined Planes — Forces on Tilted Axes

Synopsis A cart on a level rail stays put. Raise one end and it runs away down the ramp. Nothing about that is surprising — we have all ridden a bike up a hill and enjoyed the trip back down, and we all know gravity is responsible. What is worth asking is how much of gravity gets through. An incline is free fall with the gravity turned down, and the dial is the angle. That is exactly why Galileo used ramps: they slow gravity to a speed a person with a water clock can actually measure.
Before you read on — predict A large adult and a small child each sit on a sled at the top of the same snowy hill, and both are released at the same instant.
Do they slide down at the same rate, or does one accelerate harder? Do they reach the bottom together, or does one arrive first? Write down your answer and one sentence of reasoning before you read on. Galileo was the first person to settle this by measurement rather than argument.
Performance expectations
  1. Draw the free-body diagram for an object on an incline, with the axes tilted to the slope, and say why the normal force needs no resolving while the weight does.
  2. Show why the angle inside the force triangle equals the incline angle.
  3. Resolve the weight into components along and perpendicular to the surface, and derive FN = mg cos θ.
  4. Derive a = g sin θ for a frictionless incline, and explain why the mass does not appear in it.
  5. Check the 0° and 90° limits against the level-surface and free-fall results.
  6. Determine the hanging mass that holds an object in equilibrium on a frictionless incline.
  7. Derive and use a = g(sin θ − μ cos θ) when friction acts, and interpret a negative result.

Movement one · the frictionless incline

Watch the normal force shrink

Start with the object on the ramp and tilt it, step by step. Watch two things: what happens to the normal force, and what happens to the friction that goes with it. The weight, meanwhile, does not move at all — it points straight down at every angle and never changes size.

  1. At 90 degrees you are back to two forces. Why? Draw it. At that angle the surface is irrelevant to the motion — how would you describe what the object is doing?

Tilt the axes, not the physics

The rule the whole note rests on

ΣFx = max   and   ΣFy = may

Two directions, two equations, and they are yours to choose

Newton's second law is applied once in each of two perpendicular directions, and the two are independent — what the forces do along x has no bearing on what they do along y. N3 got away with a single equation every time because every force in it lay along one line. That was the special case, not the general one.

Nothing says the two directions have to be horizontal and vertical. Any two perpendicular directions will do, and the law comes out the same in all of them. That freedom is the only trick in this note: choose them along the slope, and the normal force lands entirely on one axis with nothing to resolve.

Splitting a slanted vector into two perpendicular pieces is the resolution from Module 2 — the same triangle, the same sine and cosine, applied to a force instead of a displacement. If that machinery is rusty, repair it there rather than here; everything below assumes it.

There is nothing new on an incline. It is the same vector analysis and the same kinematics as everywhere else. The one move worth making is to define the axes along the plane — x parallel to the surface, y perpendicular to it. You do not have to. It just means only one vector needs resolving instead of two.

Two things stay true at every angle, and they are the whole reason the geometry works: the weight always points straight down, and the normal force always points 90° away from the surface. Which means that on any nonzero incline they are no longer opposite each other, and no longer the same size.

  1. Which angle in the force triangle equals θ? Mark it, and show why it is the same as the incline angle.
  2. Write the component of Fg perpendicular to the surface, and the component along it, both in terms of Fg and θ.
  3. There is no net force along y. Why? Use that to write FN in terms of Fg and θ.

The key relationships

  • FN=mg cos⁡θ the object stays on the surface, so ΣFy = 0 — true at any angle, with or without friction
  • Fg,x= mgsin⁡θ the down-slope pull — the only part of the weight that drives the motion
  • a=gsin⁡θ frictionless only. Note what is missing: the mass
Where these hold

The first two hold on any incline. The third assumes a frictionless surface — it is what you get from ΣFx = mg sin θ = ma when nothing opposes the slide. Movement two puts friction back in and the expression changes.

And from Note 5: friction is always f = μFN. On this page FN is no longer mg, so f is no longer μmg — the level-surface shortcut stops working here. Find FN first, then multiply by μ.

  1. The object slides down a frictionless ramp. Why does it accelerate at all?
  2. Use Newton's second law to show that a = g sin θ. Is this larger or smaller than the free-fall acceleration?
  3. Use that equation to explain why the object is in free fall when the incline is vertical. How does that square with your physical intuition?

Back to the sled

Look at a = g sin θ and notice what is not in it. There is no m. The adult and the child accelerate identically and arrive together — for the same reason everything falls at the same rate, which you established in Note 4: the larger mass is pulled harder, and is correspondingly harder to accelerate, and the two effects cancel exactly.

The angle is the only dial. At θ = 0, sin θ = 0 and nothing moves. At θ = 90°, sin θ = 1 and a = g — free fall, with the surface gone. Everything in between is free fall turned down by a factor of sin θ. Galileo's ramps were a way of dividing gravity by a known number until it was slow enough to time by hand.

Holding it still — the measurement

Now connect the object over a smooth pulley to a hanging mass, chosen so the object does not move.

  1. Which component of Fg must the hanging weight cancel for the object to be in equilibrium? Explain why it is that one and not the whole weight.
  2. The object has a mass of 2 kg and the incline is at 30°. What hanging mass holds it still? Work it out first — then set it up and confirm your answer by measurement.

Movement two · now put friction back

A real ramp

Nothing above is wrong; it is just frictionless. On a real surface the slide is opposed, and you already know by how much — Note 5 gave you f = μFN, and this note has just told you what FN is on a slope.

Perpendicular to the surface, nothing has changed: ΣFy = FN − mg cos θ = 0. Along the surface there are now two forces instead of one: ΣFx = mg sin θ − f = ma. Substitute f = μFN = μmg cos θ, cancel the mass from every term, and:

  • a=g( sin⁡θ− μcos⁡θ ) sliding down a real incline — still no mass in it
Worked — rubber on ice, μk = 0.1
θ = 30°a = 9.8 (0.500 − 0.1 × 0.866)= +4.05 m/s²
θ = 5°a = 9.8 (0.087 − 0.1 × 0.996)= −0.12 m/s²

That negative sign is not an arithmetic slip — it is the physics. A negative acceleration down the slope means the slope is not steep enough to start the object moving at all. The expression is telling you the object stays where it is, and the angle where it changes its mind is the angle at which sin θ finally beats μ cos θ.

The forces are drawn to one absolute scale in newtons, so every arrow answers every slider. Watch three things. The weight arrow never changes as you tilt; the normal force arrow starts at the weight and shrinks with cos θ, and the friction arrow shrinks in step with it. The mass slider moves both bars and leaves the acceleration exactly where it was. And below the slip angle the acceleration is negative, which is the equation telling you the block stays put.

Where this came from, and where it goes. The normal force stopped equalling the weight back in Note 4, on a level surface with an angled push, and again in Note 9, in a moving lift. Here it stops for a third reason — geometry. Same equation each time: find FN from ΣF = ma in the direction the object does not accelerate, then use it.
