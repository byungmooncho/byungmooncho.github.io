Phenomena Illustrating the Laws of Motion

Synopsis This note runs backwards from the others. There is no equation to reach and nothing to derive. You watch four things happen, write down what you notice before you know what to call it, and only then ask which of Newton’s laws is doing the work. Two of the four you will be able to settle today. Two of them you will not — and knowing exactly why you cannot settle them yet is worth as much as settling them.
Before you read on — predict A penny is sitting on a card, and the card is resting across the mouth of a cup. Using one finger, and without touching the penny, get the penny into the cup. Write down the method you would try, and what you expect the penny to do, before you turn the page.
Performance expectations
  1. Record an observation before explaining it — what moved, how it moved, and what changed — without reaching for vocabulary you have not justified.
  2. Name the object of interest in a demonstration, and explain why the choice of object decides which forces appear in the free-body diagram.
  3. Identify which of Newton’s laws accounts for a demonstrated outcome, and state what you would see instead if the force in question were absent.
  4. Distinguish a phenomenon you can account for with the tools you have from one you cannot, and say which tool is missing.

How to work a phenomenon

Physics does not begin with an equation. It begins with someone noticing something and refusing to let it go. So for each demonstration, take the observation first, in plain words, and only afterwards ask the four questions below. They are the same four questions every time, and the order matters: the object of interest comes first, because every force you go on to draw is a force on that object. Choose a different object and you get a different, equally correct, diagram.

The four questions

object → motion → energy → force

Phenomenon 1 — a penny into a cup

Flick the card

Set it up, make your prediction, then flick the card sharply from the side with one finger. Do it slowly first, then fast, and notice that the two do not give the same result.

  1. Write at least three things you notice and three things you wonder. No explanations yet.
AskYour answer
Object of interestWhat is making the interesting motion?
The motionSpeeding up, slowing down, or no change? Straight or not?
EnergyMore or less energy in the object? Where did it come from?
Force(s)Which force, on which body, and what is it doing?
  1. The only thing touching the penny is the card, and the card is only in contact with it for a few thousandths of a second. What force acts on the penny during that time, and roughly how large an effect can it have?
  2. Now answer the question the demonstration asks: why does the penny not go with the card? Name the law.
  3. Predict: if the card and the penny were glued together, what would happen? What if the card were made of ice and the penny sat in a puddle of oil?
What this one establishes

An object with no unbalanced force on it keeps doing what it was doing. The penny was at rest and stays at rest, so it is left behind while the card goes. Friction from the card is a force on the penny, but it acts for such a short time, and is so small compared with the penny’s resistance to being moved, that it barely shifts it. Flick slowly and you extend the contact time — and then the penny does come along.

That resistance to having its motion changed is the penny’s inertia, and it is what Newton’s first law is about. Once the card is gone, the penny is unsupported and does the other thing it has always done: it falls.

Phenomenon 2 — pushing off on a trolley

Why can you not move without pushing off something?

Sit on the trolley, well clear of the wall, and try to get yourself moving. No feet on the floor, no grabbing anything. Then do it again with your hands on the wall.

  1. Three things you notice, three things you wonder.
AskYour answer
Object of interestWhat is making the interesting motion?
The motionSpeeding up, slowing down, or no change? Straight or not?
EnergyMore or less energy in the object? Where did it come from?
Force(s)Which force, on which body, and what is it doing?
  1. You are the object of interest. Which force in the picture accelerates you? Be precise about which body it acts on.
  2. You pushed the wall and the wall pushed back with the same size force. The wall did not move. Explain that without saying the forces were unequal.
  3. Now change the object of interest to the wall. Which forces appear in its diagram? Does your answer to the previous question change?
  4. Push harder. What happens to your acceleration, and what does that tell you about the form of the second law?
  5. Predict: on a perfectly frictionless floor with nothing within reach, could you get yourself moving at all? What if you were holding a heavy backpack?
What this one establishes

Forces come in pairs. If you push the wall, the wall pushes you, equally hard and in the opposite direction — and the two forces sit on two different bodies, so they never cancel each other. The one that appears in your free-body diagram is the wall’s push on you, and that is the force that accelerates you.

You cannot get yourself moving without something to push against, because the only way to get a force on yourself is to exert one on something else. This is why the choice of object of interest is not a formality: pick the wall instead of yourself and every arrow in the diagram changes.
