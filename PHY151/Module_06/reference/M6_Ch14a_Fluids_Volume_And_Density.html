Fluids, Volume and Density

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea A fluid is not a state of matter — it is a behaviour. Anything whose molecules have no fixed positions relative to each other flows, and that one property puts liquids and gases in the same chapter. Before any of the fluid physics works, two pieces of bookkeeping have to be solid: the volume of a right prism, and the density that ties volume to mass. Most of the arithmetic that goes wrong in this module goes wrong in a volume conversion.
You already know this Mass and weight from Module 3, and unit conversion from Module 0. Nothing on this page needs forces yet — it is the vocabulary and the measurement the rest of the module stands on.

What counts as a fluid

Trevor's definition is deliberately about the molecules, not about the everyday categories:

Fluids are substances in which the molecules do not have fixed positions relative to each other.

That is the whole test. It says nothing about being wet, and nothing about being a liquid — which is why air is a fluid and ice is not.

Why “incompressible” is worth marking now Every result later in this module — pressure growing linearly with depth, the continuity equation, Bernoulli's equation — assumes the density does not change as the fluid moves or is squeezed. For liquids that is close to exact. For gases it is an approximation, and the places it fails are where the fluid speeds approach the speed of sound.

Volume of a right prism

A right prism is any solid with a constant cross-section: a box, a cylinder, a hexagonal rod. For all of them the volume is the area of the base times the height, and the box and the cylinder are the same formula wearing different clothes.

Volume of any right prism
V = ℓ × w × h      V = πr² × h
V = Abase × h     units: m × m × m = m³

The boxed line is the one to remember. The other two are what it becomes once you know the shape of the base.

Trevor's symbol warning Volume, velocity and voltage all want the letter V, and this module uses two of the three in the same equation. On these pages volume is an upright capital V and speed is a lower-case italic v. Trevor writes volume with a doubled-stroke 𝕍 on his slides for the same reason. Whatever you choose in your own working, choose something, and keep it.

Volume in the metric system

The SI unit is the cubic metre, and it is far too big for anything you will measure in a laboratory. A cubic metre of water is a tonne. So the working units are the cubic centimetre and the litre, and the two are linked by a cube ten centimetres on a side.

The three equivalences, and the trap under them
1 cm³ = 1 cc = 1 mL
1000 cm³ = 1000 mL = 1 L
1 m³ = 106 cm³ = 106 mL = 1000 L

Trevor asks the last line as two questions on the slide: how many millilitres in a cubic metre, and how many litres. A metre is 100 cm, so a cubic metre is 100 × 100 × 100 = 106 cubic centimetres — a million millilitres, which is a thousand litres.

Converting with volumes

Both of Trevor's worked examples are here in full, because between them they cover the only two shapes this arithmetic takes.

Example 1 — a box in litres

What is the volume, in litres, of a rectangular prism with sides of 1.0 cm, 4.0 cm and 9.0 cm?

V = ℓ × w × h = 1.0 cm × 4.0 cm × 9.0 cm = 36 cm³
V = 36 cm³ × 1 mL1 cm³ × 1 L1000 mL = 36 L1000 = 0.036 L

Nothing here is squared or cubed, because the conversion is already written in terms of a cubic centimetre. That is what makes this the easy case.

Example 2 — cubic metres to cubic centimetres

How many cm³ are in 2.45 m³?

V = 2.45 m³ × 100 cm1 m × 100 cm1 m × 100 cm1 m
V = 2.45 m³ × 1 000 000 cm³1 m³ = 2.45 × 106 cm³

Trevor writes the conversion factor out three times on purpose. A cubic metre is three metres multiplied together, so the length conversion has to be applied three times — take care of all of the dimensions. Using 100 once gives an answer 10 000 times too small, and it is the single commonest arithmetic error in this module.

Density

Density is how much mass is packed into a given volume. It is the property that decides, in the next three pages, whether something floats, how fast pressure builds with depth, and how hard a fluid is to accelerate.

Mass density
ρ = MV     units: kg/m³
ρH₂O = 1000 kgm³ = 1 gcm³

Those two numbers are the same density written in two unit systems, and being fluent in both is most of the arithmetic in this module. One litre of water at 4 °C has a mass of one kilogram — which is where the kilogram came from, before it was redefined.

The range is the thing to notice Gases and solids are separated by roughly a factor of 104: air is 1.2, gold is 19 320. That gap is why the buoyant force of air on an everyday object is usually ignored, and why mercury — a liquid denser than most metals — keeps turning up in pressure measurements.

Review

Fluid— molecules with no fixed positions relative to each other. Both liquids and gases are fluids.
Right prism— V = Abase × h, whatever the base looks like.
Volume units— 1 cm³ = 1 mL; 1000 cm³ = 1 L; 1 m³ = 106 cm³. Convert all three dimensions.
Density— ρ = M/V, in kg/m³.
Water— 1000 kg/m³ = 1 g/cm³ at 4 °C. The anchor for every other density.
