Buoyancy and Archimedes' Principle

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Put something in a fluid and it has to push some of that fluid out of the way. The fluid pushes back, and the size of the push is the weight of exactly what was displaced — no more, no less, and nothing to do with what the object is made of. Everything else on this page is that one sentence applied: whether a thing floats, how deep it sits when it does, and why a rock feels lighter underwater.
You already know this ρ = M/V and the density table from the first page of this module; ΣF⃗ = 0 and ΣF⃗ = ma⃗ from Module 3; and the fact that pressure grows with depth, which is the mechanism underneath everything here.

Archimedes' principle

The buoyant force on an object is equal to the weight of the fluid that it displaces.
The buoyant force
FB = mf g      FB = ρfVfg

Read the subscripts carefully: ρf is the density of the fluid and Vf is the volume of fluid displaced. Neither is a property of the object. The buoyant force depends on the fluid, not the object.

Trevor asks

Will the buoyant force be larger or smaller in a dense fluid like mercury, compared with water?

Larger — by a factor of 13.5. Only ρf changed, and mercury is 13 546 kg/m³ against water's 1000. Displace the same volume and you displace 13.5 times the weight. This is why a block of lead, which sinks without hesitation in water, floats in mercury.

How high something floats

A floating object has found the depth at which it displaces exactly its own weight of fluid. That condition fixes how much of it sits below the surface, and the answer turns out to depend on nothing but the two densities.

Equilibrium for a floating object
ΣF⃗ = 0  →  F⃗B + F⃗g = 0
ρfVfg − mog = 0  →  ρfVfg = ρoVog
ρfVf = ρoVo  →  ρf ℓw hs = ρo ℓw h  →  ρfhs = ρoh
hshobj = ρobjρfl

The fraction submerged is the ratio of the densities. Ice at 920 kg/m³ in seawater at 1030 sits 89 % under — the iceberg figure, derived rather than quoted. Note also what cancelled: g, the base area, and every detail of the shape.

Sink, hover, or rise

Take the same two forces and drop the assumption that they balance. Whichever is bigger decides which way the object accelerates, and the comparison is again one of densities.

The general case
ΣF⃗ = ma⃗  →  F⃗B + F⃗g = ma⃗
ρfVfg − mog = ma  →  ρfVfg − ρoVog = ma

For a fully submerged object Vf = Vo, so the sign of the acceleration is simply the sign of ρf − ρo. Denser than the fluid, it sinks; less dense, it rises; equal, it stays put. Floating is the special case where rising stops because part of the object has left the fluid and Vf has stopped growing.

Free-body diagrams with a string

If you are suspending an object, make sure every force gets into the diagram. The one students drop is the buoyant force, and it is the whole reason the object feels lighter.

Trevor asks

Why do we usually ignore the buoyant force of air?

Because air is about a thousand times less dense than the things we weigh in it. From the density table: air is 1.2 kg/m³, water 1000, aluminium about 2700. The buoyant force on a solid object in air is ρair/ρobj of its weight — well under a tenth of one per cent — which is smaller than the error in most measurements. It is not zero, and it is exactly why a helium balloon rises.
Things to note — what this analysis left out Trevor closes the deck with three honest caveats, all of them about the difference between the model and a real tank of water.
  • No drag force was considered. Every force diagram here is for an object at rest or just starting to move.
  • Most fluids bring a moving object to terminal velocity quickly — liquids especially. A sinking stone does not keep accelerating.
  • Many objects bob at the surface, because as parts of the object move in and out of the fluid the displaced volume changes, and with it the ratio of the two forces.

Review

Archimedes— the buoyant force equals the weight of the displaced fluid.
Buoyant force— FB = ρfVfg. Fluid density, displaced volume — no property of the object appears.
Floating— hs/hobj = ρobj/ρfl. The fraction submerged is the density ratio.
Sink or rise— fully submerged, compare ρobj with ρfl. That is the whole test.
Apparent weight— the string or scale carries Fg − FB. The weight itself is unchanged.
