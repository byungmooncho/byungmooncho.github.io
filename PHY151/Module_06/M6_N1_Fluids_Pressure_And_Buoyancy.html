Fluids, Pressure and Buoyancy

SynopsisEverything so far has been about solid objects — a block, a cart, a marble — things that keep their shape and that you can point at.  A fluid does not keep its shape.  Pour water into a different glass and it becomes a different shape; it is still the same water.  So the questions we have been asking need rephrasing.  “What force acts on it?” becomes awkward when the thing you are pushing spreads out under your hand.

The rephrasing is this: instead of force, ask about force per unit area — pressure.  Instead of mass, ask about mass per unit volume — density.  Both replace a quantity that depends on how much of the stuff you happened to scoop up with one that does not.  A litre of water and a swimming pool of water have very different masses and the same density, and that is exactly why density is the useful number.

With those two, the whole of fluid statics follows from one idea: the fluid above you has weight, and it is pressing down on you.  Go deeper and there is more of it above you, so the pressure rises.  That single sentence gives you Pascal's principle, the hydraulic jack that lifts a car with one hand, and Archimedes' principle — why a steel ship floats while the same steel, melted into a lump, goes straight to the bottom.

What a fluid is, and why density replaces mass

A fluid is anything that flows — anything whose molecules are free to move past one another.  That takes in liquids and gases, which is worth saying out loud, because air behaving like a fluid is what makes half the examples in Note 2 work.

Water is the reference for everything in this note.  If a substance has a density above 1000 kg/m³ it sinks in water, and below it, it floats.  You will see in a moment that this is not a coincidence or a rule to memorise — it drops out of Archimedes' principle.

Pressure — force spread over an area

Push on a fluid and the push spreads out.  What matters to the fluid is not how hard you pushed but how hard you pushed per unit of area.

P = F/A  —  F in N, A in m², so P is in N/m², which has its own name: the pascal, Pa.

Read the fraction and the two ways to get a big pressure fall straight out of it: make F big, or make A small.

A poke with your fingertip does not

Where the pressure in a fluid comes from

Nothing is pushing on a glass of water sitting on the table, and yet the pressure at the bottom of it is higher than the pressure at the top.  The reason is the only one available: the water above has weight, and it is resting on the water below.

Two separate facts, and keeping them apart is most of this section.  Pressure depends on height — that is the gap between the two red arrows in the left cylinder.  A change in pressure does not — press on the top and every point in the fluid rises by the same ΔP, as on the right.

Building the depth formula from the weight above.  Take a column of fluid of height h sitting on an area A.  The extra pressure at the bottom is that column's weight divided by that area — nothing more:

P = weight above⁄area = mg/A
and m = ρV, so P = ρVg/A = ρ(V/A)g
but V/A is just the height of the column[m³] ÷ [m²] = [m]
P = ρhg  —  the pressure the fluid itself adds, at depth h

Notice what is not in that result: the area cancelled.  A narrow pipe and a wide lake give the same pressure at the same depth.  Shape does not matter; only depth does.

One more thing is sitting on top of the water: the atmosphere.  So the absolute pressure at a depth is what the fluid adds plus what the air was already contributing.

Pabs = P0 + ρhg  with  Patm = 101 325 Pa at sea level
How deep do you have to go in water before the pressure is two atmospheres?Your working

Two atmospheres means the water has to supply one atmosphere on its own, on top of the air already there:

ρhg = Patm  →  h = Patm / ρg = 101 325 / (1000 × 9.81) = 10.3 m

About ten metres of water is one extra atmosphere, and every further ten metres adds another.  That is the number divers carry in their heads.  It is also why a water barometer would need to be eleven metres tall, while a mercury one — 13.6 times denser — is 760 mm.

In your guided notes: the derivation from mg/A down to ρhg, with the line showing V/A = height, then Pabs = P0 + ρhg and the 10 m result.
For PHY 151 — with calculus

The same result, as a differential equation.  Take a slab of fluid of thickness dy at height y.  The pressure difference across it supports the slab's own weight, which gives

dP/dy = −ρgminus, because pressure falls as you go up

Integrate with ρ constant and you recover P = P0 − ρgy, which is the formula above with the sign absorbed into “depth” instead of “height”.  Written this way it is obvious what the constant-density assumption was doing: for a gas, ρ is itself a function of y, the integral no longer collapses to a product, and you get an atmosphere whose pressure falls off roughly exponentially rather than linearly.

  1. Integrate dP/dy = −ρg from the surface down to depth d and confirm you get P0 + ρgd.
  2. If instead ρ = ρ0e−y/H, carry out the integral and say what P(y) looks like.

Pascal's principle, and the hydraulic jack

The right-hand cylinder in the figure above is Pascal's principle: a change in pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of it.  Undiminished is the word that matters — the increase does not fade with distance.  The pressure itself still depends on height, but the change does not.

That is a statement about fluids.  It is also a machine.

The two piston faces are drawn to scale — the wide one has four times the area of the narrow one.  Because both surfaces sit at the same level, the depth term is identical on each side and drops out, leaving P1 = P2 exactly.  (The force arrows are schematic.)
P1 = P2because the two surfaces are at the same height
F1/A1 = F2/A2  →  F1 = F2 A1/A2

Small force on a small area becomes a large force on a large area.  That is a car jack, a hydraulic press, and the brakes on every vehicle you have ever been in.

What you do not get is free energy.  The small piston has to travel a long way to move the big one a little — the volume of fluid pushed out of one side has to arrive in the other.  Force is multiplied; work is not.

Archimedes' principle — why the ship floats

Put something into a fluid and it has to shove some of that fluid out of the way.  The fluid pushes back, and Archimedes' principle says exactly how hard:

FB = the weight of the fluid displaced by the submerged part
FB = Ww,dis = Mwg = ρwVdisg

Read the subscripts carefully.  ρw is the density of the fluid, and Vdis is the volume of fluid pushed aside.  Neither one is a property of the object.

Same steel, same mass, same water — and the arrows are drawn to the same scale in both panels.  The hull encloses a large volume of air, so it displaces a large volume of water before it is anywhere near submerged.  The lump displaces only its own small volume.  What changed is not the steel.  It is the volume displaced.

The floating condition.  A floating object is in equilibrium, so the buoyant force exactly carries its weight:

Fg,ship = FB  →  Mshipg = Mwg
ρsVsg = ρwVdisg  →  ρsVs = ρwVdis

So a thing floats when it can displace its own weight of water before it goes under.  A hull full of air can.  A solid lump of the same steel cannot — by the time it is fully submerged it has displaced only its own volume of water, which weighs about an eighth of what the steel does, and down it goes.

And now the rule from the first section is not a rule any more, it is a consequence: for a solid object, Vdis = Vs, so the condition collapses to ρs < ρw.  Denser than water sinks; less dense floats.

In your guided notes: both panels of the figure with the forces marked, and the derivation down to ρsVs = ρwVdis.  Then answer your prediction from the top of the page.

Weighing something twice to find its density

Here is the part of Archimedes that earns its keep in the laboratory, and it is what you will do in the Buoyancy Lab.  Hang a lump of metal from a spring scale and read the tension.  Then lower it into water, fully submerged, and read the tension again.  It weighs less — not because its weight changed, but because the water is now helping to hold it up.

The two green arrows are identical lengths, because the weight has not changed.  What the scale reports is the apparent weight, and the difference between the two readings is the buoyant force: 20 − 17 = 3 N.
From the two scale readings, find the density of the waterYour working
Tair = 20 N, Twater = 17 N

Submerged, the metal is still in equilibrium — three forces now instead of two:

Twater + FB = Tair  →  FB = Tair − Twater= 20 − 17 = 3 N

Now write each side in terms of densities.  The metal is fully submerged, so the water it displaces has exactly the metal's own volume — call it V, and it is the same V on both sides:

ρwVg = FB = Tair (1 − Twater/Tair)
Tair = the weight of the metal = Mmg = ρmVg
ρwVg = ρmVg (1 − Tw/Ta)
ρw = ρm (1 − Tw/Ta)V and g both cancel

Two scale readings and one known density give you the other one.  With the numbers above, 1 − 17/20 = 0.15, so ρw = 0.15 ρm — and if the water is the known 1000 kg/m³, the metal comes out at about 6700 kg/m³.  Run it the other way — known water, unknown sample — and you have measured the density of an object without ever measuring its volume.  That is the whole point: volume is the hard thing to measure on an irregular object, and this method never needs it.

In your guided notes: the two free-body diagrams side by side, FB = Tair − Twater, and the derivation down to ρw = ρm(1 − Tw/Ta).

What changed is the volume of water displaced, not the steel.  A hull is mostly air, so it pushes aside a large volume of water — and therefore a large weight of water — long before it is submerged.  Melted into a lump, the same steel can only ever displace its own small volume, which weighs far less than the steel does.  If you said “the shape changed”, you were right, but the reason shape matters is that shape sets Vdis.
