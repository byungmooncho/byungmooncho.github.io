Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Kinetic energy is not a new postulate. Take the constant-acceleration equation you have had since Module 1, multiply through by the mass, and the combination ½mv2 falls out on its own — with the work on the other side. That derivation is the whole lesson: net work is what changes kinetic energy, and the quantity worth naming is whatever the algebra hands you.
You already know this v2 = vo2 + 2a(x − xo) from Module 1, ΣF⃗ = m a⃗ from Module 3, friction as Ff = μFN, and work from the sibling page Ch07a. Nothing else is assumed.

The work–energy theorem

Push an object with a constant force along a horizontal frictionless surface. Nothing exotic happens — it speeds up. The question is what the work you did turned into.

Five lines, and kinetic energy appears
v2 = vo2 + 2a(x − xo)
v2 − vo2 = 2a(x − xo)
½v2 − ½vo2 = a(x − xo)
½m v2 − ½m vo2 = m a(x − xo)
½m v2 − ½m vo2 = F(x − xo)
½m v2 − ½m vo2 = W

Line 4 is the only step with an idea in it: multiply both sides by the mass, and the right-hand side becomes ma times a displacement — which is force times displacement, which is work. The left-hand side is then forced to be an energy, and the thing it is made of gets a name.

The two statements
Kinetic energy≡ K = ½mv2
Work–energy theorem— W = ΔK

The units check: kg·(m/s)2 = kg·(m/s2)·m = N·m = joules. Kinetic energy is measured in the same unit as work, which it must be if one is to equal a change in the other.

Trevor's warning, kept This version of the theorem treats all forces as doing work — the W above is the net work. Be careful once gravitational potential energy is in play: gravity does do work, and if you have already counted it as a change in potential energy you must not count it again here. Ch08b sorts out which bookkeeping to use when.

How kinetic energy scales

Two proportionalities, and they are not the same strength. This is the part students carry wrongly into the collision problems later in the module.

With mass — linear
K1 = ½m1v2      m2 = 2m1
K2 = ½(2m1)v2 = 2 · ½m1v2
K2 = 2K1

Doubling the mass doubles the energy. It takes twice as much work to accelerate an object twice as massive to the same speed.

With speed — quadratic
K1 = ½m v12      v2 = 2v1
K2 = ½m(2v1)2 = 4 · ½m v12
K2 = 4K1

Doubling the velocity quadruples the energy. It takes four times the energy to go twice as fast — and, read backwards through the stopping-distance example below, four times the distance to stop.

Why this one matters off the page The quadratic is the reason speed limits are not a linear nuisance. A car at 60 mph carries four times the kinetic energy it has at 30, and needs four times the stopping distance on the same road. The mass scaling is the gentler of the two; the speed scaling is the one that hurts.

Worked example — stopping a car

How much work is done in stopping a car that was moving at 40.0 m/s (about 90 mph)? The car has a mass of 2000 kg.

W = ΔK = Kf − Ki
W = ½m v2 − ½m vo2   with v = 0, the first term vanishes
W = 0 − ½(2000 kg)(40.0 m/s)2
W = −(1000 kg)(1600 m2/s2)
W = −1.60 × 106 N·m
Trevor asks

Why is this negative? Does that fit with your understanding of forces?

Yes — and it has to be. The car is losing kinetic energy, so ΔK is negative and so is the work. Physically: the braking force points backward while the car moves forward, so the angle between them is 180° and cos 180° = −1. The sign is not bookkeeping — it is the statement that the road is taking energy out of the car rather than putting it in.

Worked example — the stopping distance

How far does the car slide if the brakes lock? And how far does it go if the anti-lock brakes work as designed? Take the coefficients of friction for rubber on concrete to be μs = 0.50 and μk = 0.30.

Set the friction work equal to the energy that has to go
W = F⃗ · d⃗ = Ff d cos θ
W = μFN d cos 180° = −μmg d
d = W−μmg

The normal force on level ground is mg, so FN = (2000 kg)(9.8 m/s2) = 19 600 N. Both the work and the denominator are negative, so the distance comes out positive — as a distance must.

The two cases, side by side
Brakes lock — sliding, use μk = 0.30 Anti-lock works — use μs = 0.50
d = −1.60 × 106 N·m−(0.30)(19 600 N) d = −1.60 × 106 N·m−(0.50)(19 600 N)
d = 272 m d = 163 m

A difference of 109 metres — more than a football pitch — from nothing but keeping the tyres rolling instead of letting them skid. That is what anti-lock brakes buy, and the reason is that static friction is stronger than kinetic friction. A locked wheel slides, and a sliding tyre grips less than a rolling one.

Sanity-check the numbers, not just the algebra 272 metres at 90 mph is a long way — and it should be, because the kinetic energy went as v2. Halve the speed to 20 m/s and the energy falls by four, and so does the stopping distance: about 68 m sliding. The quadratic from the scaling section is doing all the work here.

Review

Kinetic energy— energy due to the motion of an object, K = ½mv2, in joules.
The theorem— W = ΔK. Net work causes changes in kinetic energy.
Scaling— linear in mass, quadratic in speed. Twice the speed is four times the energy.
Negative work— means energy is being taken out. A braking force opposes the motion, so cos 180° = −1.
The caution— this form counts every force, gravity included. Do not also count gravity as a potential energy change in the same equation.
