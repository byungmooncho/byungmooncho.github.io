Energy in Collisions

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Momentum is conserved in every collision. Kinetic energy is not — and how much of it survives is what distinguishes one kind of collision from another. Two idealised extremes bracket the real cases: elastic, where kinetic energy is conserved, and perfectly inelastic, where the objects stick together. Real collisions sit between them, and the gap is the bangs, the shakes and the heat.
You already know this Momentum, impulse and conservation from Ch09a; kinetic energy from Ch07b; and from Ch08b, the reason mechanical energy goes missing when non-conservative forces act. This page is those three put to work on one problem at a time.

Two ideal types of collision

The bracket
Elastic collisionsInelastic collisions
Kinetic energy conserved. The objects separate with the same total K they arrived with. Objects stick together. In the perfectly inelastic case they leave as one, with one final velocity.

Most collisions fit somewhere in between: some kinetic or mechanical energy is “lost”, more or less depending on the objects. Momentum is conserved in all of them — that is what makes it the more useful of the two conservation laws for collisions.

Sticking together

The easiest case to set up, because the condition “they stick” removes an unknown: if two objects stick together, their final velocities are equal.

Momentum conservation, worked through
p⃗i = p⃗f
p⃗1i + p⃗2i = p⃗1f + p⃗2f
m1v⃗1i + m2v⃗2i = m1v⃗1f + m2v⃗2f
m1v1i − m2v2i = m1vf + m2vf
m1v1i − m2v2i = (m1 + m2) vf

The minus sign on the fourth line is the second ball's leftward motion, applied once when the vector equation becomes an algebra equation. With these problems you should be able to solve for any one of the variables when given the others.

Trevor's trick for the unknown direction If you don't know — or aren't sure of — the final velocity's direction, give it no sign. Write it as vf and let the algebra decide. A negative answer means it goes the other way, and that is information, not an error.

Looking at the kinetic energy

Momentum conservation got you the final velocity. Now ask the separate question: how much kinetic energy is left?

Ki = ½m1v1i2 + ½m2v2i2      Kf = ½(m1 + m2)vf2

Compute both and compare. For a perfectly inelastic collision Kf is always less than Ki — this is the collision type that loses the most kinetic energy consistent with momentum conservation. The difference went into deformation, sound and thermal energy, exactly as Ch08c describes.

The elastic case — Newton's cradle

Trevor's worked elastic example, and the reason it is a good one: everybody has seen the device, and everybody assumes momentum conservation explains it. It does not — not on its own.

Two laws, two different equations
Conservation of momentumConservation of energy
m v1i = m v1f + m v2f ½m v1i2 = ½m v1f2 + ½m v2f2
v1i = v1f + v2f v1i2 = v1f2 + v2f2

Equal masses, so m cancels from both. Two equations, two unknowns — and they are not the same equation, which is the entire point.

Comparing the results

Square the momentum result and set it against the energy result:

(v1f + v2f)2 = v1f2 + v2f2   →   2v1fv2f = 0

So one of the two final velocities must be zero. Either ball 1 stops and ball 2 leaves with all the speed — what the cradle actually does — or ball 2 never moves and ball 1 passes through it, which is not available. Momentum conservation alone would have allowed the two balls to share the speed in any proportion. Energy conservation is what forbids it.

What the cradle is really demonstrating Not momentum conservation — energy conservation as a constraint on top of it. Ask why five balls in never give you two balls out at lower speed, and the answer is that the pair of equations has no such solution. The device “loses” energy to vibration and noise, but the loss from one swing to the next is small, which is why it works as a demonstration at all.

Here in the real world

Trevor's closing case: two rail cars, one moving and one at rest, treated first by both conservation laws and then measured against what actually happens.

General first, then equal masses
Conservation of momentumConservation of mechanical energy
m1v1i + 0 = −m1v1f + m2v2f ½m1v1i2 + 0 = ½m1v1f2 + ½m2v2f2
v1i = −v1f + v2f v1i2 = v1f2 + v2f2
(v1i)2 = (−v1f + v2f)2 = v1f2 − 2v1fv2f + v2f2

Everything up to the equal-mass step is general except the starting velocity. Compare the squared momentum result with the energy result and again the cross term must vanish — the same algebra as the cradle, reached from a different picture.

And then the real world The mechanical energy will be less than expected — bangs, shakes, couplers, deformed metal. Treating a real collision as perfectly elastic gives you an upper bound on the final speeds, not a prediction. The honest statement is that momentum conservation is exact here and energy conservation is an idealisation, which is precisely backwards from how most people's intuition ranks them.

Review

Always— momentum is conserved when no net external force acts — elastic, inelastic or anything between.
Elastic— kinetic energy conserved as well. An idealisation.
Perfectly inelastic— the objects stick together and share one final velocity. Loses the most kinetic energy momentum conservation allows.
Newton's cradle— energy conservation limits the outcomes that momentum conservation alone would permit. That is what the device demonstrates.
Real collisions— fall between the two ideals, and the missing mechanical energy is sound, deformation and heat.
