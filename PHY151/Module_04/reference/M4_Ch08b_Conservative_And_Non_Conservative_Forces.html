Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Carry a box from A to B by two different routes and ask how much work gravity did. The answer is the same both times, and it depends only on how much higher B is than A — not on the route. Any force that behaves that way is conservative, and it is exactly the property that lets us define a potential energy for it. Friction fails the test: the longer the route, the more work friction does. That one difference is what separates energy you can get back from energy you cannot.
You already know this The dot product, work as Fd cos θ, kinetic energy, gravitational potential energy U = mgh, and the friction force Ff = μFN from Module 3. The algebra below is longer than usual, but nothing in it is new.

Conservative forces — the path does not matter

Move an object from A to B along two different paths. If the work done by the force is the same for both, the force is conservative, and the change in potential energy between A and B is a property of the two points alone.

Definitions, and the minus sign
Wc≡ work done by conservative forces
Fc≡ a conservative force
Wc = Σ (F⃗c · ds⃗)path      ΔU = Uf − Ui = −Wc

The minus sign is a definition, but it fits. Lower the box and gravity does positive work on it — and the box has less gravitational potential energy than before. Work done by the conservative force and change in potential energy always run in opposite directions, because the potential energy is the bank account the force draws on.

For PHY 151 — with calculus

The sum over path pieces becomes a line integral from A to B, and the statement that the force is conservative becomes the statement that this integral has the same value for every path with those endpoints.

Wc = ∫AB F⃗c · ds⃗
UB − UA = −Wc = −∫AB F⃗c · ds⃗

Equivalently: the integral of a conservative force around any closed loop is zero. That is the version you will meet again in PHY 152 when the same question is asked about electric fields.

Gravity passes the test — worked two ways

Trevor does not assert that gravity is conservative; he computes it. Take a side view of a box moved from one corner to the opposite corner of a rectangle, first by going straight up and then across, and second by going directly along the diagonal. The two routes have different lengths and different angles between the force and the displacement, so there is real work to do.

Route 1 — up, then across
Path y (vertical leg)Path x (horizontal leg)
Wgy = F⃗ · d⃗ Wgx = F⃗ · d⃗
Wgy = Fg d cos 180° Wgx = Fg x cos 90°
Wgy = m g y (−1) Wgx = m g x (0)
Wgy = −m g y Wgx = 0
Wg1 = Wgy + Wgx = 0 − m g y   →   Wg1 = −m g y

The horizontal leg contributes nothing at all — cos 90° = 0. Gravity does no work on anything moving horizontally, however far it goes. That is already half of why the path does not matter.

Route 2 — straight along the diagonal
Wg2 = F⃗ · d⃗
Wg2 = Fg √(x2+y2) cos(180° − θ)
Wg2 = m g √(x2+y2) (cos 180° cos θ + sin 180° sin θ)
Wg2 = m g √(x2+y2) ((−1) cos θ + (0) sin θ)
Wg2 = −m g √(x2+y2) cos θ
Wg2 = −m g √(x2+y2) · y√(x2+y2)
Wg2 = −m g y

The angle-sum identity turns cos(180° − θ) into −cos θ, and then cos θ is just the adjacent side y over the hypotenuse. The hypotenuse cancels, and with it every trace of x. Same answer as route 1.

Go around the path — and get nothing

Put the two results side by side, then reverse one of them. Going out along the legs and back along the diagonal returns the object to where it started, and the total work done by gravity on the round trip is zero.

Three equivalent statements “The work is path-independent”, “the work around any closed loop is zero”, and “a potential energy can be defined for this force” all say the same thing. If you can show any one of them for a force, you have shown all three.

Non-conservative forces — the path is everything

Now run the same experiment with friction. Look down on a box being dragged around a floor, first along two legs of a right triangle and then along the hypotenuse. Friction always opposes the motion, so the angle is 180° on every leg, whichever way you go — and the work is negative on every leg, with nothing to cancel it.

Route 1 — along the legs
Along yAlong x
Wfy = Ff y cos 180° Wfx = Ff x cos 180°
Wfy = −μFN y Wfx = −μFN x
Wf1 = −μFN (x + y)
Route 2 — along the hypotenuse
Wf2 = Ff √(x2+y2) cos 180°
Wf2 = −μFN √(x2+y2)

And (x + y) is not √(x2+y2) — it is always larger, by the triangle inequality. So the two routes give different answers, and the longer one costs more. There is no cancelling on the way back either: reverse the trip and friction reverses with you, so the round trip costs double, not zero.

Why there is no “friction potential energy” A potential energy has to be a function of position alone — one number per point in space. If the work depended on how you got there, no such function could exist, because the same point would need different values. That is the real reason non-conservative forces get no potential energy: not a convention, an impossibility.

Mechanical energy — the ramp, with and without friction

Trevor brings both cases together on one picture: a block released from height hi at the top of a ramp of angle θ, sliding down to hf = 0. Mechanical energy is kinetic plus potential, and what changes it is the work done by the non-conservative forces.

Without friction — mechanical energy is conserved
ΔK + ΔU = ΔEmech = Wnc = 0
½mvf2 − ½mvi2 + mghf − mghi = 0
½mvf2 − 0 + 0 − mghi = 0
½mvf2 = mghi

Released from rest, finishing at the reference level, so vi = 0 and hf = 0 and two of the four terms vanish. Every joule of potential energy the block loses turns up as kinetic energy. Notice what is not in the answer: the ramp angle, and the mass.

With friction — mechanical energy is not conserved
ΔK + ΔU = ΔEmech = Wnc = F⃗f · d⃗
½mvf2 − ½mvi2 + mghf − mghi = μFN Hsin θ cos 180°
½mvf2 − ½mvi2 + mghf − mghi = μmg cos θ Hsin θ (−1)
½mvf2 − mgH = −μmg cos θ Hsin θ
½mvf2 = mgH − μmg cos θ Hsin θ

Two things to read off. The normal force on a ramp is FN = mg cos θ — the Module 3 result, used here unchanged. And the sliding distance is H / sin θ, not H; friction acts along the slope, not along the drop. The final speed is the frictionless answer minus a term, which is what “apply what you know about the world” should have told you before any algebra started.

Be careful of the signs

The sign of Wnc is where this material most often goes wrong, and the confusion is partly the textbooks’ fault.

Almost always negative— Wnc is negative in nearly every problem you will meet, because friction acts opposite to the motion.
Books differ— Some texts build the minus sign into the symbol; some do not. OpenStax College Physics does not — which is the convention Trevor prefers, and the one used here.
So— Write cos 180° explicitly and let it supply the −1. Do not also put a minus sign in front by hand.
Reading the terms
Wnc = ΔK + ΔU   →   0 = ΔK + ΔU − Wnc
0 = ½m(vf2 − vi2) + mg(hf − hi) − (Ff d (−1))
TermWhat it is, physically
½m(vf2 − vi2) Increase in speed and kinetic energy
mg(hf − hi) Decrease in potential energy
Ff d (−1) Energy “removed” by friction

“Removed” is in quotation marks on Trevor’s slide for a reason. The energy is not destroyed; it has left the mechanical account and turned up as thermal energy in the block and the ramp.

Where the energy goes

Pull a box across a floor at constant speed. The net force is zero, so the pull equals the friction, and all the work you do goes somewhere other than kinetic energy:

Wnc = Ff Δd cos 180° = −Ff Δd      ΔEth = +Ff Δd

The mechanical energy lost and the thermal energy gained are the same number with opposite signs. Sand a board or drill a hole and you can feel the result. The microscopic picture behind ΔEth — kinetic energy of individual atoms, and why thermal energy is not the same thing as heat — is taken up in full on the sibling page Ch08c · Dissipative Forces and Thermal Energy.

Review

Conservative— If no non-conservative forces are present, the work required to move from one spot to another is path-independent.
Non-conservative— If non-conservative forces are present, that work is path-dependent.
The effect— Non-conservative forces tend to cause objects to slow down and/or heat up.
The bookkeeping— ΔK + ΔU = ΔEmech = Wnc. Set Wnc = 0 and you have conservation of mechanical energy.
