Conservation of Energy

SynopsisEnergy is one of four quantities in nature that are conserved.  (The other three are mass, momentum, and electric charge.) When we say energy is conserved, we mean that energy can neither be created nor destroyed.  You will learn in the following topic that momentum is one such quantity.  Recall that this conservation law requires the total amount to remain constant.  As a result, an object can have a varying amount of kinetic (KE) or potential energy (PE) depending on the object’s position (which determines PE) and velocity (which determines KE).  Still, the object’s total energy or the sum of KE and PE must remain the same.  For this to happen, all changes must add up to zero (an energy gain must match an energy loss), i.e., it’s a “zero-sum game.” For example, to conserve total energy, when an object gains PE (KE), it loses KE (PE) by the exact amount or vice versa, so the total remains the same.  So here, the energy changes abide by the same zero-sum rule.

You might be wondering why I only mention KE and PE.  This is because, fundamentally, ALL types of energy we know belong to PE (position-dependent) or KE (velocity-dependent).  This might sound strange to you, but it’s true.  For example, electrical, nuclear, and chemical energies can be considered as a form of PE.

We will only describe the energies of objects that we can physically handle or easily observe, such as everyday objects, planets, or stars, rather than atoms or molecules, which are very small.  An individual object’s kinetic energy is KE = ½mv² where KE is in J, m is mass in kg, v is the velocity in m/s, and potential energy is PE = mgh where PE is in J, m is mass in kg, and h is the height from a reference point.  (There are two types of PE: Gravitational (GPE) and elastic potential energy (EPE).  GPE = mgh, the equation you just saw.  EPE will be covered later.)  Note that energy converts back and forth between KE and PE depending on the object’s motion, but the total energy is always the same.  However, this is only true if the object experiences no friction!  When there is a friction force, the object’s total mechanical energy (KE + PE, excluding thermal energy) decreases and eventually completely dissipates!  When this happens, the object has no energy for motion, so it comes to rest.  Total mechanical energy (KE + PE) converts to thermal (internal energy).  Mechanical energy can perform useful work, such as lifting and pushing objects, but thermal energy is only heat, which can’t be easily converted to push or lift objects.  Thermal energy is often considered “wasted energy.”

In summary, the total energy remains constant regardless of the object’s motion.  If there is no friction, the total mechanical energy (PE + KE) remains constant and is always maintained.  If there is friction, mechanical energy converts to thermal energy, resulting in dissipation (“disappearance”) or is wasted, no longer able to do useful work through pushing, pulling, or lifting an object.

The two cases

The conservation law is one equation, and friction adds one term to it.  Take a box at the top of a hill, at rest, height h, and let it reach the bottom.

No friction.  Total mechanical energy (PE + KE) always stays the same, so the energy at the start equals the energy at the end.  Write both sides out, and two of the four terms are zero: the box starts from rest (KEi = 0) and ends at the reference level (PEf = 0).

Ei = Ef — the law; the equals sign is the whole content of it
½mvi² + mghi = ½mvf² + mghf
mghi = ½mvf² — so the speed at the bottom follows from the height alone
vf = √(2gh) — the mass cancels; a bowling ball and a marble arrive at the same speed

Numbers: from h = 5 m, v = √(2 × 9.8 × 5) = 9.9 m/s.  For a 1 kg box that is mgh = 49 J at the top and ½mv² = 49 J at the bottom — the same 49 J, in a different form.

With friction.  Total mechanical energy gradually decreases, turning into “useless” thermal energy.  The equation gains one term on the left, the work done by the non-conservative force (friction):

KEi + PEi + WNC = KEf + PEf — “NC” = non-conservative

WNC is numerically a negative number: it subtracts from the starting energy, so Emi > Emf.  If the same 1 kg box reaches the bottom of the 5 m hill at only 5 m/s, its KE is ½(1)(5)² = 12.5 J, and the difference, 49 − 12.5 = 36.5 J, is how much energy friction turned into heat.  That is −WNC.  Eventually, all mechanical energy can be converted into thermal energy, and the object comes to a stop.

Skate ramp revisited

Number three points on the half-pipe from Note 2: the release point, the bottom, and the far side.

No friction: E1 = E2 = E3.  At 1 and 3 the speed is zero and all the energy is PE; at 2 the height is zero and all of it is KE.  So mgh1 = ½mv2² = mgh3: the skater comes back up to exactly the height she started from, and goes back and forth forever.  The lowest point is where she is fastest.

With friction: E1 > E2 > E3.  Each leg loses some mechanical energy to thermal: KE1 + PE1 + WNC = KE2 + PE2, and again KE2 + PE2 + W′NC = KE3 + PE3.  Point 3 is lower than point 1, the next turnaround is lower still, and she ends at the bottom.  Nothing vanished: KE3 + PE3 + (the thermal energy) is still the total she started with.

Roller coaster

Now the model with numbers.  A 100 kg cart is released from rest at point 1, 10 m up.  The track has no friction until the final climb, where it is rough.

Every speed on the frictionless part comes from the same line: E1 = mgh1 = (100)(9.8)(10) = 9800 J, and that number is the energy at every point until the climb.

at 2 (7 m): ½mv2² = mg(10 − 7) → v2 = √(2 × 9.8 × 3) = 7.67 m/s
at 3 (8 m): v3 = √(2 × 9.8 × 2) = 6.26 m/s — it clears the 8 m hump because 8 < 10
at 4 (1 m): v4 = √(2 × 9.8 × 9) = 13.3 m/s — the lowest point, the fastest point
at 4a, where v = 10 m/s: gh1 = ½v² + gh → h = 10 − 10²/(2 × 9.8) = 4.9 m

Check the zero-sum rule at 4a: ΔPE = −mg(5.1) = −4998 J and ΔKE = ½(100)(10)² = +5000 J.  They add to zero, to rounding.  That is what “conserved” means in numbers.

On the climb, friction acts.  The cart reaches point 5, 8 m up, and stops.  Its energy there is mgh5 = (100)(9.8)(8) = 7840 J; it started with 9800 J; so friction did 9800 − 7840 = 1960 J of work on it, WNC = −1960 J, and that 1960 J is now thermal energy in the track and the wheels.  Without friction it would have passed point 5 at √(2 × 9.8 × 2) = 6.26 m/s.

The three questions, and the CER.  Does the marble reach the other end?  Yes — the far end is 8 m and it has 10 m worth of energy.  Does it return to its starting position?  With friction, no: every pass loses mechanical energy, so it never regains 10 m.  How would you make it return?  Put energy back in — push it, or start it higher; something must do work equal to what friction took.  Written as a claim–evidence–reasoning:

Claim: the marble is still moving at 3, with v3 = 6.26 m/s.
Evidence: E1 = E2 = E3 on the frictionless part, and E1 = mgh1 = 9800 J.
Reasoning: 9800 = ½mv3² + (100)(9.8)(8) = ½mv3² + 7840, so ½mv3² = 1960 J and v3 = 6.26 m/s. Equality is the conservation law.

For PHY 151 — with calculus

Where does the law come from?  Integrate Newton’s second law once.  Start from m·dv/dt = F(x) for motion along a line and multiply both sides by v = dx/dt.  The left side becomes d/dt(½mv²) and, when the force comes from a potential energy, F = −dU/dx, the right side becomes −dU/dt.  Moving everything to one side: d/dt(½mv² + U) = 0.  The sum KE + PE has zero rate of change — it is constant.  That is the conservation law, and it was never an extra assumption: it is Newton’s second law integrated once.  Note 2 ran this in reverse (differentiate the law, get Newton).  Now add a friction force of size f that always opposes the motion.  The extra term on the right is −f·|v|, which is never positive, so d/dt(KE + PE) ≤ 0: mechanical energy can only go down, at a rate f|v|, and the total it loses over a trip is exactly the WNC of item 1.
