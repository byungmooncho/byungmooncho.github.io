Work and the Work-Energy Relation

Synopsis We left the skater on her ramp with a problem. With no friction she can never get off it — down one side, up the other, back again, forever. It is friction that lets her stop. So friction is not just something that spoils the arithmetic: it is doing something real, and something we ought to be able to measure. This Note builds the tool. We will work out what it means for a force to change an object's energy, and then take that tool first to a cart crossing a rough patch and then to a skier on a hill, and find out how much energy friction took in each case — using nothing but the speeds and the height.
Performance Expectations By the end of this note, you will be able to…
  • PE1Calculate the work done by a force acting through a distance, and say why the joule measures both work and energy.
  • PE2Decide whether a given force does positive, negative or zero work from the directions of the force and the motion alone.
  • PE3Use the work-energy theorem ΔKE = W to relate the total work done on an object to its change in speed.
  • PE4Separate WC from WNC — the work gravity does from the work everything else does — and explain why only one of them can be given back.
  • PE5Show that friction acted, and find how many joules it took, from a measured speed and a measured height, without ever measuring the friction force directly.

1 · What work is

Energy does not change on its own. Something has to push or pull an object through a distance, and that is what we call work. Work, W, is measured in joules (J) — the same unit as energy, which is no coincidence: work is energy being handed over. When the force points the way the object is moving, W = Fd, where F is the force in newtons (N) and d is the distance moved in metres (m).

work done by a force along the motion
  1. A 20 N force pushes a crate 100 m along a level floor, in the direction of the push. How much work does the force do?
  2. Work and energy share a unit. Say in one sentence why that is not an accident.

2 · A force only does work if the object moves along it

A force does not have to point the way the object is going, and when it does not, it does less work — or none at all. Push the crate forwards and you add energy to it. Friction pushes backwards against the motion and takes energy away. The floor pushes straight up on the crate while the crate slides sideways, and that force does no work whatsoever, however hard it pushes: the crate never moves along it.

Putting a number on the in-between case

The three cases above are the ends and the middle. For a force at any angle in between, only the part of it lying along the displacement does work; the part across the motion does none. Force and displacement are both vectors, so written properly the work is W = F · D = |F||D| cos θ, where θ is the angle between them. The dot product turns two vectors into a single number — work is a scalar. It carries a sign, but no direction.

3 · Work changes kinetic energy

Do work on an object and its kinetic energy changes by exactly that amount. That is the work-energy theorem: ΔKE = W. The W here is the total work done on the object by every force acting on it, not by one force you happen to be interested in.

4 · Two kinds of work, and a system that leaks

Think of the skater and her ramp as one closed system. Left alone, its total energy E = KE + PE stays constant: that is what conservation means, and it is why she never gets off. Work is how energy gets into or out of a system from outside, so once friction acts the system leaks, and Ei + WNC = Ef. That is the work-energy relation.

The leak happens because forces do not all behave the same way. Gravity gives back exactly what it takes: lift an object and gravity takes energy from it, drop it and gravity hands the same energy straight back. Friction never gives anything back — it only ever takes. So we split the total work into those two kinds, W = WC + WNC, and the theorem becomes ΔKE = WC + WNC. The work gravity does is precisely the potential energy the object loses: WC = −ΔPE.

5 · Finding friction without measuring it

Here is the move this Note has been building to. Over any trip there are two things we can usually get at: how fast the object is going at each end, which gives us ΔKE, and how far it rose or fell, which gives us WC. If gravity were the only thing doing work, those two would match. So subtract them. If ΔKE − WC comes out zero, nothing else did any work. If it does not come out zero, something else did — and the leftover is exactly how much energy it took.

First, a trip with no height change at all

A 4.0 kg cart is rolling along a level bench at 6.0 m/s. It crosses a rough patch 4.0 m long and comes off it doing 2.0 m/s.

the level bench — one term dies
  1. The bench is level. What does that make WC, and why?
  2. Calculate ΔKE for the crossing.
  3. Work out ΔKE − WC. With one of the two terms gone, what is left?
  4. The patch is 4.0 m long. How big was the friction force?

When the force is not constant

Everything above used W = Fd, which assumes the force held steady over the whole distance. When it does not — a spring, a hand that tires — the work is the area under the force-against-distance graph, W = ∫ F · dx, taken along the path. A constant force is just the case where that area is a rectangle, and the integral collapses back to Fd. Note the variable of integration: work is kept over distance. Keep the same force over time instead and you get something different, which is where this Module goes next.

area under F against x
  1. A force rises steadily from 0 N to 40 N over the first 5.0 m of a push. Sketch F against x and find the work from the area.
  2. Show that ∫ F · dx gives Fd when F is constant.

6 · Looking ahead

Work changes an object's energy, and it is a force acting over a distance. Later in this Module you will meet impulse, which changes an object's momentum, and which is a force acting over a time. Same force, same event — one set of books kept over distance, the other over time.
