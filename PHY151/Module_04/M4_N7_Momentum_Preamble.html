What is Momentum?  Momentum Preamble

SynopsisWhat is momentum?  It is simply a quantity that is a product of mass and velocity: p = mv, where p is the momentum in kg m/s, m is the mass in kg, and v is the velocity (vector!) in m/s.  So imagine a person of mass 50 kg, moving at 2 m/s.  This person has momentum, p = (50 kg)(2 m/s) = 100 kg m/s.  Note that 1) to have momentum, you must be moving or have a velocity, so anything at rest has no momentum (or zero momentum); 2) velocity is a vector quantity, and so is the momentum.  That means the direction of movement is important.  If moving to the east is positive, then the west is negative, and so, accordingly, momentum toward the east and momentum toward the west have the opposite sign (the east is, by convention, positive).

Momentum is a quantity that is as fundamental as energy, mass, velocity, position, temperature, time, and so on.  You should think of it as a distinct entity as you think of these other quantities.  It’s just that you can quantify momentum if you know both the mass and the velocity of the object of interest.  And it’s the quantity that Nature really cares about conserving.  Just as energy and mass are conserved, i.e., never created or destroyed (so the total always remains the same), Nature behaves to ensure the momentum is conserved (we will learn about the conservation in greater detail soon).

Momentum is a useful quantity when dealing with dynamics (force and motion).  For example, why would stopping a 200 kg football player running at 5 m/s be much harder than stopping a 20 kg toddler running at 5 m/s?  The key quantity is neither just the mass nor just the velocity.  It’s the product of those two, the momentum.  The greater the momentum, the more difficult it is to stop.  In other words, you need a larger force to slow a larger momentum.  You need to apply a large force to produce a significant change in momentum.  This change is called impulse, which depends on the time duration of the force you apply and the amount of force being applied, i.e., the product of force and time (we will learn about the impulse-momentum relation in greater detail soon).

In this Note, I will introduce the definition of momentum and consider a few examples illustrating this fundamental concept.

Momentum is mass times velocity

One product, two ingredients.  A heavy thing moving slowly and a light thing moving fast can carry the same momentum, and that is exactly why the quantity is useful: it is the one number that says how hard something is to stop.

  1. Calculate the momentum of a 200 kg footballer running at 5 m/s and a 20 kg toddler running at 5 m/s.
  2. If you double the velocity, what happens to the momentum?
  3. If you triple the mass, what happens to the momentum?

Momentum has a direction

Velocity is a vector, so momentum is too.  On a line that means a sign: east positive, west negative.  The sign is not decoration — it is what makes a bounce a change rather than a pause.

  1. A bouncy ball moving toward the east has a momentum of 10 kg m/s.  The ball bounces off the wall and now moves in the opposite direction.  What is its new momentum?
  2. The answer to this question requires a little bit of careful thinking.  How much has the ball’s momentum changed after bouncing off the wall?  (Hint: It’s not zero.  Take the difference between momentum after and momentum before, and be careful with the signs of momenta.)

A slow train and a fast bullet

Two objects at opposite ends of the mass scale, and the arithmetic says which one you can stop.  Convert the units first — the momentum wants kg and m/s.

  1. A freight train has a mass of 5000 tons (1 ton = 1000 kg).  It is lumbering along at a snail’s pace, at 0.03 mph.  Calculate the momentum in kg m/s.
  2. A bullet with a mass of 25 g is fired from a rifle.  It travels at 3000 km/h.  Calculate the momentum in kg m/s.
  3. Based on your calculations above, discuss why bullet-proof jackets can stop a bullet, but one has zero chance of stopping a train.  (Imagine the same momentum in a 200 kg football player – how fast would the player be going?)
i) m = 5000 × 1000 = 5 × 10⁶ kg;  v = 0.03 mi/h × (1609 m / 1 mi) × (1 h / 3600 s) = 0.0134 m/s;  p = (5 × 10⁶)(0.0134) = 6.7 × 10⁴ kg·m/s — large momentum
ii) m = 25 g = 0.025 kg;  v = 3000 km/h × (1000 m / 1 km) × (1 h / 3600 s) = 833 m/s;  p = (0.025)(833) = 20.8 kg·m/s — small momentum
iii) The train carries about 3000 times the bullet’s momentum.  Put each into a 200 kg player: the bullet’s 20.8 kg·m/s is a player at 0.10 m/s — a slow shuffle, and a jacket can take that out of a bullet;  the train’s 67 000 kg·m/s is the player at 335 m/s, about the speed of sound.  Nothing you can wear stops that.
For PHY 151 — with vectors

Momentum as a vector.  p = mv is a vector equation: the momentum points the way the velocity points, and a component form px = mvx, py = mvy is available whenever the motion is not along one line.  A bounce off a wall is a change of sign in one component, and the change in momentum is the vector difference Δp = pf − pi, which is why a ball that comes back with the same speed has changed its momentum by twice its size.

  1. A 0.50 kg ball hits a wall at 6.0 m/s, moving at 30° to the wall’s normal, and leaves at the same speed and angle.  Write pi and pf in components (x along the normal) and find Δp.
  2. Say in one sentence why only the normal component changed.
i) |p| = (0.50)(6.0) = 3.0 kg·m/s.  pi = (3.0 cos 30°, 3.0 sin 30°) = (2.6, 1.5);  pf = (−2.6, 1.5);  Δp = pf − pi = (−5.2, 0) kg·m/s — straight out from the wall, size 2|px|
ii) The wall can only push along its normal; it has no grip along its face, so the parallel component passes through untouched.
