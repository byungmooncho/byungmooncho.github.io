Unit Vectors

Synopsis You have already written one. In Note 3 the launched ball's velocity came out as v⃗(t) = v0x î + (−9.8t) ĵ, and those two hatted symbols did real work in an equation that carried the whole argument — without ever being defined. This note names them. A unit vector is pure direction: magnitude exactly 1, and no units at all. Everything with a size and a unit sits in front of it. Once you have î and ĵ, every vector in the plane is two ordinary numbers, and adding vectors becomes collecting like terms.
Performance Expectations By the end of this note, you will be able to…
  • PE1State what makes a vector a unit vector — magnitude exactly 1, no units — and explain why the hat contributes nothing to the units of the quantity it describes.
  • PE2Write a vector in unit-vector form from its components, and read a unit-vector expression back as a magnitude and a direction.
  • PE3Multiply a vector by a scalar and say what changes, what cannot change, and why a negative scalar is a statement about direction rather than a negative magnitude.
  • PE4Add two vectors given in unit-vector form by collecting the î and ĵ terms separately, then recover the magnitude and direction of the result.

Predict first. In the velocity you wrote in Note 3, v⃗(t) = v0x î + (−9.8t) ĵ, what does ĵ contribute to the units of v⃗?

You already used this

Look again at the equation from Note 3 and read it in pieces rather than as one lump:

v⃗(t)  =  v0x î  +  (−9.8 t) ĵ What each piece is doing: v0x is a number with units (m/s) and it is the whole size of the horizontal part. î says which way that part points, and contributes no size and no units. Likewise −9.8t is a number with units (m/s), and ĵ says it points along the vertical axis — with the minus sign meaning downward, because up was chosen as positive.

So the equation is not one complicated object. It is two ordinary numbers, each with a label saying which axis it belongs to. That is the whole idea, and the rest of this note just makes it official.

What makes a vector a unit vector

📐Three things, and that is the whole definition
1️⃣ Magnitude exactly 1 Not approximately, not "about one unit long." Exactly 1, always, in every unit system.
🧭 Along a coordinate axis î points along +x, ĵ along +y. They are locked to your axes and do not rotate with the object.
🚫 No units of its own It is dimensionless. All the physics, and every unit, lives in the number in front.
|î|  =  |ĵ|  =  1 Where this holds: always — it is the definition, not a result, and it does not depend on the units of the grid you drew. A consequence worth keeping: a magnitude is never negative. When you write −4 î, the minus sign is a statement about direction — magnitude 4, pointing along −x. "The magnitude is −4" is always wrong.

What a number in front can — and cannot — do

Multiplying a vector by a scalar does exactly two things: it changes the length, and if the scalar is negative it reverses the direction end for end. That is the complete list. No scalar can rotate a vector to a new angle. If you need a direction that is not along an axis, you build it by adding î and ĵ pieces — which is the next section, and is exactly what Note 2 had you doing with a ruler.

Adding vectors becomes collecting like terms

This is the payoff, and it is the reason the notation exists. In Note 2 you discovered that components add independently: Cx = Ax + Bx and Cy = Ay + By. Written with hats, that discovery becomes ordinary algebra — gather the î terms, gather the ĵ terms, and do not let them mix:

A⃗ = (3.0 m) î + (5.0 m) ĵ     B⃗ = (−1.0 m) î + (2.0 m) ĵ
A⃗ + B⃗  =  (3.0 − 1.0) m î + (5.0 + 2.0) m ĵ  =  (2.0 m) î + (7.0 m) ĵ Where this holds: both vectors must be the same kind of quantity, in the same units, on the same set of axes. You may add a displacement to a displacement; you may not add a displacement to a velocity, however tidy the algebra looks. The î and ĵ terms never mix — that is the independence of the axes, the same fact Note 3 used to argue that horizontal motion cannot change the fall time.

And the magnitude and direction come back the way they always have, from Note 2a:

|A⃗ + B⃗| = √(2.0)² + (7.0)² m = 7.3 m     θ = tan−1(7.0 ⁄ 2.0) = 74.1° Watch the quadrant. The inverse tangent alone cannot tell +x, +y from −x, −y — it returns the same angle for both. Sketch the vector first and read the quadrant off the signs of the two components, exactly as you did in Note 2a.

Two notations, same three arrows

You will seeRead asWhere
î, ĵ, k̂"i hat, j hat, k hat" This course. It is what your textbook and WebAssign use, and it is what is written on every note from here on.
x̂, ŷ, ẑ"x hat, y hat, z hat" The same three vectors, named after the axes instead. You will meet it in the Chapter 3 reference pages and in plenty of other books. î = x̂, ĵ = ŷ, k̂ = ẑ.

Neither is more correct. Pick one and stay with it inside a single problem — mixing them halfway through a solution is how sign errors get in.

Looking ahead — in Note 4 the launcher tilts, and the velocity picks up a vertical piece at the start: v⃗(t) = v0cosθ î + (v0sinθ − 9.8t) ĵ. Every term in it is something you can now read. The notation is not decoration — it is what keeps the two motions visibly separate while they share one clock.
