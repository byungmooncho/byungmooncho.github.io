Horizontally Fired Projectiles

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Throw a baseball straight out from a rooftop. It travels forward and it falls, and the two things happen at the same time without interfering with each other — the independence of horizontal and vertical motion. So you do not solve one curved problem; you solve two straight-line problems from Module 1 and staple them together with the one shared clock. Firing horizontally buys you one extra gift: the initial vertical velocity is zero, which collapses the vertical equation from a quadratic into a single square root.
Performance Expectations By the end of this lesson, you will be able to…
  • PE1Solve projectile problems in which the projectile is shot horizontally.
  • PE2Determine the time of flight, distance travelled, launch speed, and/or impact velocity for projectiles shot horizontally, when given sufficient information.
Dropping the “horizontally” restriction — any launch angle you like — is the sibling page 04b. Maximum height and range get their own special equations in 04c.
You already know this Every equation on this page is a Module 1 equation. You already solve constant-velocity problems (x = v Δt) and free-fall problems (a = −9.80 m/s², dropped from rest). You also already know how to resolve a velocity into components from N2. Nothing new is being asked of your algebra — only that you run two familiar problems side by side on one clock.

The treatment for any projectile

Trevor's recipe is three steps, and it is the same three steps whether the launch is horizontal, angled up, or angled down:

  1. Find the components of the initial velocity, making sure the angles are correct.
  2. Apply the equations of motion to each component separately. The x equations see only x information; the y equations see only y information. The only thing they share is Δt.
  3. Recombine to answer the question that was actually asked — a final position, a final velocity, a magnitude and a direction.

Step 1 for a horizontal launch: the launch speed is all of v0x

Resolving a launch at θ = 0°
v0x = v0 cos θ = v0 cos 0° = v0 (1) = v0
v0y = v0 sin θ = v0 sin 0° = v0 (0) = 0

Cos 0° = 1 and sin 0° = 0, so the whole launch speed lands in the horizontal column and the vertical column starts empty. Keep the step visible anyway: on 04b the angle will not be zero, and the habit of writing both lines is what keeps you out of trouble there.

Why the two directions separate

The claim is Cho's N3 claim: the horizontal and vertical motions are independent. Here is the reasoning, and it is entirely about the vector nature of the force.

Gravity pulls straight down. A straight-down vector has no horizontal component. So in the horizontal column the acceleration is ax = 0 and the horizontal velocity never changes — not because gravity switches off, but because gravity has nothing to contribute in that direction. In the vertical column the acceleration is the full ay = −9.80 m/s², and the ball is in free fall from the instant it leaves your hand.

Say it carefully Not “the horizontal motion is unaffected by gravity” in the sense of no force acting — the ball has weight the whole way down. Say instead: gravity has no horizontal component, so ax = 0 and vx is constant. That sentence is the claim, the evidence and the reasoning in one line.
One standing assumption Air resistance is neglected everywhere on this page. A real baseball loses a noticeable bite of its horizontal speed to drag, so the real landing point is short of the one we compute.

Predict first A ball is thrown horizontally at 40.0 m/s. Halfway down, its horizontal velocity component is…

Pick a sign convention and never drift

Two directions means two chances to lose a minus sign. Fix the convention once, in writing, before you touch a number. On this page: up is positive, the origin sits on the ground directly below the launch point, and therefore ay = −9.80 m/s² every single time. The launch is in the +x direction.

The horizontal-projectile equations

Take the six equations of motion — three for x, three for y — and feed them the two facts a horizontal launch gives you: ax = 0 and v0y = 0. Most of the terms die.

General equation of motionHorizontal launchWhat collapsed, and why
x = x0 + v0xΔt + ½axΔt² x = v0Δt ax = 0 (gravity has no horizontal component); x0 = 0 by our choice of origin
vx = v0x + axΔt vx = v0 constant horizontal velocity for the whole flight
vx² = v0x² + 2axΔx vx² = v0² an identity once ax = 0 — it tells you nothing new
y = y0 + v0yΔt + ½ayΔt² Δy = −½gΔt² the important one: v0y = 0 kills the term linear in Δt, so no quadratic formula
vy = v0y + ayΔt vy = −gΔt free fall from rest, vertically
vy² = v0y² + 2ayΔy vy² = −2gΔy the time-free route to the impact speed (Δy is negative, so the right side is positive)
Six relations for a horizontal launch — up positive, ay = −g
v0x = v0   ·   v0y = 0
x = v0x Δt   ·   Δy = −12g Δt²
vy = −g Δt   ·   vy² = −2g Δy

Six relations, and six quantities that could in principle be unknown: v0, v0x, vy, Δy, Δt and x. In practice a problem hands you nearly all of them and asks for one or two. And they are valid only for a horizontal launch — the moment the launch angle is not zero, v0y ≠ 0 and the vertical equation goes back to being a quadratic. That is page 04b.

If you would rather work with the drop height h = |Δy| as a positive number measured downward, the second and fourth read h = ½gΔt² and vy² = 2gh. Same physics; just be sure you have committed to one convention and stay there.

Worked example: the baseball off the roof

A baseball is thrown horizontally from the top of a 50.0 m tall building. Its initial velocity is 40.0 m/s. Where does the baseball land?

Set-up — write down everything before you compute anything

Horizontal→x0 = 0,  v0x = 40.0 m/s,  ax = 0,  x = ?
Vertical→y0 = 50.0 m,  y = 0,  v0y = 0,  ay = −9.80 m/s²
Shared≡Δt — the one quantity both columns agree on, and therefore the bridge between them

“Where does it land” is a question about x, and the horizontal column cannot answer it: x = v0xΔt has two unknowns. So go get Δt from the vertical column, which knows the whole story of the fall.

Step 2a — the time, from the vertical column

Vertical: free fall from rest through 50.0 m
y = y0 + v0y Δt + ½ay Δt²
0 = y0 + 0 + ½(−g) Δt²   →   ½g Δt² = y0
Δt = √ 2y0g = √ 2 (50.0 m)9.80 m/s² = √10.204 s² = 3.1944 s

The units work out: m ÷ (m/s²) = s², and the square root of s² is s. The algebra also offers Δt = −3.1944 s; we throw it out because the ball was thrown at t = 0 and we want the future, not the past.

No quadratic formula. That is the whole payoff of v0y = 0 — the Δt term vanished and what was left was a bare square.

Step 2b — the distance, from the horizontal column

Horizontal: constant velocity for 3.1944 s
x = x0 + v0x Δt + ½ax Δt² = v0x Δt
x = (40.0 m/s)(3.1944 s) = 127.78 m   →   x = 128 m

Both given values carry three significant figures, so the answer gets three: the ball lands 128 m from the base of the building, measured horizontally. Carry the guard digits (3.1944 s, not 3.19 s) through the middle of the calculation and round only here — rounding the time first would cost you a metre.

Step 3 — recombine, if the question asks for the impact velocity

Magnitude and direction at impact
vx = v0x = 40.0 m/s   ·   vy = −g Δt = −(9.80 m/s²)(3.1944 s) = −31.3 m/s
|v⃗| = √vx² + vy² = √(40.0)² + (31.30)² m/s = 50.8 m/s
θ = tan−1 31.3040.0 = 38.0° below the horizontal

The minus sign on vy is the convention doing its job: up is positive, so a downward velocity reports negative. For the magnitude you square it anyway; for the direction, state the sign in words — “below the horizontal” — rather than leaving a bare negative angle for a grader to interpret.

Cross-check with the time-free equation: vy² = −2gΔy = −2(9.80 m/s²)(−50.0 m) = 980 m²/s², so vy = −31.3 m/s. Same answer by a different road — always worth 20 seconds on an exam.

Predict first Same roof, same 50.0 m, but you throw the ball at 80.0 m/s instead of 40.0 m/s. The time it spends in the air…

Review

  • A horizontally fired projectile follows a parabolic path from its launch height down to some lower height. Horizontal projectile problems simplify the initial conditions compared with general projectile problems, and they generally avoid the quadratic formula.
  • The horizontal and vertical motions are independent: gravity has no horizontal component, so ax = 0 and vx is constant, while vertically the projectile is in free fall at ay = −9.80 m/s². The two columns share exactly one quantity: Δt.
  • “Horizontally” means θ = 0°, so v0x = v0 and v0y = 0. That second fact is the one that collapses the vertical position equation to Δy = −½gΔt² — a single square root instead of a quadratic.
  • The standard route is: get Δt from the vertical column, then feed it to the horizontal column. For the 50.0 m building at 40.0 m/s: Δt = 3.19 s and x = 128 m, landing at 50.8 m/s, 38.0° below the horizontal.
  • Resolve, compute, recombine. Step 3 is not optional bookkeeping — a velocity answer is not finished until it has both a magnitude and a direction.
  • Everything here assumes no air resistance, and everything here assumes the launch is exactly horizontal. Release the second assumption and you are on page 04b.

Looking ahead: tilt the launcher and v0y is no longer zero. The three-step treatment does not change at all — but the vertical position equation keeps its linear term, so solving for Δt means a genuine quadratic. That is 04b, General Projectile Problems; the shortcut formulas for maximum height and range are 04c.
