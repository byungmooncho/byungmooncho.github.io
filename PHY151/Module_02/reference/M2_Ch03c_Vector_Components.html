Vector Components

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea On page 03a you added arrows by drawing them head-to-tail. That works, but a ruler and a protractor are not a calculation. The fix is to run the construction backwards: take one slanted arrow and resolve it into two perpendicular pieces — a piece along x and a piece along y. Those pieces are the vector's components, and once you have them, two-dimensional motion becomes two one-dimensional problems you already know how to solve. The one thing that goes wrong is deciding which trig function to use, and that is what most of this page is about.

You already know this On grid paper in 03a you found the resultant by counting squares: “2 right and 4 up, plus 4 right and 0 up, makes 6 right and 4 up.” You were already using components — you just did not have the word. You also already have the trigonometry: sin, cos, tan of a right triangle, and the Pythagorean theorem. Nothing new is being asked of your math.

Breaking a vector apart

Head-to-tail addition has an inverse. Given the resultant, ask: which two perpendicular arrows, added head-to-tail, would produce it? There is exactly one answer, and the two arrows are the vector's components.

The definition

Component≡The signed length of the perpendicular projection of the vector onto a coordinate axis. Write the x-component of A⃗ as Ax and the y-component as Ay — plain letters, no arrows, because each is just a number.
Resolve≡To find those two numbers. “Resolve A⃗ into components” and “find Ax and Ay” mean the same thing.

“Signed” matters: a component can be negative. In the first quadrant both are positive, which is where this page stays — page 03e handles the other three.

A component is the shadow the vector casts

Shine a light straight down on a slanted arrow and it throws a shadow on the x-axis; that shadow is Ax. Shine the light in from the side and the shadow on the y-axis is Ay. The picture is worth keeping, as long as you remember what it is a picture of: the perpendicular projection onto each axis.

Predict first Each individual component of a vector will always be…

Why “or equal to”? Because Ax = A cos θ and cosine never exceeds 1 — but it reaches 1 when θ = 0, which is the vector lying flat along the x-axis. A vector that points straight along an axis is its own component in that direction, and has zero component in the other. Equality is the special case, not an impossibility.

Which function goes with which axis?

Here is the heart of the lesson. Cosine does not “go with x.” Cosine goes with the side adjacent to the angle you were handed, and sine with the side opposite. Which of those is the x-side depends entirely on where the angle was measured from.

The same two numbers, written four ways
sin θ = ryr  ⇒  ry = r sin θ      cos θ = rxr  ⇒  rx = r cos θ
sin φ = rxr  ⇒  rx = r sin φ      cos φ = ryr  ⇒  ry = r cos φ

All four lines describe the identical arrow. The top pair uses the angle from the x-axis; the bottom pair uses the angle from the y-axis. They agree because φ = 90° − θ, and sin(90° − θ) = cos θ.

Trevor's warning — the one to remember Don't blindly learn that cos goes with x and sin goes with y. Draw the triangle, label the angle you were actually given, and ask which side is adjacent to it. SOH-CAH-TOA is about opposite and adjacent — never about x and y.

The sneaky dirty trick

Trevor's name for it, and he is not joking: the homework problem and the quiz problem can look identical and need opposite functions. Only the position of the little angle mark has changed.

Two worked examples — deliberately not the same

Example 1 — A = 10.0 m, θ = 30.0° from the x-axis
cos θ = AxA  ⇒  Ax = A cos θ = (10.0 m)(cos 30.0°) = (10.0 m)√32 = 8.66 m
sin θ = AyA  ⇒  Ay = A sin θ = (10.0 m)(sin 30.0°) = (10.0 m)12 = 5.00 m

Sanity check: 8.66 m and 5.00 m are both smaller than 10.0 m, and 8.66² + 5.00² = 75.0 + 25.0 = 100 = 10.0². A 30° vector leans mostly along x, so the bigger number belongs to Ax — which is what the picture says too.

Example 2 — B = 5.00 m, θ = 45.0° from the y-axis
Bx = B sin θ = (5.00 m)(sin 45.0°) = (5.00 m)√22 = 3.54 m
By = B cos θ = (5.00 m)(cos 45.0°) = (5.00 m)√22 = 3.54 m

The sine now belongs to x, because θ is measured from the y-axis. At 45° the two answers happen to be equal, so a student who used the wrong function still gets 3.54 m and never finds out. That is exactly why the trick is “sneaky.”

Do this one yourself Repeat Example 2 with θ = 30.0° from the y-axis. The right answers are Bx = (5.00 m)(sin 30.0°) = 2.50 m and By = (5.00 m)(cos 30.0°) = 4.33 m. Blindly pairing cosine with x gives 4.33 m and 2.50 m — the two numbers swapped, and a vector pointing the wrong way. The 45° case hides the error; this one does not.

Predict first A vector has a fixed magnitude. You rotate it in the plane from θ = 30° to θ = 60°, measured from the x-axis. What happens to the magnitude of its x-component?

Trevor pairs that question with a second one: what happens to the magnitude of the vector itself? Nothing. Rotating an arrow does not stretch it. For a 10.0 m vector, Ax falls from 8.66 m to 5.00 m and Ay rises from 5.00 m to 8.66 m — and √Ax² + Ay² is 10.0 m both times. Components trade off; the magnitude is untouched.

Why anyone bothers: the dimensions come apart

This is the payoff, and it is worth more than the arithmetic. A projectile's path, y as a function of x, is an ugly thing to write down. But x as a function of t and y as a function of t are both equations you already solved in Module 1 — one of them has no acceleration at all.

One messy equation, or two easy ones
y(x) = [ y0 − v0yv0xx0 + ayx0²2v0x² ] + [ v0yv0x − ayx0v0x² ]x + [ ay2v0x² ]x²

That is the “messy” equation. Nobody memorizes it. (It comes from eliminating t between the two lines below; the version on Trevor's slide has a couple of typos, so this is the re-derived form.)

x(t) = x0 + v0xt        ax = 0
y(t) = y0 + v0yt + 12ayt²     ay = −g

The recipe: figure out x, figure out y, then plot y against x. Time is the thread that ties the two one-dimensional problems together — the same t appears in both.

Plotted here: v0x = 2.60 m/s, v0y = 9.60 m/s, ay = −9.80 m/s², giving a 1.96 s flight, a 5.09 m range and a 4.70 m peak. You will do this problem properly in 03d and beyond.

Predict first If each component of a vector is doubled, what happens to the direction angle of that vector?

Going the other way: vector from components

PE3. You have rx and ry and you want the magnitude and direction. Same triangle, read in the other order: Pythagoras for the magnitude, an inverse tangent for the angle.

From components back to magnitude and direction
rx² + ry² = r²   ⇒   r = √rx² + ry²
tan θ = ryrx   ⇒   θ = tan−1 (ryrx)

Put Example 1's numbers back in and the original vector reappears:

r = √rx² + ry² = √(8.66 m)² + (5.00 m)² = √100 m² = 10.0 m
θ = tan−1 (5.00 m8.66 m) = tan−1 (0.577) = 30.0°

Units cancel inside the inverse tangent — an angle is a pure number. And notice why probe 3 came out the way it did: double both components and the ratio 5.00/8.66 is unchanged, so θ is unchanged; but the magnitude becomes √(17.32)² + (10.00)² = 20.0 m. Doubling the components scales the vector without turning it.

One caution before 03e Your calculator's tan−1 always returns an angle between −90° and +90°, so it cannot tell (3, 4) from (−3, −4) — both give 53.1°. In the first quadrant, where this page lives, that is harmless. Outside it you must look at the signs of the components and fix the quadrant by hand. That is page 03e.

Review

  • To resolve a vector is to replace it with two perpendicular components that, added head-to-tail, rebuild it. A component is the signed length of the vector's perpendicular projection onto an axis — its shadow on that axis.
  • A component's magnitude is never larger than the vector's magnitude, and equals it only when the vector lies along that axis (the other component is then zero).
  • Cosine does not belong to x. Cosine goes with the side adjacent to the given angle, sine with the side opposite. With θ from the x-axis, rx = r cos θ and ry = r sin θ; with φ from the y-axis those swap. Draw the triangle first, every time.
  • Rotating a vector trades one component for the other and leaves the magnitude alone; scaling both components equally changes the magnitude and leaves the direction alone.
  • Going back: r = √rx² + ry² and θ = tan−1(ry / rx), with the inverse tangent's quadrant checked by hand.
  • Why it matters: components let you treat two-dimensional motion as two independent one-dimensional problems tied together by the clock. That is the whole of Module 2.
