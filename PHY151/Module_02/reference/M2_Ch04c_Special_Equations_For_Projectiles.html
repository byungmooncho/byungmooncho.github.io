Special Equations for Projectiles

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Every projectile problem can be solved from the component equations alone — resolve the launch velocity, run x and y separately, let the flight time link them. Do that once for the general level-ground launch and the algebra collapses into two tidy formulas: an apex height and a range. They are worth deriving because the derivation is the whole course in miniature — and worth deriving carefully, because each one is only true under conditions that the formula itself no longer shows you.
Performance Expectations By the end of this lesson, you will be able to…
  • PE1Determine the maximum height above the launch point a projectile travels when given the launch angle and speed.
  • PE2Derive and use the range equation to find the horizontal distance traveled by a projectile when given the launch angle and speed.
The rest of the Chapter 4 projectile objectives are carried by the sibling pages: 04a for a horizontal launch, 04b for the general problem.
You already know this Three things, all from earlier pages. Resolve a launch velocity into components: v0x = v0 cos θ and v0y = v0 sin θ. Independence of motion: horizontally ax = 0, vertically ay = −g, and the two never talk to each other except through the shared clock. And the three kinematic equations, including the one with no time in it, vy² = v0y² + 2ay Δy. Nothing new enters this page. Everything on it is those three facts pushed through to the end.

One restriction, stated up front: a level plane

Trevor's second slide sets the ground rules, and they matter more than anything else on this page: in this lesson we are looking at projectiles fired on a level plane. The projectile lands at the same height it left. Nothing on this page is true for a ball thrown off a cliff, a golf ball landing on a green above the tee, or an arrow shot from a tower.

Two more assumptions ride along quietly, as they have all module: no air resistance, and g constant over the whole flight. So the full contract is:

Level ground≡landing height = launch height. This is the one that breaks most often in real problems.
No air drag≡the only force in flight is gravity, so the horizontal velocity never changes.
Constant g≡g = 9.80 m/s² throughout — fine for anything that stays near the ground.
Sign convention — read this once, carefully On the component pages you wrote ay = −g = −9.80 m/s², with the minus sign doing real work. The two formulas on this page are derived from that, and the minus signs cancel on the way. In the finished formulas g is a positive magnitude: substitute g = +9.80 m/s². Put −9.80 into the range equation and you will get a negative range. The derivation below shows exactly where the sign goes.

At the top of the path, vy = 0

This single sentence is the hinge of the whole lesson. On the way up the projectile is still rising; on the way down it is falling; at the apex it is doing neither. The vertical component of velocity passes through zero — the object stops moving up. It does not stop: the horizontal component is untouched by gravity and carries straight on.

Things to know about projectiles

  • The flight time up equals the flight time down — on level ground. Half the total flight time is spent reaching the apex.
  • At equal heights the speed is the same, once going up and once coming down. Only the direction differs, and only in its vertical part.
  • The speed at the apex is not zero. It is v0x = v0 cos θ — the whole horizontal component, unchanged since launch.
  • The speed at landing is not zero either. Its magnitude is v0 again, with its direction now θ below the horizontal.
The trap “At the top the velocity is zero” is the single most common error in this chapter. vy = 0 at the apex; vx never was and never will be. Write the subscript. It is the difference between a right answer and a projectile that falls straight out of the sky.

Maximum height above the launch point

A projectile is launched with velocity (v0, θ). What equation describes the maximum height? There are two routes, and Trevor shows both, because they need different information.

Form 1 — from the launch velocity alone

Use the kinematic equation that has no time in it, applied to the y motion only, and impose the apex condition.

Maximum height, no clock required
vy² = v0y² + 2ay (y − y0)
0 = v0y² + 2(−g)(h − 0)

Substituted three things at once: vy = 0 at the apex; ay = −g with the y-axis pointing up; and the launch point as the origin, y0 = 0, so y at the apex is the height h we want.

2gh = v0y²

Moved the negative term across. Both minus signs are now gone — this is where g becomes a positive magnitude.

h = v0y²2g = (v0 sin θ)²2g

Substituted v0y = v0 sin θ. Note the square covers the whole product: (v0 sin θ)², not v0 sin(θ²).

Form 2 — the version that requires the time

If you already know when the projectile reaches the apex, use the position equation instead. First find that time, from the velocity equation:

Time to the apex, then the height
vy = v0y − g Δt   →   0 = v0y − g tapex
tapex = v0yg = v0 sin θg

Again the apex condition vy = 0, this time in the velocity equation.

h = v0y tapex − 12 g tapex²
h = v0y²g − v0y²2g = v0y²2g

Substituted tapex = v0y/g into both terms. The two routes agree, as they must — they are the same physics with the clock eliminated in a different order.

Which form to reach for Form 1 needs only the launch velocity, so it is the one to use when the question hands you v0 and θ and nothing else. Form 2 earns its keep when the time is already on the page — and it is the form that survives when the ground is not level, because it never assumed anything about where the projectile lands.

Deriving the range equation

The range R is the horizontal distance from launch to landing. Horizontally there is no acceleration, so the horizontal problem is trivial — the work is all in finding the flight time, and that is where level ground gets used.

Step 1 — the horizontal motion
x = x0 + v0x Δt + 12 ax (Δt)²
R = v0x T

Substituted x0 = 0 (launch at the origin) and ax = 0 (no air resistance, so nothing pushes horizontally). T is the total flight time. Distance = speed × time, no more than that — but we do not know T yet.

Step 2 — the flight time, from the vertical motion
y = y0 + v0y Δt + 12 ay (Δt)²
0 = v0y T + 12(−g)T²

This is the level-ground step. Setting the left side to zero says the projectile finishes at the same height it started: y = y0 = 0. Everything downstream of this line inherits that assumption.

12 g T² = v0y T
T = 2v0yg = 2v0 sin θg

Divided both sides by T. That throws away the root T = 0, which is a genuine solution — the projectile is also at y = 0 at the instant of launch — but not the one we want. Notice T = 2tapex: up and down take equal times, exactly as the picture promised.

Step 3 — put them together
R = v0x T = v0x 2v0yg = 2v0xv0yg
R = 2(v0 cos θ)(v0 sin θ)g = v0² (2 sin θ cos θ)g

Substituted both components and pulled the two factors of v0 out front. Nothing clever has happened yet — it is still just v0xT.

2 sin θ cos θ = sin(2θ)

The step students cannot reproduce. This is the double-angle identity from trigonometry — read the right-hand side as “the sine of the quantity two theta,” not as two times sin θ. It is a rewriting, not a physics assumption: the line above and the line below say exactly the same thing.

R = v0² sin(2θ)g   ·  the range equation

With g = +9.80 m/s². Set it beside the height: h = (v0 sin θ)²/(2g). Same v0², same g downstairs — the two equations differ only in the trig factor and the 2. That similarity is a memory aid, and it is also a trap: it makes them easy to swap by accident.

Predict first Two identical balls leave the same spot on a flat field at the same speed, v0 = 20.0 m/s. One is fired at 30°, the other at 60°. Which one lands farther away?

What sin(2θ) tells you at a glance

Once the range is written as R = (v0²/g) sin(2θ), three results fall out of the shape of the sine function alone. No further physics is needed — only the fact that sin(2θ) runs from 0 up to 1 and back to 0 as θ goes from 0° to 90°.

What the formula saysWhy it is immediate
R = 0 at θ = 0° sin(0°) = 0. Fired horizontally from ground level, the projectile is already on the ground: the flight time is zero.
R = 0 at θ = 90° sin(180°) = 0. Straight up has the longest flight time of all, but v0x = 0, so it comes back down on your head.
R is largest at θ = 45° sin(2θ) peaks at 1 when 2θ = 90°. So Rmax = v0²/g.
θ and (90° − θ) give the same R sin(2(90° − θ)) = sin(180° − 2θ) = sin(2θ). The two angles feed the same number into the same formula.

The last row is worth a sentence of physics, not just trigonometry. The low angle wins on horizontal speed and loses on flight time; the high angle does the reverse. Because R = v0xT is a product, and cos θ and sin θ simply trade places when you swap θ for 90° − θ, the two effects cancel exactly. The trajectories look nothing alike — the 60° shot climbs three times as high — but they come down in the same place.

Worked example 1 — v0 = 20.0 m/s at θ = 30.0°

A ball leaves the ground at 20.0 m/s, 30.0° above the horizontal, on a level field. Take g = 9.80 m/s². Find the maximum height, the flight time, and the range.

Resolve first, then substitute
v0x = (20.0 m/s) cos 30.0° = 17.3205 m/s
v0y = (20.0 m/s) sin 30.0° = 10.0000 m/s

Guard digits kept; everything is rounded once, at the end.

h = v0y²2g = (10.0000 m/s)²2(9.80 m/s²) = 5.1020 m  →  5.10 m
T = 2v0yg = 2(10.0000 m/s)9.80 m/s² = 2.0408 s  →  2.04 s
R = v0xT = (17.3205 m/s)(2.0408 s) = 35.3480 m  →  35.3 m

Check it against the range equation directly: R = (20.0 m/s)² sin(60.0°)/(9.80 m/s²) = (400 m²/s²)(0.86603)/(9.80 m/s²) = 35.3480 m. Same number, as it must be — the range equation is v0xT with the substitutions already made. The time to the apex is T/2 = 1.02 s.

Worked example 2 — the same speed at θ = 60.0°

Now fire the identical ball at 60.0°, the complement of 30.0°. Everything about the flight changes except one number.

The complementary launch
v0x = (20.0 m/s) cos 60.0° = 10.0000 m/s  ·  v0y = (20.0 m/s) sin 60.0° = 17.3205 m/s

The two components have simply swapped values with Example 1. That is the whole content of “complementary angle.”

h = (17.3205 m/s)²2(9.80 m/s²) = 15.3061 m  →  15.3 m
T = 2(17.3205 m/s)9.80 m/s² = 3.5348 s  →  3.53 s
R = (10.0000 m/s)(3.5348 s) = 35.3480 m  →  35.3 m

Three times the height, 1.73 times the flight time, and the identical range. Horizontal speed fell by the same factor 1.73 that the flight time rose by, and the product is untouched. In the range equation this is one line: sin(2 × 60.0°) = sin(120.0°) = sin(60.0°) = 0.86603.

For reference, the best either ball can do is 45.0°: Rmax = v0²/g = (400 m²/s²)/(9.80 m/s²) = 40.8 m, with h = 10.2 m and T = 2.89 s.

Limitations — where these equations stop working

Trevor closes the deck on this point, and it is the right place to close. The two formulas are compact because the assumptions have been absorbed into them. Once absorbed, they are invisible: nothing in R = v0² sin(2θ)/g reminds you that the ground was flat. You have to remember.

Launching from a cliff

Take the same launch — same v0, same θ — but fire it from the top of a cliff of height H above the plain below. Go back through the derivation and check each line:

ResultStill true from a cliff?Why
vy = 0 at the apex Yes It is a statement about the vertical motion only, and says nothing about where the projectile eventually lands.
h = (v0 sin θ)²/(2g) Yes Derived from vy² = v0y² − 2gh with the launch point as the origin. It is the rise above the launch point. The height above the ground is h + H.
T = 2v0 sin θ/g No This came from setting y = 0 at landing. From a cliff the landing point is at y = −H, and the equation 0 = H + v0yT − ½gT² is a genuine quadratic that no longer factors so kindly.
R = v0² sin(2θ)/g No It is v0xT, so it inherits the wrong T. It will under-predict: the extra fall buys extra time, and the real range is longer.

So one of the four survives. That is the honest answer to “can I use the special equations here?” — the apex height yes, the flight time and the range no.

One more casualty The 45° result goes too. Because the extra drop rewards a flatter, faster shot, the angle that maximises range from a cliff is always less than 45°, and it gets smaller the taller the cliff. “Aim at 45° for maximum distance” is a level-ground statement, not a law of nature.

Looking ahead: page 04b — General Projectile Problems is where the level-plane restriction is dropped for good. Without it there is no closed-form flight time to substitute, so you solve the quadratic instead — and if the unknown is the launch angle rather than the time, the algebra reorganises into a quadratic in tan θ, which is why such problems usually have two answers. Page 04a — Horizontally Fired Projectiles is the opposite simplification: θ = 0, so v0y = 0 and there is no apex at all.

Review

  • Everything on this page assumes a level plane — landing height equal to launch height — plus no air resistance and constant g. Those assumptions are baked into the formulas and are no longer visible in them.
  • At the apex, vy = 0. The speed there is v0 cos θ, not zero.
  • Maximum height above the launch point: h = v0y²/(2g) = (v0 sin θ)²/(2g), from vy² = v0y² − 2gh with vy = 0.
  • Time to the apex t = v0 sin θ/g, and on level ground the flight time is exactly twice that, T = 2v0 sin θ/g.
  • Range: R = v0xT = v0² sin(2θ)/g, using 2 sin θ cos θ = sin(2θ).
  • The two boxed results are very similar in form — same v0², same g beneath — which makes them easy to remember and easy to confuse. In both, g is a positive magnitude: substitute +9.80 m/s².
  • From sin(2θ) alone: R = 0 at 0° and 90°, R is largest at 45° (Rmax = v0²/g), and complementary angles θ and 90° − θ give equal ranges.
  • Be careful of the limitations. Launch from a cliff and the apex height still holds, but the flight time and the range both need modification — see 04b.
