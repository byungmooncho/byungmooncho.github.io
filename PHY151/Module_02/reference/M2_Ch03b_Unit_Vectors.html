Unit Vectors

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea A vector is an arrow, and drawing arrows is slow. So we build every vector out of one stock part: a unit vector — an arrow of magnitude exactly 1 that points along a coordinate direction and wears a hat, x̂. Once you have x̂ and ŷ, any vector in the plane is just two numbers times those two arrows: V⃗ = 4 m x̂ + 3 m ŷ. That is the notation the whole rest of this course is written in.

Turning a magnitude and an angle into the two numbers out front is the job of components, the next page in this set (03c). Here we only set up the notation and the arithmetic that goes with it.
You already know this On page 03a you moved a rook one square right and two squares up and wrote the move as x̂ + 2ŷ. Those hatted symbols were unit vectors, and you already read them correctly: “one step in the x direction, two steps in the y direction.” This page just makes that reading official.

A “regular” vector and a unit vector

Notice where the red and blue arrows are drawn — out in the middle of the grid, not at the origin. A unit vector is still a vector, so it does not care where you draw it. What makes it a unit vector is only its magnitude and its direction.

Be precise A unit vector is dimensionless. Its magnitude is the bare number 1 — not 1 m, not 1 m/s. All of the physics, and all of the units, live in the number you put in front of it. You will sometimes see x̂ written as (1 m, 0°) beside a diagram whose squares are metres — that is showing you where the arrow points on that grid. Read the “m” as belonging to the grid, not to the hat.

What the hat buys you

x̂, ŷ, ẑ≡The three unit vectors along the coordinate axes. This is the notation used in PHY 151, because the hat tells you which axis at a glance.
î, ĵ, k̂=The same three vectors, in the notation most textbooks use — read “i hat, j hat, k hat.” î = x̂, ĵ = ŷ, k̂ = ẑ. OpenStax uses this set; expect both.
|x̂|=1. Always, for every unit vector, in every unit system. That is the definition.

A scalar times a vector

The only two things a scalar can do to a vector are change its magnitude and flip it end-for-end.

Sign, not magnitude A magnitude is never negative. When you write −4x̂, the minus sign is a statement about direction: the vector has magnitude 4 and points along −x. Writing “the magnitude is −4” is always wrong.

Building any vector out of unit vectors

Take 4 m of x̂, then 3 m of ŷ, and lay them head-to-tail. The single arrow that closes the path is the resultant — and because the two pieces are perpendicular, the Pythagorean theorem hands you its magnitude.

Magnitude and direction from the unit-vector form
|V⃗| = √(4 m)² + (3 m)² = √25 m² = 5.0 m
θ = tan−1 3 m4 m = 36.9°

The units cancel inside the inverse tangent — an angle is a pure number, so they had better. If your calculator gives 0.6435, it is in radians; switch it to degrees.

A worked example: 5.0 m and 12.0 m

A vector has a 5.0 m component along x and a 12.0 m component along y. Write it, then find its magnitude and direction.

Checking the numbers
V⃗ = 5.0 m x̂ + 12.0 m ŷ   ≡   (5.0 m, 12.0 m)
|V⃗| = √(5.0 m)² + (12.0 m)² = √25 m² + 144 m² = √169 m² = 13.0 m
θ = tan−1 12.0 m5.0 m = 67.4°

13.0 m is bigger than either piece and smaller than 5.0 + 12.0 = 17.0 m. Components never simply add to the magnitude — that only happens for parallel vectors, which these two are not.

Reporting the value of a vector

Every vector in this course can be written two ways, and you will be asked for both. Going from the polar form to the Cartesian form is called resolving the vector: you replace one arrow with two perpendicular components that add back up to it.

The same vector, written both ways
Polar form    A⃗ = (10.0 m, 30.0°)   ≡   A⃗ = 10.0 m @ 30.0°
Ax = A cos θ = (10.0 m)(cos 30.0°) = 8.66 m
Ay = A sin θ = (10.0 m)(sin 30.0°) = 5.00 m
Cartesian form    A⃗ = 8.66 m x̂ + 5.00 m ŷ   ≡   A⃗ = (8.66 m, 5.00 m)

Check it backwards: √(8.66 m)² + (5.00 m)² = 10.0 m, and tan−1(5.00 / 8.66) = 30.0°. Same arrow, different bookkeeping.

Watch the angle Ax = A cos θ only because θ is measured from the x-axis, which makes the horizontal side adjacent to θ. Measure the angle from the y-axis instead and cosine and sine trade places. Draw the triangle, then decide — never the other way round.

Components do not have to be horizontal and vertical

A coordinate system is a choice. You pick it; nature does not. Put a ball on a ramp and the useful axes are not horizontal and vertical at all — they are along the surface and perpendicular to it, because that is how the ball is allowed to move.

What does and does not change Tilting the axes does not change the weight. W⃗ still points straight down with the same magnitude; only the two numbers you report for it change. The vector is physical, the components are bookkeeping — and good bookkeeping is chosen to make the algebra easy.

Review

  • A unit vector has a magnitude of exactly 1, is dimensionless, points along one coordinate direction, and is written with a hat: x̂, ŷ, ẑ — or î, ĵ, k̂ in most textbooks.
  • A scalar times a vector changes the magnitude and nothing else, except that a negative scalar reverses the direction. No scalar can rotate a vector to a new angle, and the multiplier need not be a whole number.
  • Any vector in the plane is the head-to-tail sum of two perpendicular pieces: V⃗ = Vx x̂ + Vy ŷ. The numbers out front are its components; the arrow they build is the resultant.
  • Polar and Cartesian are two reports on one arrow. To resolve a vector, Ax = A cos θ and Ay = A sin θ when θ is measured from the x-axis; to go back, |A⃗| = √Ax² + Ay².
  • The coordinate system is a choice. Components do not have to be horizontal and vertical — on a ramp they should not be — and tilting the axes changes only the numbers, never the vector.
