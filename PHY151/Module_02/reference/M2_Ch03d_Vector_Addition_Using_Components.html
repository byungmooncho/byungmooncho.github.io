Vector Addition Using Components

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Adding vectors with a ruler and protractor works, but it is slow and only as accurate as your drawing. There is an exact route. Resolve each vector into its components, and the two-dimensional problem falls apart into two one-dimensional problems: every x-component lies along the same line, so any two of them are parallel or antiparallel and therefore just add as signed numbers. Same for the y-components. Add the two columns, then rebuild one arrow from the two totals — that arrow is the resultant, and you get its magnitude and direction from a right triangle instead of from a protractor.

You already know this You have already done this with your hands. On grid paper in N1 you added arrows head-to-tail and counted squares; in N2 you noticed that the squares counted across for the resultant were exactly the squares counted across for the first arrow plus the squares counted across for the second — that is, Cx = Ax + Bx and Cy = Ay + By. This page is that discovery written in algebra, with sines and cosines doing the counting so the vectors no longer have to land on grid lines.

Parallel, antiparallel, perpendicular

Before the components, the three special cases. Only the first two let you work with magnitudes alone:

RelationshipAngle between themWhat the magnitudes do
Parallel0° — same directionSimply add: 3 m + 2 m = 5 m
Antiparallel180° — opposite directionsSimply subtract: 3 m − 2 m = 1 m, pointing along the longer one
Perpendicular90°Do not combine arithmetically. Use the Pythagorean theorem: √(3 m)² + (2 m)² ≈ 3.6 m

Anything in between needs geometry — which is the whole reason for the component method. Keep the first two rows in view, because the component method works by manufacturing the parallel and antiparallel cases on purpose.

Why components can simply be added

Here is the one idea that makes the rest of the page mechanical. Pick an x–y axis system and resolve every vector in the problem. Now look at the x-components you have collected: every one of them lies along the x-axis. Two arrows that lie along the same line have no choice — they are parallel (both along +x) or antiparallel (one along +x, one along −x). And you just saw what happens in those two cases: the magnitudes add, or they subtract. Carry the direction in the sign of the number and both cases become one rule: add the signed numbers.

The y-components are handled exactly the same way, on their own line, without ever interacting with the x-components. That independence is the real content of this chapter, and it is what lets us split projectile motion into two separate problems later in Module 2.

Subtraction is not a new operation A⃗ − B⃗ means A⃗ + (−B⃗). The vector −B⃗ has the same magnitude as B⃗ with its direction reversed — so in components every sign flips: (−B⃗)x = −Bx and (−B⃗)y = −By. Reversing a vector does not make its magnitude negative; magnitudes are never negative.

The recipe

Two vectors A⃗ and B⃗ add to the resultant C⃗. Draw them head-to-tail and their components stack up the same way:

The component method, start to finish
C⃗ = A⃗ + B⃗
Cx = Ax + Bx        Cy = Ay + By
C⃗ = Cx x̂ + Cy ŷ   →   C⃗ = (C, θ)
C² = Cx² + Cy²   →   C = √Cx² + Cy²
tan θ = CyCx   →   θ = tan−1CyCx

Line 2 is the whole method; everything else is trigonometry you already own. Note what is not there: no line says C = A + B. Magnitudes do not add — components do.

Order of operations Resolve first, add second, recombine last. Students who try to find the magnitude of the resultant before adding the columns end up adding magnitudes, which is only legal for parallel vectors. And do not stop at Cx and Cy: a question that asks for a vector wants both a magnitude and a direction.

Negative components: you insert the sign

Resolving a vector is the step where students lose marks, and almost always for the same reason. A component's sign comes from the direction the component points, and nothing else. Your calculator will hand you a positive number for cos 53° whether or not the arrow points left. Look at the picture, decide which way each component points, and write the sign yourself.

The same components, two ways
Using θ = 127°, measured from the +x axis
Ax = A cos θ = (5.00 m) cos 127° = −3.01 m
Ay = A sin θ = (5.00 m) sin 127° = +3.99 m

The calculator supplies the minus sign, because cosine is negative between 90° and 270°. This is the reason we measure directions counterclockwise from the +x axis whenever we can.

Using φ = 53°, measured from the −x axis
Ax = −A cos φ = −(5.00 m) cos 53° = −3.01 m
Ay = +A sin φ = +(5.00 m) sin 53° = +3.99 m

Here cos 53° = +0.602 and the calculator has no idea the arrow points left. You put the minus sign in, by reading the picture. Both routes give the same components, as they must — it is the same arrow.

Predict first A force vector has magnitude 8.0 N and points 30° above the −x axis — up and to the left. Without touching a calculator, what is Fx?

Example problem

Trevor's problem, worked in full. Two 1.0 m displacement vectors point in different directions, as shown. What is the final displacement of an object subject to both displacements?

Predict first Before you calculate anything: both displacements are 1.0 m. What is the magnitude of the resultant?

Step 1 — resolve each vector

Work each vector on its own, and keep a guard digit or two; round only at the very end.

Step 1 · Resolve A⃗ and B⃗ into components
Ax = (1.0 m) cos 45° = (1.0 m)√22 = +0.7071 m
Ay = (1.0 m) sin 45° = (1.0 m)√22 = +0.7071 m
Bx = (1.0 m) cos 30° = (1.0 m)√32 = +0.8660 m
By = −(1.0 m) sin 30° = −(1.0 m)12 = −0.500 m

That minus sign on By is inserted by hand, because B⃗ points below the axis. If instead you describe B⃗ as being at −30° from the +x axis and let the calculator work, sin(−30°) = −½ and the sign takes care of itself. Both are correct; only one of them is correct by accident if you do not look at the picture.

Steps 2 and 3 — add the columns

Step 2 · Add the x-components  ·  Step 3 · Add the y-components
Cx = Ax + Bx = (+0.7071 m) + (+0.8660 m) = +1.5731 m
Cy = Ay + By = (+0.7071 m) + (−0.500 m) = +0.2071 m

Both x-components point along +x, so they are parallel and their magnitudes add. The two y-components are antiparallel, so they partly cancel — and because Ay is the larger of the two, the resultant ends up just barely above the axis. No protractor required.

Step 4 — rebuild one arrow

Step 4 · Recombine into magnitude and direction
C = √Cx² + Cy² = √(1.5731 m)² + (0.2071 m)² = √2.5176 m² = 1.5867 m
θ = tan−1CyCx = tan−10.2071 m1.5731 m = tan−1(0.13165) = 7.4997°
Answer: C⃗ = 1.6 m at 7.5° above the +x axis
equivalently   C⃗ = (1.6 x̂ + 0.21 ŷ) m

Two significant figures, because the data were given as 1.0 m. The guard digits were carried through the middle of the calculation and dropped only here. Reporting “1.5867 m” would claim five-digit precision you were never given.

Step 5 — check it

Trevor closes the deck with a page of his own handwriting: the same problem done in exact form, and then a short list of checks. Both are worth copying into your own habits.

The same answer without a calculator until the last line
Cx = √2 + √32 m     Cy = √2 − 12 m
tan θ = √2 − 1√2 + √3 = 0.131652  →  θ = 7.5000°

The metres cancel in the ratio, which is the check that you have a legitimate angle and not a length. The exact value here is θ = 7.5000°. Rounding the components to four figures before taking the inverse tangent is what moves the last digit around; it does not matter at two significant figures, but it is the reason to carry guard digits.

Three checks worth doing every time 1. Work backwards. C cos θ = (1.5867 m) cos 7.5° = 1.573 m, which is Cx again.
2. Size. The magnitude must be larger than either component: 1.59 m > 1.57 m and 1.59 m > 0.21 m. The hypotenuse is always the longest side.
3. Direction. A⃗ reaches 45° up and B⃗ only 30° down, so the resultant should end up slightly above the axis. It does, at 7.5°.
A physicist's shortcut — and why you still do it the long way When the two vectors have equal magnitudes, the parallelogram is a rhombus, so the resultant bisects the angle between them. The angle between A⃗ and B⃗ here is 45° + 30° = 75°, so the resultant must sit at 45° − 75°/2 = 7.5° — exactly the answer above — with magnitude 2(1.0 m) cos(75°/2) = 1.5867 m. Independently, the law of cosines gives C² = (1.0 m)² + (1.0 m)² + 2(1.0 m)(1.0 m) cos 75° = 2.5176 m². Three routes, one answer. The component method is the one that keeps working when the magnitudes are unequal and there are five vectors instead of two.

Review

  • Two vectors are parallel (0°, magnitudes add), antiparallel (180°, magnitudes subtract), or neither. Perpendicular vectors (90°) do not combine arithmetically at all.
  • Once you resolve every vector onto the same axes, all the x-components lie on one line, so they are parallel or antiparallel — and therefore add as signed numbers. The y-components do the same on their own line, independently. That is why the component method works.
  • The method in one line: Cx = Ax + Bx and Cy = Ay + By. Rebuild the resultant with C = √Cx² + Cy² and θ = tan−1(Cy/Cx). Magnitudes never add; components do.
  • A component's sign comes from the direction it points. Measure the angle counterclockwise from the +x axis and the calculator handles the sign; measure it from any other axis and you insert the sign, by looking at the picture.
  • A⃗ − B⃗ is A⃗ + (−B⃗), where −B⃗ has the same magnitude as B⃗ and the opposite direction. Magnitudes are never negative.
  • Two 1.0 m displacements, at 45° above and 30° below the axis, give a resultant of 1.6 m at 7.5° above the +x axis — not 2.0 m.
