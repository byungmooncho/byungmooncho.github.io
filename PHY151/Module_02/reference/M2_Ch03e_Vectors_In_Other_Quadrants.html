Vectors in Other Quadrants

Web version of the lecture slides by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman (Physics This Week), adapted for PHY 151.

The idea Every component you have computed so far pointed up and to the right. Real vectors point every which way, and the moment a vector leaves the first quadrant one of its components goes negative. The trigonometry does not change at all — it is still one right triangle — but you now owe the answer two extra pieces of bookkeeping: the sizes come from sine and cosine, and the signs come from looking at your own drawing. Do those in the wrong order and you will hand in a vector pointing the opposite way from the one you meant.
Performance Expectations By the end of this lesson, you will be able to…
  • PE1Find the components of a vector when given its magnitude and direction — in any quadrant.
  • PE2Attach the correct sign to each component by reading the quadrant off your own sketch, rather than off the calculator.
  • PE3Convert between a reference angle and a standard-position angle, and say which of the two your calculator just handed you.
Trevor's objective slide lists PE1. PE2 and PE3 are what the rest of the deck actually drills — and they are where the marks are lost.
You already know this You can resolve a vector into two perpendicular components with SOH-CAH-TOA, and you can go back the other way with the Pythagorean theorem and an inverse tangent. That is all of page 03c, and it is all the mathematics this page needs. Everything new here is about direction: which axis an angle is measured from, and which way each component points.

Breaking a vector apart, once more

On 03c you took a single vector and split it into two pieces, each one along or parallel to an axis. Those pieces are its components, and adding them back head-to-tail returns the original vector. Nothing about that construction cares where the vector points:

Where angles are measured from: the unit circle

Before any of the signs make sense, the convention has to be nailed down. Trevor states it in one line: the unit circle measures angles from the x-axis, and ‘unit’ refers to the fact that the radius is 1 unit. Two things are hidden in that sentence, and both matter:

From the +x axis≡ Zero degrees points along the positive x axis, and the angle grows counterclockwise. An angle written this way is in standard position. Unless a problem says otherwise, this is the angle a physics answer means.
Radius 1≡ On a circle of radius 1, the horizontal leg of the triangle is cos θ and the vertical leg is sin θ — no dividing needed. That is the whole reason the unit circle is worth drawing: it is a picture of the sine and cosine of every angle at once.
Two different angles A standard-position angle is measured counterclockwise from the +x axis and can be anything from 0° to 360°. A reference angle is the acute angle between the vector and the nearest axis — always between 0° and 90°, and always positive. They are equal only in the first quadrant. From here on, this page writes θ for the standard-position angle and φ for the reference angle, exactly as Trevor does on his slides.

The four quadrants

The axes cut the plane into four quadrants, numbered counterclockwise starting from the upper right. Which quadrant a vector points into fixes the sign of each of its components — and that is a fact about the picture, not about any formula:

QuadrantAxAyStandard-position θFrom the reference angle φ
I — upper right++0° – 90°θ = φ
II — upper left−+90° – 180°θ = 180° − φ
III — lower left−−180° – 270°θ = 180° + φ
IV — lower right+−270° – 360°θ = 360° − φ

In this table φ is the reference angle measured to the nearest horizontal axis — the +x axis in quadrants I and IV, the −x axis in quadrants II and III. Measure to the vertical axis instead and every formula in the last column changes, which is the usual reason a student's answer is off by exactly the complement of the angle.

Use a right triangle, then add the signs for direction

That sentence is the title of one of Trevor's slides, and it is also the procedure. In order:

  1. Draw the vector from the origin, roughly to scale, on a real set of axes. This step is not optional — it is the step that supplies the signs.
  2. Build a right triangle by dropping the two component legs. The hypotenuse is the vector; the legs are parallel to the axes.
  3. Find the acute reference angle φ between the vector and the nearest horizontal axis.
  4. Compute the two magnitudes with sine and cosine, using φ and the magnitude A. Both come out positive.
  5. Attach the signs by inspection, from the quadrant.
Step 4 — sizes only, from the reference angle
|Ax| = A cos φ   ·   |Ay| = A sin φ

Written with the standard-position angle instead, the signs come out on their own — because the cosine and sine of an angle past 90° are already negative where they need to be:

Ax = A cos θ   ·   Ay = A sin θ

Both routes give the same answer. Trevor teaches the first because a reference-angle triangle is something you can see; the second is faster once you trust your angle. Pick one and say which you used — mixing them is how a sign goes missing.

What your calculator actually answered

This is the one place on this page where students reliably lose marks, so it is worth being precise about whose fault it is. Trevor's line is the right one: your calculator does not know where your vector is. It is not wrong — it answered the question you asked, which was narrower than the question you meant.

What tan−1 can and cannot return
tan θ = AyAx   →   θ = tan−1 AyAx

The function tan−1 has to return exactly one angle for each input, so it is defined to return one from the interval (−90°, 90°) — quadrant I for a positive input, quadrant IV for a negative one. It cannot return a quadrant II or quadrant III angle, ever. And the ratio you fed it has already thrown your signs away: 4/(−3) and (−4)/3 are the same number.

Vector is in…tan−1 returnsWhat to do
Quadrant I  (+, +)a positive angle, 0° to 90°Nothing. It is already the standard-position angle.
Quadrant II (−, +)a negative angleAdd 180°.
Quadrant III (−, −)a positive angle — it looks like quadrant IAdd 180°.
Quadrant IV (+, −)a negative angleReport it as is (a negative angle), or add 360°.
The trap Quadrant III is the nastiest of the four, because the two minus signs cancel in the ratio and the calculator hands back a cheerful positive angle that looks like a perfectly good quadrant I answer. Nothing in the number warns you. Only your sketch does.

Trevor gets around this on his second-quadrant slide by using cos−1 instead: cos−1(−3.0 / 5.0) = 126.9° straight away, because the inverse cosine returns angles from 0° to 180° and therefore can reach quadrant II. That works, and it is a good trick — but notice it only moves the problem: the inverse cosine cannot tell quadrant I from quadrant IV, or II from III, because cos(−θ) = cos θ. No single inverse trig function can do the job alone. Two components and a drawing can.

Predict first A velocity has vx = −10.9 m/s and vy = −5.07 m/s. Your calculator reports tan−1(−5.07 / −10.9) = 25.0°. Measured counterclockwise from the +x axis, which way is the object moving?

Worked example: a velocity in the third quadrant

A puck slides across a table with a velocity whose components are vx = −10.9 m/s and vy = −5.07 m/s. Find its magnitude and its direction, and then turn the answer back into components as a check.

Components → magnitude and direction
|v⃗| = √vx² + vy² = √(−10.9)² + (−5.07)² m/s = √144 m/s = 12.0 m/s

The squares kill the signs, so the magnitude never comes out negative. That is a feature, not a loophole: a magnitude is a size.

φ = tan−1 |vy||vx| = tan−1 5.0710.9 = 25.0°   (from the −x axis)
θ = 180° + φ = 180° + 25.0° = 205.0°   (from the +x axis, counterclockwise)

Feed the calculator the signed ratio instead and it returns tan−1(−5.07 / −10.9) = tan−1(0.465) = 25.0° — numerically the same, and still not the answer. It is the reference angle, and it needs 180° added because the sketch puts the vector in quadrant III.

Magnitude and direction → components (the check, and PE1)
vx = v cos θ = (12.0 m/s) cos 205.0° = (12.0 m/s)(−0.906) = −10.9 m/s
vy = v sin θ = (12.0 m/s) sin 205.0° = (12.0 m/s)(−0.423) = −5.07 m/s

Used with the standard-position angle, cosine and sine deliver both signs for free — cos 205° and sin 205° are both negative, which is precisely what quadrant III requires. Run it with the reference angle instead and you get 12.0 cos 25.0° = +10.9 m/s and 12.0 sin 25.0° = +5.07 m/s, both sizes right and both signs wrong. Then you attach the minus signs yourself, from the drawing.

Where this pays off

Page 03d added vectors by adding their components. Everything on this page is what makes 03d safe: a resultant built from signed components lands in whatever quadrant the signed sums put it in, and the direction you report for that resultant has to come out of a sketch, not out of an inverse tangent. If Rx = −10.9 and Ry = −5.07, the resultant is the quadrant III vector you just worked — and any answer of 25° points the arrow the wrong way down the table.

In your own notes You built this triangle by hand in N2 — Resolving A Vector, where one vector is split head-to-tail into perpendicular parts, and you set up the sine and cosine ratios in N2a — Trig Definitions. The N2a worked example is not in the first quadrant by accident: it deliberately lands outside it, with Cx positive and Cy negative, and it writes the sign convention out explicitly — which is exactly the point of this page. Read the three together as one idea: N2 gives you the picture, N2a gives you the ratios, and this page gives you the bookkeeping that decides which signs hang on them.

Review

  • The four quadrants fix the signs of a vector's components: (+, +) in I, (−, +) in II, (−, −) in III, (+, −) in IV. That is read off a drawing, not off a formula.
  • The procedure never changes: draw the vector, build the right triangle, get the two sizes from A cos φ and A sin φ with the acute reference angle, then attach the signs by inspection.
  • A reference angle is the acute angle to the nearest axis; a standard-position angle is measured counterclockwise from the +x axis. They agree only in quadrant I. Convert with θ = φ, 180° − φ, 180° + φ, or 360° − φ in quadrants I to IV.
  • Used with the standard-position angle, Ax = A cos θ and Ay = A sin θ produce the signs automatically.
  • The unit circle is the convention behind all of it: angles counterclockwise from the +x axis, and “unit” means the radius is 1, so the two legs of the triangle are cos θ and sin θ themselves.
  • tan−1 can only return an angle in (−90°, 90°), and the ratio Ay / Ax has already discarded your signs. Quadrants II and III need 180° added; quadrant IV can be reported as a negative angle or as 360° minus the reference angle. The calculator is not wrong — it does not know where your vector is.
  • This is what keeps 03d honest: the resultant of a head-to-tail addition has whatever direction its signed components say it has, and its magnitude is always positive.
