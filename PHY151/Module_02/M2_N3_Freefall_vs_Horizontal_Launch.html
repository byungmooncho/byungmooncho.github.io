Freefall vs. Horizontal Launch

Synopsis Picture a cannonball fired from the top of a tower — the cannon aimed perfectly horizontally. At the exact instant it fires, a second cannonball is simply dropped from the same spot. The fired ball travels some horizontal distance before it lands; the dropped ball falls straight down. If you timed both balls, would one hit the ground before the other — and if so, which one — or would they land at the very same instant? This note works through a Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (CER) argument to find out, then tests it against a real demonstration.
The launcher–drop device: one steel ball is pushed horizontally while a second is released straight down, at the exact same instant.
Performance Expectations By the end of this note, you will be able to…
  • PE1State a testable claim about whether a dropped ball and a horizontally launched ball, released from the same height at the same instant, hit the ground at the same time.
  • PE2Draw on prior evidence and experience when reasoning about the claim — and recognize where everyday intuition can be misleading.
  • PE3Observe the launcher–drop device and record what actually happens when both balls are released together.
  • PE4Sketch velocity vectors at regular time intervals for the dropped ball (A), the launched ball (B), and a hypothetical zero-gravity case (C).
  • PE5Explain, using vector reasoning, why horizontal and vertical motion are independent — constant vx, ever-changing vy.
  • PE6Write the launched ball's velocity as a vector, v⃗(t) = v0x î + (−9.8t) ĵ, and identify what each term represents.
🔎Evidence: what you already have

Before you commit to a claim, take stock of what you've already seen — even if it turns out to be misleading. That's part of building good evidence:

🪙 Two coins off a table Flick one coin off the edge of a table while a friend drops a second coin from the same height, at the same instant. Which do you expect to hit the floor first?
🎬 Movie physics In action movies, a car driven off a cliff often seems to hang in the air before it drops. Is that really how gravity works?
🏀 A thrown ball You've watched a ball thrown sideways arc downward through the air. Does it really spend more time falling than one just dropped from the same height?

Claim — predict first: a ball is dropped from a tower at the same instant a second ball is fired horizontally from the same height. Ignoring air resistance, which one hits the ground first?

Observe: the launcher–drop device

The device pictured above holds two steel balls at the same height. A spring-loaded arm pushes one ball out horizontally at the exact instant it releases the second ball to drop straight down — no reaction time, no guessing about "at the same time." Watch (and listen) as your instructor fires it.

What you should notice: however hard the launcher fires the horizontal ball, you hear a single "thud" when the balls land — not two. Both balls reach the floor at the same instant, every time, no matter the launch speed.

🧭Explain: reading the vectors

Every arrow you toggled on above is a velocity vector, and together they tell the whole story. Path A's arrow only ever points straight down, and it keeps growing — that's gravity acting alone, accelerating the ball downward at 9.8 m/s² with nothing pushing it sideways. Path C's arrow only ever points sideways, and it never changes length — that's what horizontal motion looks like with no gravity at all: constant velocity, forever. Path B's arrow is just those two combined, added component by component — and the dashed guides show it directly: B's horizontal piece exactly matches C at every instant, and B's vertical piece exactly matches A at every instant. Neither component knows the other exists.

That's the independence of motion: a projectile's horizontal velocity and vertical velocity change (or don't change) completely independently of each other. For the horizontally launched ball, we can write that as a single vector equation:

v⃗(t)  =  v0x î  +  (−9.8 t) ĵ

The horizontal component, v0x, never appears with a t — it's constant. The vertical component, −9.8t, grows steadily with time — the same acceleration acting on the dropped ball. Set v0x to zero and this is exactly Path A's velocity. That's the reasoning behind the claim: since vertical motion (the only thing that determines when a ball reaches the ground) is identical for A and B, they must land at the same instant — no matter how fast the ball is launched sideways.
