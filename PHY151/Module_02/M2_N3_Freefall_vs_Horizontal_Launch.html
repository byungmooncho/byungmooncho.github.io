Freefall vs. Horizontal Launch: How Do the Fall Times Compare?

Synopsis Imagine a cannon at the top of a tower, aimed perfectly horizontally. At the instant it fires, an identical cannonball is simply dropped from the same height. The fired ball sails out over the ground; the dropped ball falls straight down. Which one hits the ground first — or do they land together, and if so, why? This note is a CER exercise: you will commit to a Claim, back it with Evidence from your own experience, defend it with Reasoning built on the vector nature of velocity and force — and then test it against the real experiment. The result is the doorway to all of projectile motion.
Performance Expectations By the end of this note, you will be able to…
  • PE1Perform a CER exercise — state a claim about the fall times, support it with evidence from experience, and justify it with reasoning.
  • PE2Use the vector nature of velocity, acceleration, and force in your reasoning — the launched ball's velocity is horizontal, while gravity pulls only vertically.
  • PE3Compare the observed fall times of a dropped ball and a horizontally launched ball, and state the result: they land at the same time.
  • PE4Revise and refine your explanation after the observation, drawing velocity vectors at regular intervals along both trajectories.
  • PE5Explain trajectory C — the straight-line path the ball would follow if fired in space with no gravity, and why real paths bend away from it.
  • PE6Recognize that horizontal and vertical motion are independent — the launched ball is freefalling vertically the whole time. (You'll apply this fully to angled launches in Note 4.)

In this note, we work through

  1. Claim — are the fall times different or the same?
  2. Evidence — what previous experiences and observations relate to this situation?
  3. Reasoning — use velocity, acceleration, and force (especially their vector nature) to justify your claim.
  4. Observe — the launcher device pushes one steel ball horizontally while simultaneously dropping another. How do the fall times compare?
  5. Explain — revise your reasoning, draw the velocity vectors along trajectories A and B at regular intervals, and explain why the no-gravity path C looks the way it does.

The launched ball's two lives

The horizontally launched ball runs two completely independent motions on one shared clock:

DirectionWhat happensWhy
Horizontal (x)Constant velocity — equal strobe spacings sidewaysno horizontal force — nothing speeds it up or slows it down
Vertical (y)Freefall — identical to the dropped ball, starting from vy = 0gravity is vertical — it pulls both balls down the same way
Path C (no gravity)A straight horizontal line at constant velocityno force at all — nothing changes the motion, so nothing bends the path

Looking ahead: here the launch was perfectly horizontal, so the vertical motion started from rest — pure freefall. In Note 4 we tilt the cannon: the launch velocity then has both components (Fx = V cos θ from Note 2a returns!), and the vertical motion becomes the vertical rocket from Module 1, Note 9. Everything you need is already on your desk.

Three ideas to remember

⏱️ tfall is the same Dropped or launched horizontally from the same height — both start with vy = 0 and fall with the same g.
✂️ x ⟂ y — independent Gravity (vertical) can't touch the horizontal velocity; the horizontal velocity can't slow the fall.
🚀 No force → straight line Path C: fired in space with no gravity, the ball keeps its velocity forever — straight and steady.
