Determining Launch Parameters To Hit Targets

Synopsis This is a lab-companion worksheet, worth 10% bonus credit on the Module 2 exam. Your launcher sits on the bench and fires a projectile at a fixed launch speed. Your job is to (1) find that launch speed two independent ways — a timed vertical launch and a photogate reading — and then (2) work the projectile-motion equations backward: instead of "where does it land?", you're asked "what angle makes it land here?" for targets set 1 m, 2 m, and 3 m from the launcher. That's called an inverse problem — solving for a condition (the angle) that produces a chosen outcome, rather than predicting an outcome from given conditions. As you'll see, a target is usually reachable by two different launch angles — a steep high arc and a flatter low arc — the two-angle solution.

Finding the launch speed, two ways

Method 1 — timed vertical launch. Point the launcher straight up and fire. Time the flight from launch to the moment it returns to launch height, t. By symmetry, the ball spends half that time going up and half coming down, so the launch speed is V₀ = g·t/2. This method is limited by your reaction time on the stopwatch — twice, once at launch and once at return.

Method 2 — photogate. The launcher has a photogate right at the muzzle that times how long the projectile blocks a light beam of known width as it exits, giving V₀ directly, electronically, at the instant of launch. There's no human reaction time in this measurement at all — the main uncertainty is the gate's own timing resolution and the width measurement, both far smaller than a human stopwatch-press.

Record both values on your worksheet and compare them. In general, the photogate reading is the more reliable of the two, since it removes the two human reaction-time errors baked into the timed method.

Worked example (optional) — Extra CreditSame quadratic N4 derives in general, worked here for specific numbers.
Dr. Cho's Extra Credit sketch — the launcher sits a height H above the bench where the target line is marked.

Given: launch height H = 1.06 m, launch speed V₀ = 5 m/s, target distance D = 1.5 m. Find the launch angle(s) θ.

  1. Start from the two position equations, with the target level as y = 0 and the launch point at y = H:   x(t) = V₀cosθ·t,   y(t) = H + V₀sinθ·t − ½gt².
  2. Solve the x-equation for the time of flight: t = D / (V₀cosθ), and substitute into the y-equation, using y = 0 at the target.
  3. Replace 1/cos²θ with 1 + tan²θ and collect terms. This is exactly the step N4 carries out in general — here it turns the y-equation into a quadratic in tanθ:
    k·tan²θ − D·tanθ + (k − H) = 0,   where k = gD² / (2V₀²).
  4. Plug in the numbers: k = (9.8)(1.5)² / (2·5²) = 0.441. The quadratic becomes 0.441·tan²θ − 1.5·tanθ − 0.619 = 0.
  5. Solve with the quadratic formula for u = tanθ. The two roots give the two launch angles.
Result: θ₁ = 75.2° (high arc) and θ₂ = −20.4° (low arc — aimed below the horizontal, since the launcher starts 1.06 m above the target line and still has room to fall).
