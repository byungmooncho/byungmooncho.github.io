Velocities From Distance vs. Time Graphs — Average Velocity and Instantaneous Velocity

Synopsis Real motion almost always involves acceleration. With constant acceleration (like freefall, a = g = −9.8 m/s²), velocity increases linearly with time, and that's what curves the distance-vs-time graph. This note teaches two ways to read velocity off a curved distance-vs-time graph: the average velocity over a time interval (slope of a secant line connecting two points), and the instantaneous velocity at one instant (slope of the tangent line touching a single point).
Performance Expectations
  • PE1Define average velocity and instantaneous velocity.
  • PE2Extract average velocity from the secant line and instantaneous velocity from the tangent line on a distance-vs-time graph.

Preamble

1) Why is the slope of the velocity-vs-time graph zero in constant-speed motion?

2) Using your knowledge of linear equations, explain why the plot of vf as a function of time has slope equal to the acceleration.

1) In constant-speed motion, acceleration = 0, and slope of v–t IS the acceleration — so the slope must be zero (a flat line).
2) vf = vi + at has the form y = b + mx, a straight line where the slope m is a (the acceleration) and the y-intercept b is vi.
