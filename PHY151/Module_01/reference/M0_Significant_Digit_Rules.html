Significant Digit Rules

A lesson by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman, adapted for PHY 105.

Why this matters Following the rules of significant digits will help make sure that you don't over- or understate the precision of your measurements. In this course, you can lose points for misstating the significant digits in your answers. In “real life,” overspecifying the tolerance of a piece of equipment can cost you significant amounts of money: a 220 ohm resistor is relatively cheap, but a 220.00 ohm resistor (with a tolerance of 0.01 ohm) is insanely expensive.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able…

  1. To explain what significant digits and/or significant figures mean.
  2. To determine the significant digits in a given measurement.
  3. To determine the significant digits for a given calculation.
  4. To use significant digits properly.

Adding or subtracting numbers

When adding or subtracting two (or more) numbers, the number with the significant digit furthest to the left (the least precise decimal place) determines the number of significant digits in the sum:

Line up the decimal places
12.40 g [4] + 12.0001 g [7] ───────────────── 24.4001 24.40 g [4]

The 12.40 is only known to the hundredths place, so the answer can't claim anything past the hundredths place either.

Three more sums — watch how the least precise number rules
154.3̲2 cm 15̲0 cm 150.̲ cm + 43.1 cm + 43.1 cm + 43.1 cm ─────────── ────────── ────────── 197.42 cm 193.1 cm 193.1 cm = 197.4 cm = 190 cm = 193 cm

154.32's partner is precise to tenths → keep tenths. Plain 150 is only precise to the tens place → round to 190. But “150.” (with the decimal point) is precise to the ones place → 193.

Multiplying or dividing numbers

When multiplying or dividing, the number with the smaller number of significant digits determines the significant digits of the result:

245 × 23
245 × 23 = 5636 = 5600

23 has only two significant digits, so the answer keeps only two.

Why does this make sense? Suppose each factor of 27 × 8 is uncertain by ±1. Wiggle each number and watch the product swing:

27 × 8 = 216 — but how much of that is trustworthy?
27 × 7 = 189 26 × 8 = 208 27 × 8 = 216 27 × 8 = 216 27 × 9 = 243 28 × 8 = 224
27 × 8 = 216 → 200

The product ranges from below 200 to above 240 — only the leading digit or so deserves any confidence. These rules don't always “feel” right, but you should follow them.

“Exact numbers”

If you have a number that is “exact,” don't use it to determine the answer's significant digits. For example, the ½ in the kinematics equation is exact, and the 2 in the v² = v₀² + 2a(x − x₀) equation is exact — they are pure numbers, not measurements.

Rounding errors — wait till the end to round

The last digit in a calculated number may vary: depending on how it is calculated, rounding off insignificant digits along the way may cause major problems.

Example: 8% tax on a $2.21 + $1.35 purchase
Tax at the end: Tax on each item first: $ 2.21 $2.21 + 8% = $2.3868 → $ 2.39 + $ 1.35 $1.35 + 8% = $1.458 → $ 1.46 ───────── ───────── $ 3.56 = $ 3.85 + $ 0.2848 (3.56 × .08) ───────── $ 3.8448 = $ 3.84

Round early and the totals disagree by a penny. Keep the extra digits through the calculation and round once, at the end.

Online homework will check your significant figures

Online homework systems (such as WebAssign) check the significant figures of your answers. For example: if your speedometer has an uncertainty of 2.7 km/h at a speed of 93 km/h, the percent uncertainty is 2.9032258…% on your calculator — but an answer entered with all those digits gets flagged with “Check the number of significant figures.” Expressed properly, the answer is 2.9%.

Grading note You might receive partial credit if you give the correct value with more than the required significant figures — but no credit is awarded if you give too few. Also be aware that textbooks and homework systems sometimes count significant figures in slightly different ways than you were taught.

Data tables in Excel

Sometimes divisions cause cells to have lots of decimal places (0.33333, 0.66667, 1.33333…). Excel doesn't adjust these automatically — make sure that you do. Select the cells and use the decrease-decimal command icon in the Number group of the Home tab, so the table shows an appropriate 0.33, 0.67, 1.33, …

Review

  • There are very specific rules on how to determine the number of significant digits for a number.
  • Using too many significant digits suggests that your answer is much more precise than it should be.
