Significant Digits and Uncertainty

A lesson by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman, adapted for PHY 105.

Why this matters In physics, we want to measure things and make predictions. Using significant digits helps us share with other scientists how precisely we expect our measurements and predictions to be. Having $1000 in your pocket is different from having $1123.34 in your pocket — and a measurement of 2.33 m is much different from 2.333333333 m. The second one suggests you could measure down to a single atom! You will run into significant-digit problems if you just write down whatever your calculator shows.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able…

  1. To explain the difference between accuracy and precision.
  2. To explain the link between significant digits and uncertainty.
  3. To describe how significant digits (or figures) tell us information about the measurement.

Precision vs. accuracy

Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the “real” value. Precision is how tightly repeated measurements cluster together — how finely our tools can possibly measure. They are not the same thing:

Significant digits (or significant figures)

The significant digits of a number tell us something about the precision of a measurement — and the precision tells us about the uncertainty in the measurement:

Same length, different claims
Written asClaims precision to about…
15 m1 m
15.0 m10 cm
15.00 m1 cm
15.000 m1 mm

“15 meters” means the true value lies somewhere between about 14 and 16 — anywhere in that range rounds to 15. “15.0 meters” narrows the range to roughly 14.9–15.1. Every extra significant digit shrinks the claimed uncertainty by a factor of ten.

Uncertainty

The uncertainty in a measurement is an estimate of how precise the measurement might be. Read the ruler below — the rod ends just shy of the 60 mm mark:

Uncertainty as “error”

“Error” in science should really be referred to as uncertainty. Error is not a mistake in this context. The “error” is a limitation of our tools and our time. One person reads the rod above as 56.8 mm from a close-up look, another as 57 mm — neither made a mistake; they hit the limit of the ruler.

The proper use of significant digits tells us the uncertainty in a measurement.

Reaction time

When measuring time, we can be limited by reaction time. While this is often referred to as “human error,” it is really just an added uncertainty in our measurement.

Stopwatch example A stopwatch reads 1 m 2.08 s. That display suggests I can measure down to 1/100th of a second — but if a human was running the stopwatch, the actual reported number should be 1 m 2 s ± 2 s.

Calculating the uncertainty

The true uncertainty in a measurement is related to the number of measurements that you make. In higher-level science and statistics classes, you will learn that the uncertainty is calculated by finding the standard deviation:

Standard deviation
σ = √( Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1) )

Repeat a measurement many times and the results pile up in a bell curve; σ measures the width of that pile. In Excel, use the STDEV.S function.

If all the experimental measurements are the same, the standard deviation becomes zero — but that does not mean the measurement is perfect. Even though we treat the standard deviation as the uncertainty, you should default back to the uncertainty of the measuring tool. Here the reported value would be 2.351 ± 0.005 m.

Uncertainty for compound measurements

The relative uncertainty for one measurement S is its uncertainty as a percentage of its value:

Relative (percent) uncertainty
%unc S = δSS × 100%

To find the relative uncertainty for combined (multiplied or divided) measurements, simply add the relative uncertainties. If B = S₁ × S₂, where S₁ ± δS₁ and S₂ ± δS₂:

Combining, then undoing
%unc B = δS₁S₁ × 100% + δS₂S₂ × 100%
δB = %unc B × B100%

To get back to the absolute uncertainty δB, “undo” the percent calculation.

Example with area

A rectangle has measured length L ± δL and width W ± δW, and area A = L·W. Adding the relative uncertainties:

Uncertainty of an area, symbolically
%unc A = %unc L + %unc W
δAA = δLL + δWW
δA = A ( δLL + δWW )

Now with numbers: L = 3 ± 1 cm and W = 5 ± 1 cm. The area could be as small as 2 × 5 = 10 or as large as 4 × 5 = 20 around the central 3 × 5 = 15:

Uncertainty of the area, with numbers
δA = 15 ( 13 + 15 ) = 15 ( 515 + 315 ) = 15 ( 815 ) = 8
A ± δA = 15 ± 8 cm²

Note that this uncertainty is usually an overestimation.

Review

  • The accuracy of a measurement is how close it is to the “real” value.
  • The precision tells us about how finely our tools can possibly measure.
  • Good measurements are both precise and accurate.
  • Significant digits help show the precision of our measurements.
