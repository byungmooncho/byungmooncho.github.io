Conversions in Physics

Notes on the video by Dr. H. Trevor Johnson-Steigelman, Physics This Week, adapted for PHY 105.

The idea How do you convert from kilometers to miles — and know whether your answer is reasonable and correct? A unit conversion is just a multiplication by a special version of the number one, arranged so that the unit you don't want cancels out. The same method works for length, mass, time, and every other kind of measurement.

A conversion factor is a special version of one

A typical conversion factor relates one unit to another:

1.000 mi = 1.609 km

Both sides are equal — the numbers are different, but the units make sure we know which is which. The same length, written two ways.

Now divide one side by the other. One mile divided by one mile is obviously 1 — but because the two sides are equal, 1.609 km divided by 1.000 mi is also 1, and so is 1.000 mi divided by 1.609 km:

1.000 mi1.609 km = 1   and   1.609 km1.000 mi = 1

Some textbooks call this “the magic of the number one.” It isn't magic — 1 is the multiplicative identity: one times a number gives that number back. Multiplying by 1 doesn't change the value of a measurement; it only changes the way it looks. The distance between two markers on a road stays exactly the same — what changes is the unit you describe it with.

Worked example — the 5k race

A “5k” race is 5.0 kilometers (two significant digits). How many miles is that? Multiply by the version of one that puts kilometers on the bottom:

Converting 5.0 km to miles
5.0 km × 1.000 mi1.609 km  =  3.108 mi  →  3.1 mi

Kilometers appear in the numerator and the denominator, so they cancel — leaving miles. Keep four digits through the calculation (3.108…), then round to the two significant digits you started with.

Use four-digit conversion factors Your textbook may list conversion factors to nine or ten decimal places. Four digits is usually sufficient; one or two usually isn't quite enough. Do the whole calculation with the four-digit factor, and round at the end. (For a 5k race, the difference is a few feet — nobody minds jogging a little farther into the parking lot — but in other problems the rounding error can bite.)

Conversions you must know

Most conversion factors you can look up as needed — but a handful you should know by heart, and in this class they will be on quizzes:

Know these by heart
ConversionNote
1.00 in = 2.54 cm Exact, by definition — written into law when the units were tied together.
1.000 mi = 1.609 km Sometimes quoted as 1.6, but prefer the four-digit version.
1 min = 60 s You already know this one.
1 hr = 60 min …and this one. So there are really only two new ones.

Sanity check — which unit is bigger?

Before (and after) any conversion, decide which of the two units is the larger one. A mile is longer than a kilometer — about 0.6 of a kilometer longer. So:

  • Converting a distance in miles into kilometers? You'd better end up with more kilometers.
  • Converting kilometers into miles? You'd better end up with a smaller number.

Keep these size comparisons in the back of your head — they catch wrong answers before they cost you points.

Multiply or divide? Let the units decide

Students often ask: do I multiply or divide by 1.609? The beauty of this method is that you never have to guess. Write down the measurement you're starting from, then arrange the conversion factor so the unit you want to get rid of cancels — it must appear on the bottom:

Right — kilometers cancel
5.0 km × 1.000 mi1.609 km  =  3.1 mi ✓

Right unit (miles), and the right size — a number smaller than 5, as the sanity check demands.

Wrong — the factor is upside down
5.0 km × 1.609 km1.000 mi  =  8.0 km²/mi ✗

Two signals of a mistake: the units came out as km²/mi — we were trying to get to miles and never got there — and the number got bigger (5 → 8) when kilometers-to-miles should make it smaller. The units are telling you that you've made a mistake. Flip the factor and try again.

Review

  • There are a handful of conversions you should know by heart; most others you can look up as necessary.
  • A conversion factor is a special version of one: take one side of an equality and divide it by the other — either way gives 1, and multiplying by 1 never changes the value.
  • Arrange the factor so the units you're trying to get rid of cancel out.
  • Use at least four digits in your conversion factor, and round your answer at the end.
