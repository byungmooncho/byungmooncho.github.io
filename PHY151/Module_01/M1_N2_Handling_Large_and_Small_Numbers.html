Handling Very Large and Small Numbers

Synopsis Physics is a quantitative science — we put numbers to things, and those numbers range from the enormous (Earth's mass: 5,980,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 kg) to the tiny (an atom's diameter: 0.0000000001 m). Writing all those zeros is unworkable, so we use powers of 10 and scientific notation, and we learn to use the calculator's EE button to input these numbers correctly.

Zeros are tricky

  1. All leading zeros are not significant, e.g. 0.00234 m = 2.34×10⁻³ m (3 sig figs).
  2. Trailing zeros are significant only when there's a decimal point, e.g. 100.00 kg (5 sig figs).
  3. Trailing zeros are not significant without a decimal point, e.g. 142000 s (3 sig figs).
  4. Zeros sandwiched between nonzero digits are always significant, e.g. 1004 m (4 sig figs).

Practice: EE-button calculations

When inputting scientific notation, use the EE button (2nd → , on many calculators), not ^ or 10ˣ. Example: 1.56×10⁻⁷ is typed 1.56 EE (-) 7, shown as 1.56E-7.
